# The Lost Century
**Version:** 0.1  
**Status:** Provisional  
**Last Reviewed:** 2026-08-02

---

## Purpose

Identify the historical period in which the Gumma could most plausibly have originated — not by inventing history, but by discovering where history is genuinely silent. The birth of the Gumma must fit inside a real gap: a period where the civilizational problem they solve actually existed, where the historical record is thin enough to permit their existence without contradicting established scholarship, and where the conditions uniquely demanded an institution of binding civilizational memory.

---

## Assumptions

1. The Gumma do not rewrite history. They exist in its margins.
2. Historical plausibility requires that their origin period have: (a) a genuine power vacuum, (b) a demonstrated need for inter-community binding memory, (c) insufficient documentary record to disprove their existence, and (d) a civilizational transition complex enough to generate their function.
3. South India (Karnataka/Deccan focus) is the geographic starting point — the user's directional choice. Expansion to other regions comes later.
4. What historians do not know is more useful to us than what they do know.
5. We separate verified history (sourced from epigraphy, literature, and archaeological record) from speculative opportunity (gaps where the record is silent) at every step.

# PART 1: Timeline of South India (500 CE – 1800 CE) — Periods of Silence, Collapse, and Opportunity

---

## I. The Chalukya-Pallava Wars and Early Rashtrakuta Period (500–753 CE)

**What happened:**
The Deccan was contested between the Badami Chalukyas and the Pallavas of Kanchi for over two centuries. Wars, sackings, destructions of capitals. The Chalukya dynasty was overthrown by their feudatory Dantidurga (Rashtrakuta) in c. 753 CE.

**Periods of silence/collapse:**

| Period | Event | Why It Matters |
|--------|-------|---------------|
| c. 540–550 CE | Pulakeshin I's consolidation of Badami — the period *before* the dynasty stabilized is almost undocumented | The mechanisms by which a local chief became a king are invisible. Who brokered alliances? Who carried messages between clans? |
| c. 642 CE | Pallava sacking of Badami under Narasimhavarman I — the Chalukya capital destroyed | Records destroyed. Administrative continuity broken. Who maintained inter-community agreements when the state collapsed? |
| c. 733–745 CE | Late Chalukya decline — succession disputes, weakening feudatories, the internal rot before Rashtrakuta overthrow | The historiographic gap: how did local communities survive when central authority was visibly failing? |
| c. 753 CE | The Rashtrakuta overthrow itself — Dantidurga's coup against Kirtivarman II | No clear documentary trail of HOW the coup's local alliances were organized. Feudatory coordination across the Deccan required an information network no historian has fully mapped. |

**Narrative opportunity:** In every major dynastic transition, there is a gap between the old state's collapse and the new state's consolidation. During that gap, who maintained order between communities? Who remembered what was owed? These gaps last months to decades — and they are almost undocumented.

---

## II. Rashtrakuta Supremacy (753–973 CE)

**What happened:**
The Rashtrakutas ruled one of India's largest empires from Manyakheta (modern Malkhed, Karnataka). They fought in all four cardinal directions simultaneously. Their administrative system was complex but partially obscured.

**Periods of silence/collapse:**

| Period | Event | Why It Matters |
|--------|-------|---------------|
| c. 753–780 CE | Early Rashtrakuta consolidation — the mechanics of absorbing Chalukya feudatories | How were loyalty oaths and inter-feudatory agreements maintained when the feudatories had served a different master for centuries? |
| c. 915–927 CE | Indra III to Govinda IV — succession instability, rapid throne changes | Four rulers in twelve years. Court records thin. Local administration must have operated *independently* of a throne changing every three years. |
| c. 968–973 CE | Final Rashtrakuta collapse — Tailapa II (Kalyani Chalukya) overthrows Karka II | Manyakheta was sacked and possibly destroyed. An entire imperial capital's records — agreements, treaties, revenue pacts — gone. Someone, somewhere, preserved continuity while the state didn't. |

**Narrative opportunity:** The Rashtrakuta empire's sheer scale and its multiple-front wars created a structural dependency on *non-state* networks for information, diplomacy, and inter-regional coordination. The empire could not have functioned without intermediaries historians cannot fully identify.

---

## III. The Kalyani Chalukya Period (973–1189 CE)

**What happened:**
The Western Chalukyas of Kalyani replaced the Rashtrakutas, but never achieved the same degree of centralized control. Their relationship with powerful feudatories (Hoysalas, Kadambas, Seuna Yadavas) was perpetually tense.

**Periods of silence/collapse:**

| Period | Event | Why It Matters |
|--------|-------|---------------|
| c. 1000–1050 CE | The Chalukya-Chola wars — Southern Karnataka contested between two empires | Border communities caught between two imperial claims. Their existing agreements with neighbors under the old regime became *stateless* — neither empire adjudicated them. |
| c. 1126–1162 CE | Tailapa III and Jagadhekamalla III — weakened Chalukya authority, feudatories effectively autonomous | The de facto end of Chalukya power preceded the de jure end by decades. During this interregnum, local powers governed without reference to a credible central authority. |
| c. 1162–1189 CE | The Kalachuri usurpation — Bijjala and his successors briefly seized the Chalukya throne | A dynasty *replacement* that lasted only decades. During and after it: administrative chaos. Who maintained legitimacy? How did communities know which agreements from the Chalukya era still held? |

**Critical note:** This period also saw the rise of the Veerashaiva (Lingayat) movement under Basavanna (c. 1131–1167 CE) — a radical social reform movement that explicitly challenged caste hierarchy and priestly authority. This created massive social upheaval. Inter-community agreements about caste-based obligations were suddenly *contested*. Who held the older agreements while the social order was being challenged?

---

## IV. The Hoysala Period (1026–1346 CE)

**What happened:**
The Hoysalas rose from Chalukya feudatories to independent power, ruling from Dvarasamudra (Halebidu). Their kingdom was culturally rich but militarily pressured from the north.

**Periods of silence/collapse:**

| Period | Event | Why It Matters |
|--------|-------|---------------|
| c. 1310–1311 CE | Malik Kafur's invasion — Halebidu sacked | Temple records destroyed. Palace archives seized or burned. A kingdom's institutional memory physically destroyed in weeks. |
| c. 1327–1346 CE | Terminal Hoysala period — the kingdom fragmenting under repeated Delhi Sultanate pressure | Twenty years of slow collapse. No single authority. Local powers improvising governance. Inter-community memory dependent entirely on oral and non-state systems. |

**Narrative opportunity:** The destruction of Halebidu is one of South India's great documentary catastrophes. Thousands of inscriptions survive in stone (the Hoysalas were prolific builders), but the *soft records* — treaties, agreements, revenue arrangements, dispute resolutions — were overwhelmingly lost. Stone tells us what kings wanted remembered. What about what communities needed remembered?

---

## V. The Vijayanagara Foundation and Empire (1336–1565/1646 CE)

**What happened:**
The Vijayanagara Empire — one of India's greatest — was founded in the aftermath of complete political collapse in the Deccan. Its foundation is itself historically contested.

**Periods of silence/collapse:**

| Period | Event | Why It Matters |
|--------|-------|---------------|
| c. 1311–1336 CE | **THE INTERREGNUM** — the period between Hoysala/Kakatiya/Yadava collapse and Vijayanagara's founding | This is the most significant political vacuum in South Indian history. For approximately twenty-five years, no single indigenous power controlled the Deccan. The Delhi Sultanate's governors held major cities but had no administrative depth. The countryside was ungoverned. |
| c. 1336–1356 CE | The first generation of Vijayanagara — Harihara I and Bukka I's consolidation | How did two brothers (or possibly feudatories of the Hoysalas or the Kampili chief) assemble a coalition large enough to create an empire? The *mechanism* of coalition-building across dozens of displaced communities with competing claims is unexplained. |
| c. 1485–1505 CE | Saluva dynasty usurpation and Tuluva succession | Dynasty replacement. The transition from Saluva to Tuluva (through the general Narasa Nayaka, then his son Vira Narasimha, then Krishnadevaraya) involved palace coups and disappearances. Administrative disruption. |
| c. 1565 CE | Battle of Talikota — Vijayanagara capital sacked and destroyed | The greatest single destruction event in South Indian history. A city of possibly 500,000 people destroyed. Archives, libraries, administrative records, treaty documents — immolated. An empire's memory erased in weeks. |
| c. 1565–1646 CE | Post-Talikota successor states — Aravidu dynasty's fragmented rule | The empire existed in name for another 80 years but held no real central power. Regional Nayaka governors became de facto independent. Each region's relationship with its neighbors needed re-negotiation from scratch — with no central authority to adjudicate. |

---

## VI. The Deccan Sultanates and Mughal Absorption (1347–1687 CE)

**What happened:**
The Bahmani Sultanate and its five successor states (Bijapur, Golconda, Ahmednagar, Bidar, Berar) controlled the northern Deccan, constantly warring with each other and with Vijayanagara, until the Mughal conquest of the Deccan (1636–1687).

**Periods of silence/collapse:**

| Period | Event | Why It Matters |
|--------|-------|---------------|
| c. 1518–1527 CE | Bahmani fragmentation — five successor states emerging | The administrative relationships between communities that had existed *under* the Bahmani state now had to be renegotiated under five different masters. |
| c. 1636–1687 CE | Mughal absorption of the Deccan sultanates | Another complete regime replacement. Hundreds of thousands of people's inter-community relationships became subject to a distant empire that had no knowledge of local agreements. |

---

## VII. Maratha Expansion and the Eighteenth-Century Vacuum (1674–1799 CE)

**What happened:**
The Marathas under Shivaji and his successors challenged Mughal authority. After Aurangzeb's death (1707), the Mughal Deccan collapsed. The Marathas, Nizam of Hyderabad, Hyder Ali, Tipu Sultan, and the British competed for the same territory.

**Periods of silence/collapse:**

| Period | Event | Why It Matters |
|--------|-------|---------------|
| c. 1707–1724 CE | Post-Aurangzeb Deccan collapse | Five competing powers, no clear authority, constant realignment of local communities |
| c. 1761–1799 CE | Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan's Mysore — revolutionary administrative restructuring | Tipu deliberately destroyed older feudal arrangements and replaced them with new administrative systems. Ancient inter-community agreements were systematically dismantled by state policy. |
| c. 1799 CE | Fall of Tipu Sultan — British supremacy begins | Complete transition from indigenous to colonial governance. Every pre-existing non-state arrangement entered a new regime with no knowledge of, or interest in, local binding memory. |

# PART 2: Major Kannada Dynasties — Detailed Profile

---

## A. Badami Chalukyas (543–753 CE)

**Dates:** c. 543–753 CE  
**Capital:** Vatapi (Badami)  
**Territory:** Modern Karnataka, parts of Maharashtra and Andhra Pradesh

**Strengths:** Military capability; temple architecture as political legitimation; epigraphic culture (extensive stone inscriptions); sophisticated revenue system.

**Weaknesses:** Perpetual war with Pallavas drained resources; feudatory loyalty was contingent, not structural; repeated sackings of capital (642 CE by Pallavas) destroyed institutional continuity.

**Administrative system:** Feudal hierarchy — vishayapati (district governors), gramika (village headmen). The king's authority depended on personal relationships with feudatories who could, and did, defect.

**Military system:** Feudatory levies supplemented by standing army. Communication via mounted couriers. Limited cavalry early; later adopted from interactions with northern dynasties.

**Known intelligence methods:** Copper-plate grants recording land grants — but these are *outcomes* of decisions, not the information-gathering that preceded them. No surviving espionage documentation. The Arthashastra tradition prescribes spy systems, but direct evidence for this specific dynasty is inferential, not documentary.

**Record keeping:** Stone inscriptions (thousands survive); copper-plate grants (scores survive); palm-leaf manuscripts (almost all lost — organic material did not survive centuries). The stone record is *monumental* — what kings wanted remembered. The palm-leaf record — administrative, diplomatic, personal — is almost entirely gone.

**Relationship with local communities:** Revenue extraction balanced with temple patronage. Legitimacy required visible piety and visible redistribution. Communities with grievances could appeal through feudatory hierarchies — but non-state dispute resolution between communities is undocumented.

**How information travelled:** Mounted couriers on established routes. River networks. Temple-to-temple communication (priests travelled between temple complexes). Merchant caravans carried news alongside goods.

**How disputes were resolved:** Royal courts for inter-feudatory disputes. Village assemblies (sabhas) for local matters. Between communities that fell under *different* feudatories — mechanisms are undocumented. This is a gap.

**Speculative opportunity:** Who resolved inter-community disputes when both communities' feudatory patrons were in conflict with each other? No recorded mechanism. The gap is structural.

---

## B. Rashtrakutas (753–973 CE)

**Dates:** c. 753–973 CE  
**Capital:** Manyakheta (Malkhed)  
**Territory:** One of India's largest empires — from Gujarat to Tamil Nadu, from the Arabian Sea to the Bay of Bengal at maximum extent.

**Strengths:** Military power projection across unprecedented distances; sophisticated multilingual administration (Kannada, Sanskrit, Prakrit); cultural patronage (Ellora caves); revenue system capable of funding multi-front wars.

**Weaknesses:** Imperial overextension; constant warfare weakened the core; feudatories maintained semi-independence; succession crises every second generation.

**Administrative system:** Mandala system — provinces, districts, sub-districts. Governors (often royal princes) with significant autonomy. Villages retained self-governing assemblies. The gap between imperial command and village reality was vast.

**Military system:** Elephant corps, cavalry, infantry. Professional army supplemented by feudatory levies. Communication across the empire required relay systems historians cannot fully reconstruct.

**Known intelligence methods:** Arab travellers (Al-Masudi, c. 940 CE) describe the Rashtrakuta state as having sophisticated governance, but specific intelligence mechanisms are not documented. The Arthashastra tradition was certainly known. Practical implementation: inferential only.

**Record keeping:** Stone inscriptions (plentiful); copper plates (significant corpus); court literature (substantial in Kannada and Sanskrit). But again: the *administrative* records — the day-to-day governance documentation — has not survived. We know outcomes. We do not know process.

**Relationship with local communities:** Revenue arrangements. Temple grants. Irrigation projects. Merchant guild protections (the Ayyavole 500 merchant guild was active under Rashtrakuta protection). But inter-community relationships *outside* state jurisdiction are undocumented.

**How disputes were resolved:** Royal courts. Village assemblies. Religious arbitrators (Jain monks, Brahmin councils). Merchant guild tribunals. Between communities of different faiths, different castes, different regions — documentation thins to nothing.

**Speculative opportunity:** The Rashtrakutas ruled an empire so large that central control was structurally impossible at the local level. Between communities that were governed by different feudatories, different faith traditions, different caste systems — how was peace maintained? No historian can fully answer this.

---

## C. Kalyani Chalukyas (Western Chalukyas) (973–1189 CE)

**Dates:** c. 973–1189 CE  
**Capital:** Kalyani (modern Basavakalyan)  
**Territory:** Northern Karnataka, parts of Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh

**Strengths:** Cultural efflorescence (Kannada literature peaks); temple architecture; sophisticated administration; ideological legitimacy through Chalukya lineage.

**Weaknesses:** Never achieved the reach of the Rashtrakutas; constantly challenged by Cholas from south and by own feudatories; last century was a slow collapse.

**Administrative system:** Similar to earlier Chalukyas but with stronger feudatory autonomy. Mahamandaleshwaras (great feudatories) were near-independent rulers owing nominal allegiance.

**Known intelligence methods:** The Veerashaiva movement (Basavanna's period) describes a sophisticated network of itinerant preachers (jangamas) moving between communities. While their purpose was religious, their *infrastructure* (routes, contacts, trusted status in multiple communities) is precisely the kind of network that could serve non-religious functions. Documented? No. Plausible? Very.

**Record keeping:** Extensive epigraphic corpus. The Kalyani Chalukya period has some of the densest stone inscription coverage in Indian history. However: inscriptions record *grants and achievements*. They do not record *disputes, agreements, failures, or inter-community tensions*. The negative space is vast.

**Speculative opportunity:** The Basavanna period (c. 1160s) is particularly rich — a social revolution that challenged caste, priestly authority, and inherited status. During this upheaval, pre-existing inter-community agreements based on *old* social categories (caste hierarchy, priestly arbitration) would have been invalidated in some communities while persisting in others. Who mediated between communities that had accepted reform and those that had not? No record exists.

## D. Hoysalas (1026–1346 CE)

**Dates:** c. 1026–1346 CE  
**Capital:** Dvarasamudra (Halebidu), later Belur  
**Territory:** Southern Karnataka, parts of Tamil Nadu

**Strengths:** Temple architecture (among the world's most intricate); strong patronage of Jain, Shaiva, and Vaishnava communities simultaneously; effective local administration; skilled diplomacy.

**Weaknesses:** Smaller than northern empires; vulnerable to invasions from the north; dependent on trade routes controlled by others; ultimately unable to resist Delhi Sultanate's military technology.

**Administrative system:** Nad (district) system with significant village-level autonomy. Village assemblies well-documented (more so than any previous dynasty in the region). Inscriptions record assembly decisions in detail.

**Known intelligence methods:** No direct documentation. However: the Hoysalas maintained simultaneous diplomatic relationships with the Cholas, the Kalyani Chalukyas, the Pandyas, and the Seuna Yadavas — often playing them against each other. This required an information infrastructure. No historian has identified its institutional form.

**Record keeping:** Stone inscriptions (prolific — over 1,500 Hoysala inscriptions survive). Temple records. But: the soft archives of Dvarasamudra were destroyed in 1311 CE. Everything we know about Hoysala governance comes from stone and copper. The *paper trail* — if it existed — was annihilated.

**Speculative opportunity:** The Hoysalas' destruction (1311 CE) is the single most important documentary catastrophe in Karnataka's history. A kingdom's full administrative memory was physically destroyed. Yet the communities that survived the destruction continued to function. They did not descend into anarchic violence. They organized themselves under new rulers (eventually Vijayanagara). *How?* On what basis did these communities re-establish cooperative relationships? The old state's records were gone. Something else held the memory.

---

## E. Vijayanagara Empire (1336–1646 CE)

**Dates:** c. 1336–1646 CE  
**Capitals:** Vijayanagara (Hampi), later Penukonda, Chandragiri  
**Territory:** At peak, most of peninsular India south of the Krishna-Tungabhadra rivers

**Strengths:** Enormous wealth from trade; military innovation (adopted gunpowder, horses from Arabian trade); cultural synthesis; administrative sophistication; religious pluralism despite Hindu identity.

**Weaknesses:** Perpetual warfare with Bahmani/Deccan sultanates; succession crises; dependence on Nayaka governors who eventually became autonomous; the catastrophic vulnerability of a single capital.

**Administrative system:** Nayankara system — provincial governors (Nayakas) held territories as quasi-feudal lords, owing military service. At local level, village assemblies (panchayats) governed daily life. Revenue administration through *ayagars* (village service-holders).

**Known intelligence methods:** Portuguese accounts (16th century) describe Vijayanagara's intelligence capabilities as significant. Fernão Nuniz and Domingo Paes describe the court's access to information about distant enemies. Specific institutional structures: not documented. The *mechanism* by which a city of 500,000 received intelligence from borders hundreds of kilometers away is not explained in surviving sources.

**Record keeping:** Stone inscriptions (thousands); copper plates; palm-leaf manuscripts; possibly paper records (by the late period). Portuguese visitors describe archives and administrative offices. ALL soft records were destroyed in 1565. The post-Talikota capital is a ruin. What survives is stone, foreign traveller accounts, and literary works. The *binding* documents — treaties, agreements, dispute resolutions — are overwhelmingly lost.

**Speculative opportunity:** Vijayanagara's foundation is itself a mystery. How did Harihara and Bukka build a coalition large enough to create an empire from the debris of three collapsed kingdoms (Hoysala, Kakatiya, Seuna Yadava) in barely a decade? The standard answer — they were governors or generals who seized opportunity — does not explain the *mechanism*. Coalition-building across displaced communities, broken feudatories, refugee populations, and demoralized elites required an *infrastructure of trust* that historians cannot identify. Who brokered those alliances?

## THE ONE:

> **The Interregnum: 1311–1336 CE.**

Twenty-five years during which South India had no central authority, no functioning state memory, no public institution capable of maintaining inter-community agreements — and yet communities survived, cooperated, and eventually reconstituted political order.

# PART 6: Why This Period Is Uniquely Suited

---

## Why 1311–1336?

**The civilizational problem that existed ONLY here:**

For the first time in South Indian history, ALL of the following were simultaneously true:

1. **No indigenous state existed.** The Hoysalas were destroyed. The Kakatiyas were destroyed. The Seuna Yadavas were destroyed. No local power could adjudicate inter-community disputes.

2. **The conquering state was incompetent at local governance.** The Delhi Sultanate held cities but had no administrative depth in the countryside. Its governors had no knowledge of local agreements, no interest in local relationships, and no capacity for inter-community arbitration.

3. **The documentary record was physically destroyed.** Halebidu's archives were gone. Temple records were damaged or scattered. The *soft* memory of the state — its knowledge of who owed what to whom — was annihilated.

4. **The population had not dispersed.** Unlike a famine or plague, the invasion destroyed the *state* but left the *population* largely in place. Communities that had coexisted under Hoysala authority still lived next to each other. Their agreements still mattered. But the authority that had adjudicated them was gone.

5. **Multiple religions, castes, and linguistic communities shared the same territory** — without any overarching framework for managing their coexistence. Hindu communities, Jain communities, Muslim communities (new arrivals), various caste configurations, multiple linguistic groups — all sharing space without a state to mediate.

---

## Why Not 200 Years Earlier (c. 1100 CE)?

In 1100 CE, the Hoysalas and Kalyani Chalukyas were functional states. Inter-community disputes had a court system. Agreements had custodians (the state itself). The civilizational problem the Gumma solve did not yet exist at crisis intensity.

There were precursors (the Gavunda system, the oath-witnesses, the temple mediators). But these operated as *informal supplements* to state function. They did not need to be an *institution* because the state still worked.

**The Gumma do not emerge during stability. They emerge when the infrastructure of trust collapses completely and must be rebuilt from the ground up.**

---

## Why Not 300 Years Later (c. 1600 CE)?

By 1600 CE, the problem was already being managed. The Nayaka successor states, the Deccan Sultanates, the Mughal administration — all provided *some* framework for inter-community relations. Imperfect, often unjust, but present.

More importantly: by 1600 CE, written record-keeping was far more developed. Portuguese presence meant foreign witnesses. Mughal administration meant centralized archives. The *opacity* necessary for the Gumma's secrecy was declining.

The Gumma needed to be born in a period dark enough to permit their existence without leaving documentary traces — and desperate enough to require their function.

Only 1311–1336 satisfies both conditions simultaneously.

---

## The Specific Civilizational Problem

Between 1311 and 1336, the Deccan faced a problem unprecedented in its history:

> **Hundreds of communities that had coexisted under Hoysala/Chalukya authority now had no arbiter, no custodian of their mutual agreements, no mechanism for dispute resolution, and no documentary proof of what had been agreed — because the state that held all of this had been physically destroyed.**

This is not a war. It is not a famine. It is not a succession crisis.

It is a **civilizational memory collapse** — the sudden, total destruction of the institutional infrastructure that made coexistence possible.

In this void, someone — some group of people — must have performed the function: remembering what was agreed, arbitrating what was disputed, maintaining what was binding. Otherwise, the Deccan would have collapsed into perpetual inter-community violence. It did not.

**Who held it together?**

Historians genuinely do not know.

The Gumma are our answer.

# PART 7: Attempting to Destroy the Conclusion

---

## Alternative 1: The Rashtrakuta Collapse (973 CE)

**Case for:**
- Also a major empire collapsing
- Also documentary disruption
- Earlier = more time for the Gumma to develop
- The Manyakheta destruction was significant

**Case against:**
- The Chalukyas of Kalyani *immediately* replaced the Rashtrakutas
- The transition was military, not civilizational — the new state adopted existing administrative structures
- No period of statelessness: one dynasty replaced another within months
- Communities were not left without an arbiter — the new state claimed the old state's authority immediately
- The documentary gap is smaller: the Kalyani Chalukyas actively documented their own legitimacy by referencing Rashtrakuta precedents

**Verdict:** The Rashtrakuta collapse was a *regime change*, not a *civilizational memory collapse*. The state was replaced, not destroyed. The Gumma's function was not existentially needed — because someone (the new state) immediately claimed custody of the old arrangements.

**1311–1336 survives this challenge.** In the Interregnum, no indigenous state replaced the fallen one. The vacuum was absolute.

---

## Alternative 2: The Kalachuri-Basavanna Period (1160–1200 CE)

**Case for:**
- Social revolution (Basavanna) challenged all inherited obligations
- Political instability (Kalachuri usurpation)
- Genuine crisis of legitimacy — who held authority?
- Strong narrative appeal (reform vs. tradition)
- Culturally unique to Karnataka

**Case against:**
- A state still existed throughout. The Kalachuris and then the restored Chalukyas provided (imperfect) governance.
- The Basavanna movement was primarily *intra-community* (within the Hindu social order), not *inter-community* (between different communities sharing territory)
- The Gumma's function is inter-community binding memory. The Basavanna crisis was about *intra-community* hierarchy reform.
- Documentary coverage of this period is actually *good* — extensive inscriptions, literary evidence, historical accounts. The Gumma need darkness. This period is well-lit.

**Verdict:** The Basavanna period is an excellent setting for a *story* within the Gumma Universe (a crisis the Gumma navigated) but is not the founding period. The founding requires: (a) complete state collapse, (b) inter-community crisis, (c) documentary darkness. The Basavanna era provides only (a) partially.

**1311–1336 survives this challenge.**

---

## Alternative 3: Post-Talikota Collapse (1565–1600 CE)

**Case for:**
- Catastrophic destruction (Vijayanagara city destroyed)
- Massive documentary loss
- Political vacuum
- Population displacement
- Multiple competing powers
- More globally known (easier for international readers)

**Case against:**
- The vacuum was not absolute. Nayaka governors *immediately* claimed independent authority. The Keladi Nayakas, Madurai Nayakas, Thanjavur Nayakas — all provided governance within their territories within years.
- By 1565, written record-keeping was far more developed. Portuguese records, Mughal administration, and local temple records provide significant documentary coverage.
- The period is too well-documented for the Gumma to hide. Foreign travelers, multiple court chronicles, and surviving administrative records make total invisibility implausible.
- Most critically: if the Gumma only formed in 1565, they had only ~250 years before colonial disruption (c. 1800). The franchise needs the Gumma to be old enough to have *deep* institutional memory. A 250-year-old institution does not carry the same weight as a 500-year-old one.

**Verdict:** Post-Talikota is an excellent *crisis* the Gumma survived and thrived during — proof of their value. But as a founding period, it is too late, too well-documented, and too quickly resolved by successor states.

**1311–1336 is confirmed as the founding period.**

---

## Final Confirmation

**The Lost Century is approximately 1150–1350 CE** — the period from late Chalukya decline through the Interregnum to early Vijayanagara.

**The founding moment is 1311–1336 CE** — the twenty-five years of statelessness.

**The precursor period is c. 800–1150 CE** — during which the Gavunda system, oath-witnesses, temple mediators, and merchant guild arbitrators operated as *informal* functions that the Interregnum forced into conscious institutionalization.

**The first proof of existence is c. 1336–1356 CE** — the Vijayanagara coalition-building that required a trust infrastructure no historian can identify.
