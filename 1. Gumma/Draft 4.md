# Chapter 1 — Visitor Parking

## S01 — Tagged

The rice was still warm enough to eat without reheating.

Meera took the plate from the microwave, felt the heat through ceramic, and set it on the kitchen counter where evening light had gone thin and yellow. She did not bother with a proper seat. Hip against the edge. Spoon in hand. Work clothes still on — the soft kurta she wore for office days that did not require a jacket, jeans that had held their shape through a long Wednesday. Outside, Malleshwaram released the day's heat from concrete and glass. The window screen held no breeze. The ceiling fan turned with the useless sincerity of machines that know they are decorative after seven.

She had been looking forward to this exact nothing: leftover rice, phone facedown later, the small private theatre of being alone without performing competence for anyone.

First bite. A little dry. Oil at the edges. Fine.

Her phone lay face-up beside the salt, screen dark, charging cable still attached from when she had dropped her bag an hour earlier. She had meant to put it in another room. She never did.

It buzzed once against the counter.

She chewed. Did not look. The group could wait. Everything in every group could wait. That was the rule she had made for evenings, and she had kept it for almost a week, which, for her, counted as discipline.

Another buzz. Then a third, close enough that the vibration travelled into the plate and made a soft ring against the spoon.

Meera swallowed and kept eating. Traffic murmured beyond the compound wall. A pressure cooker two floors down hissed and finished. She wanted them to be enough.

The fourth buzz brought a preview that slid across the glass before she meant to see it.

*unauthorized visitor vehicle*

She put the spoon down.

Not yet, she told herself, and wiped her fingers on a tissue, delaying by half a breath the thing that had already arrived. Then she pulled the cable free and unlocked the phone.

The apartment WhatsApp group was a climbing stack. Messages over messages. Asha's name at the head of most of them, the way Asha's name always found the head of things.

*Visitor Bay 3 — vehicle without sticker. Photo attached. Timestamp 6:48 p.m.*

*Bylaw 4.2. Visitor parking is for visitors only. Residents are requested to comply.*

*Who parked this?*

*Second reminder. Photo clear. Number plate visible.*

Meera scrolled with her thumb, the rice cooling beside her hand.

The photo loaded slowly on her network, then all at once: bay paint, a strip of evening concrete, a white hatchback angled a little wrong. The plate number sharp under whatever light Asha had used. Caption neat as a filled form.

Visitor Bay #3.

Meera's thumb stopped.

She knew the car before she let herself know it. The small scratch near the rear bumper from a Bastille Day parking adventure Divya still told as comedy. The faded sticker on the windshield Divya had never peeled. The way it sat — Divya always parked a little crooked and laughed when anyone mentioned it.

I parked that this morning.

She pinched the screen. Zoomed. Released. Plate unchanged. Bay unchanged. Timestamp offering no alternate morning in which she had been wiser.

More messages arrived while she stared.

*Please identify.*

*This is not the first time with visitor bays.*

*If residents use visitor slots, actual visitors have to double-park. Kindly note.*

*@Meera — is this yours?*

Her name sat in the chat like a sticker on a parcel nobody wanted to claim.

She checked the photo's time against the morning she remembered: Divya in the doorway with a trolley bag, late for the airport train, spare key pressed into Meera's palm, a quick *just till evening, na*, and the visitor bay empty and convenient and, Meera had thought, the sort of small wrong that dissolved by dinner.

Facts lined up. They did not argue.

The rice waited, half-eaten, grains going stiff at the rim. She did not pick up the spoon again.

Her thumb hovered over reply.

*Sorry — moving it now.*

Too thin. She deleted.

*It's my friend's — temporary —*

Already a defence. Deleted before it became a sentence.

In the group, blue ticks appeared under Asha's photo. People were looking. Names she knew from Diwali sweets and AGM attendance and the hellos of people who shared a lift and little else.

Heat pressed at the window glass. The fan kept its useless circle. On the counter the plate cooled by degrees she would feel later with the back of her hand.

Meera stood with the phone warm in her palm and the meal unfinished, her name still bright on the screen beside the bay.

## S02 — Keys Upstairs

She left the plate where it was.

Bag in pocket. Flat door shut with more care than the situation deserved. The stairwell smelled of dust, phenyl, old paint. Tube light on the second landing flickered once and held. Her slippers slapped concrete in a rhythm that felt too loud, as if the building were listening for the person attached to Bay 3.

By the ground floor her face was warm in a way that had nothing to do with March.

Visitor Bay #3 was exactly where the photo had said.

Divya's hatchback. Still. Slightly crooked. The painted number at her feet faded to the colour of old teeth. Heat rose off the concrete in a slow sheet. Somewhere a scooter reversed with a complaining whine. The air smelled of dust and hot metal and the faint sweetness of something rotting in a drain the association had been meaning to fix since last monsoon.

Asha was already there.

Phone in hand, screen angled toward the plate, posture of a person completing documentation rather than starting a quarrel. She looked up when Meera's slippers reached the bay. No smile. No sneer. Only the tight composure of someone who had been correct since morning and saw no reason to stop.

"Meera. Thank you for coming down."

Behind Asha, near the scooter row, an uncle from the third floor stood with his hands linked behind his back. Watching. Not commenting. Watching that turned a private mistake into corridor conversation by tomorrow's milk delivery. Meera knew his face from festival sweets and from the way he always pressed the lift button twice. She had never disliked him. She disliked, suddenly and precisely, that he was here.

She nodded once and went to the driver's door as if speed itself could shrink the audience. The handle bit heat into her palm. She reached for the spare key she was certain she had dropped into her pocket upstairs —

Empty.

Other pocket. Empty. Kurta side pocket: phone, nothing else. She patted once more, uselessly, hoping physics might revise itself.

Of course.

Divya's spare was in the bag on the kitchen chair, dropped there that morning while Meera was already late for a stand-up. She saw it with insulting clarity: strap hanging, side pocket, key under a lip balm and a folded bill from the kirana. The morning had been hurry and kindness. The evening had turned both into evidence.

"I'll move it," Meera said. "Now."

Asha did not step into her path. She also did not make space as if space were a gift. She remained where she was, phone still lit, the small circle of light finding the number plate and then the painted 3. Sweat had made a faint shine at Asha's temple. Even correctness had a body.

"Bay three is visitor only," Asha said. "As per the circular last month. If residents use it, visitors have nowhere. You understand."

Meera's mouth found something short. "I understand. Friend's car. Airport. I'll shift it."

"Acknowledgment helps," Asha said, mild as water left too long in a bottle. "So we don't have to keep posting photos. The group gets… noisy."

As if the noise had arrived by weather.

Meera could have said that Divya was gone, that the bay had been empty, that she would not make a habit of it. Each sentence arrived pre-weighed and too heavy. In committee English she was always a step behind. She heard herself offer only the logistics again, thinner: "I'm moving it."

"Yes," Asha said. "After you have the key."

Not unkind. Exact.

The uncle shifted his weight from one slipper to the other. Looked at the bay number. Looked at the hatchback. Looked away toward the gate, where a guard was arguing with a delivery boy about a parcel that would not fit on a bike. Said nothing. Meera felt that if he had joked or scolded it might have been easier than this neutral attention, the attention of a man who would remember and not intervene.

Meera's phone buzzed against her hip — the group still chewing. She did not take it out. Somewhere above, a child's voice called for water and was answered. Life continued in layers that did not know about Bay 3.

"Key's upstairs," Meera said. The sentence came out flatter than pride preferred. "Two minutes."

Asha's flashlight clicked brighter. The beam found the painted 3 and held it — white number in a hard white circle, a parking bay turned into a stage with one prop and an audience of two. The light caught a moth, lost it. The number remained.

"I'll wait," Asha said.

No cruelty in it. Only the calm of a person who had nowhere else she needed to be more than here.

Meera turned toward the stairs. Her slippers found the first step. Then the second. The hatchback stayed where it was, crooked, innocent of committee language. Heat came off the wall beside her hand.

The climb was longer than the descent. Air trapped in the stairwell, thick with cooking from somebody's delayed dinner. Behind her the flashlight stayed on the bay, and every step stretched a small wrong.

She did not look back. She did not need to. The light on the number followed her up the concrete.

## S03 — Face-Down

The spare was exactly where she had pictured it — cold metal, side pocket, under the lip balm.

She came back down with it fisted, unlocked Divya's car under Asha's still-bright phone, and did not offer further speech. The uncle had drifted closer to the gate. She started the engine, reversed with care she hoped looked ordinary, and drove the hatchback to the far open spot near the compound wall — the one nobody preferred because the gulmohar dropped sticky litter and birds used the mirror as a perch.

Thirty seconds of competence. Gear. Wheel. The small clean sequence of putting a thing where it was allowed to be. Her shoulders loosened.

Then it was done. Bay #3 empty behind her. Asha still in the lot with her phone, finishing whatever record needed finishing. Meera locked the car, walked back without hurrying, and took the stairs because waiting for the lift would have meant standing still in fluorescent light with her own face.

Upstairs, the flat had not moved to meet her.

The rice sat on the counter, dull and cold, a skin forming at the rim. She shut the door and stood with her back against it for a count of three, then put the key in the bag as if returning an object to its place could return an hour.

Her phone had not rested.

Asha's new message sat at the top of the group like minutes from a meeting Meera had not agreed to attend.

*Update: Vehicle in Visitor Bay 3 has been moved by resident. Residents are reminded that visitor bays are not for personal / guest overflow parking. Please cooperate so we don't need to escalate next time. Cleared-bay photo attached for record. Timestamp 8:14 p.m.*

Below it, read receipts multiplied. Names from Diwali. Names from the complaint about generator noise. Blue ticks stacking without replies — people who had seen enough and agreed by looking.

Meera opened a reply box.

*Noted. It was a one-time situation —*

Delete. Already defensive.

*Sorry for the inconvenience. Won't happen again. The car belongs to a friend who —*

Too much. Delete.

*Moved. Bay free.*

Too clipped. Sounded angry. Or guilty. Delete.

She typed a fourth beginning — *Thanks for flagging* — and erased it before the second word finished, because thanks would look like submission and no thanks would look like contempt and she had run out of angles that did not cost something she did not want to pay on a Wednesday.

The ticks continued. The fan turned its slow circle. Night heat sat in the rooms like a held breath. Somewhere a neighbour's television laughed on cue.

She turned the phone face-down on the counter.

Light leaked through the case — thin, blue-white, the chat still alive underneath, ticks still arriving in a world she had decided not to watch. She scraped the cold rice into the bin. The spoon chimed once against steel. A few grains clung to the plate; she rinsed them without ceremony and left the plate in the rack.

Then the flat held only the fan and the small glow that would not quite go dark.

Outside, Malleshwaram went on being Wednesday — horns, a dog, someone's late scooter. Inside, a phone lay face-down on laminate, blue light stacking unread beneath plastic.

# Chapter 2 — The Hallway Promise

## S01 — The Shoulder Touch

Naveen stood three metres from Vikram's glass cabin with his phone open to notes he had rewritten twice that morning and once in the auto on the way in.

Bullet points. Clean. Professional-casual. *Equity structure — ask, don't accuse. Vesting clarity. Timeline that isn't "soon." Calendar hold before we leave the conversation.* The words looked like a person who had slept. He had not slept well. Outside the corridor windows the March glare sat white on the glass, turning the street into a washed photograph; inside, the AC hummed its expensive indifference. Carpet under his shoes, the slightly too-soft kind that made every step sound like apology. Somewhere down the hall a printer coughed out a page and went quiet.

He had asked for a quick sync. Vikram had said, earlier in the week, with the brightness of a man who meant it in the moment, *catch up*. Today was the catch-up. Naveen's pulse knew it before his face arranged itself into something suitable for fluorescent light.

The cabin was empty when he arrived. Vikram's jacket hung on the chair inside, good jacket, better cut than Naveen's, and a laptop open to something dense with slides, the board deck probably, the thing that ate weeks. Through the glass the open-plan floor swam in calm: heads, monitors, the soft clatter of keyboards pretending urgency was evenly distributed. Meera was at her desk in the far cluster, headphones on, frowning at a ticket the way she frowned when a dependency had lied to her. She did not look up. Good. He did not want an audience for hope. Hope looked stupid in hallways.

A colleague from growth stood at the printer with a sheaf of papers, scrolling his own phone between page grabs. Close enough to see a conversation. Not close enough, Naveen told himself, to hear one. Public enough that pressing would become a story. He knew that. He had still come.

Eleven twenty-two. He refreshed nothing on his notes. The notes did not need refreshing. They needed a person on the other side who would stay still long enough to answer them.

The cabin door opened.

Vikram came out moving already, phone in one hand, the other lifting in greeting before his eyes had fully found Naveen. The smile arrived on schedule: warm, quick, the smile of a man who remembered names and birthdays and the precise temperature at which loyalty stayed liquid. His shirt looked pressed. His watch caught the glare and threw it.

"Hey — yes, I wanted to catch you too," Vikram said, as if Naveen had not been the one waiting with a script in his palm.

Naveen's prepared opener shortened in his mouth, the way prepared things always did in weather. "Got a minute? On the ESOP thing — just want to understand the structure, lock a proper time on calendar—"

Vikram's hand landed on his shoulder.

Warm. Firm. A touch that photographed well in a company culture deck. Naveen felt the heat of it through cotton and felt, stupidly, younger.

"Absolutely," Vikram said. "Let's lock it."

Naveen almost believed the sentence had edges.

Then, still smiling, still touching: "After the board deck ships next week. Once that's out of the way we can sit properly. You know how it is — this week's a wash. Back-to-backs. You of all people get it."

The hand stayed a beat longer than the sentence needed, as if duration could substitute for a calendar invite. Vikram's body had already angled toward the cabin again, toward the next eight minutes, toward whatever meeting waited behind glass with its slides and its hunger. His tone did not cool. The deferral arrived wrapped in the same heat as the promise, gift-paper over an empty box.

"Next week," Naveen said, and heard how little it contained. He had three follow-ups ready. None of them fit in a corridor with a colleague at a printer and Vikram's phone lighting up against his thigh.

"Next week," Vikram echoed, brighter. "You've been patient. I see it. We sort this. You're early team, Naveen. That doesn't evaporate because we're busy."

Early team. The phrase landed with the soft weight of something reused.

At the printer, the growth colleague glanced over, saw the shoulder, the smile, the performance of care, and looked back at his papers with the mild envy of a person who had not been patted. Appearance filed. Content unread. Meera, far across the floor, might have seen the same silhouette through glass and desks: two men, one hand, a conversation that looked like mentorship from ten metres away.

Vikram squeezed once, released, and turned.

His back crossed the carpet, passed through the glass door, became a shape behind glare and slides. The cabin sealed with a soft corporate click. Naveen stood with his phone still open to talking points he had not used, the shoulder warm where a hand had been, the warmth already beginning to leave the way heat leaves a mug.

Same cadence.

2021. Night. Vikram's old car smelling of coffee and new plastic, the city sliding past in streaks. Streetlight over the dashboard. Naveen still high on a pilot that had nearly failed and then hadn't. Vikram's voice easy, intimate, the voice of a founder before the vocabulary of decks. *You're early team. When we raise, we take care of our people. I'll make sure you're looked after.* Hand on shoulder then too. Same pressure. Same brightness. Same *after* tucked inside the *yes*, so neatly you could miss it if you wanted the yes enough.

Series A had come in 2023. Parties. Photos. A cake with the company logo slightly wrong. The paper had not come. Naveen had told himself timing. Timing had become weather. Weather had become the corridor.

He closed the notes app without saving anything new. There was nothing new to save. Somewhere a Slack notification pinged from a pocket that was not his. His own shoulder cooled by degrees he could measure only because he was standing still enough to notice.

He walked back toward the open floor carrying nothing he could show on a calendar. Only the fading print of a hand.

## S02 — Cooling Coffees

At his desk the tickets refused to become English.

Naveen opened one about a latency spike. Closed it. Opened Slack. Closed Slack. The corridor replayed not as dialogue but as geometry: Vikram's angle of departure, the colleague's glance that had mistaken theatre for substance, the glass door sealing on a week that would become another week. Eleven-forty. Eleven-fifty. He drank water from a bottle that tasted of plastic and office. His notes app sat unused under other apps, a fossil of preparation.

People moved around him with the noon hunger of open plan. Someone microwaved something that smelled of sambar. Someone laughed too loudly into a headset. Ordinary Thursday. His body had not received the memo.

Meera pulled one headphone off when he stopped beside her monitor. Her screen showed a sprint board in mid-argument with itself, red tickets, a dependency named after a person who was on leave. She looked tired in the precise way of people who had slept and still owed the night something.

"Lunch?" he said.

She glanced at the clock, at him, at the hallway that led past Vikram's glass. Whatever she had seen from her desk that morning sat behind her eyes without announcing itself. "You look like a person who needs coffee more than food."

"Both. Neither. Come."

"Five minutes. Let me park this comment so it doesn't look like I vanished mid-war."

He waited. She typed three lines with the economy of someone who had learned that long comments were where blame hid. Then she stood, took her phone, left the headphones on the desk like a promise to return to a different kind of noise.

They took the stairs because the lift meant standing still with other bodies and the risk of Vikram's voice arriving bright from a different floor. Down one level the café breathed its noon crowd. Laptops, salads in plastic, other people's deadlines braided low. They ordered without ceremony, the way they had ordered since the month they both started, when this table by the window had been theirs before it belonged to anyone. Two coffees. No food. Naveen almost added a sandwich and didn't. Appetite was elsewhere.

The table came free. Cups arrived with a soft ceramic knock. Steam lifted, thinned, began to fail.

Naveen wrapped his hands around his cup and did not drink. Meera slid his saucer a centimetre toward him without looking, the way she always did when his elbows wandered. A habit from their first year, when standups ran long and lunch was the only place a sentence could finish.

"Had the chat with Vikram," he said, keeping it light, keeping it like a status update you could put in a standup. "The equity thing."

Meera blew once across her coffee and waited. She had always been good at waiting without filling.

"He said absolutely. Let's lock it. After the board deck next week."

She tilted her head the way she did when a requirement sounded complete and wasn't. "Did he say no?"

The question landed exactly where he had hoped it would not. Clean. Practical. A blade made of common sense.

"No," Naveen said. "But—"

He stopped. The *but* had nowhere factual to go. Vikram had not refused. Vikram had affirmed. Vikram had called him early team with the warmth of a man who believed his own myth in real time. The wound was in the temperature of the affirmation, in the body already leaving, in the shoulder used as punctuation. Saying that aloud sounded like a person inventing injury for sport.

Meera watched him. Not unkind. "Then what happened exactly?"

Naveen looked at the window glare, at a scooter flashing past outside with a delivery bag strapped on, at the two cups between them still full, steam now only a rumour.

"He said the same thing in 2021," he said, and his voice dropped without his deciding it. "Car ride. After we closed that first ugly pilot, the one that nearly killed us. Same words. Same—" He touched his own shoulder once, a small stupid gesture, then let his hand fall to the table. "Same hand. *We'll lock it. You're early team.* Then Series A. Cake. Photos. Then nothing on paper. Today was the same movie with better lighting."

Meera's face changed. Not much. A shift around the eyes, the mouth holding still as if a laugh or a curse had been considered and rejected. She had seen the hallway from her desk; now she had the rhyme.

Neither of them said *he's lying*. The word was too clean for what had happened. Lies were edges you could point at in a meeting. This was weather.

Silence held between them. Spoons. A laugh from the next table about someone's wedding. The espresso machine's short violence. Their coffees cooled by degrees you could see: surface still, heat retreating from the ceramic into the room's indifference.

Meera's phone lit on the table beside her wrist. A WhatsApp preview, apartment group, Asha's name half-visible in the stack, the blue of a world that enforced itself with screenshots. She dismissed it with one thumb, no comment, eyes returning to Naveen. He saw it. She did not offer it. He did not ask. Parallel weather, untranslated.

"I don't know what you do with that," she said finally. Not advice. An admission of limit, offered without apology.

"Neither do I."

She held his gaze.

He almost said *maybe I'm overreading*. He did not. She had already seen his face when he named 2021. Overreading was a story you told yourself to keep opening tickets.

Meera turned her cup a quarter-turn on the saucer. "Next week," she said, testing the phrase the way you tested a loose tooth with your tongue.

They had a name for it between them, never written down, from the month Vikram first used the phrase and Meera had looked at Naveen across this same table and said, without smiling, *There it is. Next week.*

"Next week," Naveen agreed, and neither of them smiled.

A tray clattered somewhere behind the counter. Someone called a name that was not theirs. The world continued its Thursday with insulting competence.

The cups stayed full. The coffee went on cooling toward the temperature of the room.

# Chapter 3 — Ticket PDF

## S01 — Three Missed Calls

The kitchen light made everything look later than it was.

Meera set her bag on the chair by the door, the same chair that had held Divya's spare key two nights ago, and kicked off her slippers. The flat smelled faintly of the morning's coffee and of rooms that had held heat all day without complaint. Fan on high, blades chopping the air into motion that was not coolness. Through the window, Bengaluru's Friday evening was a continuous hush of tyres and someone's television laughter two floors down. On the sill, a basil plant she had bought after a bad sprint and forgotten to water properly sat brown at the edges, still trying.

Her phone lay face-up on the counter where she had dropped it when she came in.

Three missed calls. Amma. Timestamps marching through the afternoon like a complaint filed in triplicate. 2:14, during the architecture review she could not leave. 4:02, during the one-on-one where she had nodded at problems that were not hers. 6:41, watched arrive on the desk during standup, blue and insistent, while she let it die into voicemail with the cowardice of people who loved their mothers and still needed ten minutes of unmarked air.

Week nearly done. Nothing in the fridge worth inventing into dinner. She filled a glass from the filter and drank standing up, performing the evening as if missed calls were a weather system that might pass if she did not look at the sky.

She put the phone face-down.

Washed her hands. The apartment group had been quieter today, Asha on generator hours, polite thumbs-ups, and Meera had scrolled past with the thin pride of surviving Wednesday's bay. Naveen's face at the café sat behind her ribs without asking to be unpacked. Manageable.

The phone buzzed against laminate.

Fourth. Or a message that would become a fourth if ignored. The counter transmitted the vibration into her hip where she leaned.

Meera turned it over, saw Amma, exhaled through her nose, and called back before her body could invent another chore.

Saroja answered on the first ring, as if she had been holding the phone in a grip that denied the possibility of further delay.

"Finally," Saroja said, not unkind, already moving at logistics speed. "I thought maybe you fell into one of those office holes and forgot you have a family. Are you home? Eat something. You sound like you haven't eaten since Tuesday."

"I'm fine, Amma."

"Fine is what you say when the rice is finished and you're drinking water for dinner. Listen. Ananya's things are moving fast. Pallavi is asking about who is coming from our side, as if I'm a booking agent. Lakshmi keeps saying your name like I'm hiding you in a cupboard. When are you coming?"

Meera leaned her hip harder against the counter. The phone warmed against her ear. Fan blades strobed the wall with soft, repeating shadow. Outside, a scooter braked too late and someone swore without heat.

"I'll look at dates this weekend, Amma."

"This weekend." Saroja repeated it the way she repeated recipes that had gone wrong. Not shouting, correcting. "Beta, the wedding is not a cloud. It has a date. You need to be here before the fifth if you can. Better fourth evening, then you can help. There is so much. Flowers. The cousins from Mysore who will arrive early and expect coffee every twenty minutes. Your father won't come, you know that, so you are the only one representing properly from our—"

"Amma."

"—side. Don't Amma me through the important part. Ananya is expecting you. She asked twice. Once in the morning, once when they were measuring for the entrance thing. Lakshmi asked this morning, *Is Meera coming or only sending parcel?* As if my daughter is a courier service. I told her you're coming. Don't make me a liar in my own mother's house."

Ananya would notice absence. Lakshmi would notice with the quiet accuracy of women who counted bodies without counting aloud. Meera's father would not come, that fact had hardened years ago, and so the duty of being seen fell where it always fell.

She stared at the stove clock. 8:41. Numbers indifferent. A magnet held a takeaway menu she never used.

"I have a project mid-stretch," she said. "If I leave too early, the release—"

"Everyone has a project. The wedding does not wait for Jira." Saroja's voice climbed half a note, then softened. "I'm not asking you to move permanently. Come. Be seen. Help your cousin not feel like the city forgot her. And your Ramesh-mama is being impossible these days, some land thing, I don't know, he's in a mood, so the house needs extra hands who can smile and not argue. You can do that. You were always good at that."

Ramesh-mama. Impossible. Land thing. Meera let it pass the way she let most village weather pass when she was in Malleshwaram with a fan and a glass of water: noted, unopened, filed under Uncles And Weddings. She said, "Mm," and meant nothing by it except that she had heard a noise shaped like information and would not chase it down a phone line on a Friday.

"Before the fifth," Saroja pressed. "Book something. Don't do that thing where you look and look and then the tickets are three thousand and you blame the universe, which is the same as blaming me when I'm the one calling."

"I don't blame you for ticket prices."

"You blame timing. Timing is also me, apparently." A breath, shorter. Then warmer, the voice that still undid her when she was not careful: "Just come, kanna. Lakshmi will make the lemon rice you pretend you don't miss. Sleep in your old room. Let the house see your face before all the strangers arrive."

Meera's mouth found the sentence before her plans finished their committee meeting.

"I'll book tonight, Amma."

Silence for half a second. Surprise, or satisfaction disguised as it.

"Good," Saroja said, already shifting to the next cell. "Send me the screenshot. Not tomorrow morning when you've talked yourself into Monday. Tonight. And tell that office of yours you're going for family. They can survive without you for a few days. The world survived before product managers."

"Okay."

"Okay is not a screenshot."

"I'll send it."

"Love you. Eat something that is not water. You sound like fluorescent lights."

"Love you, Amma."

The call ended with Saroja's small click, the sound of a person who had gotten what she needed and still had three other calls to make. Meera lowered the phone.

Her own mouth, offering *tonight* like a gift. She set the phone on the counter face-up this time, as if honesty of position could substitute for the booking she had not yet made.

The stove clock read 8:58.

## S02 — Non-Refundable

By 9:30 she had moved the problem to the bed, as if geography could make obligation softer.

Laptop open on a pillow that still held the dent of last night's poor sleep. Kurta exchanged for an old T-shirt. Fan on high, its round shadow crossing the wall in steady arcs. Phone dark beside her knee, screen asleep, Saroja's satisfaction temporarily fed. The bedroom held the day's heat in the mattress and the curtains. Laptop fans whirred; the machine added its own small summer to her thighs.

The booking site loaded with cheerful colours and a stock photo of a bus that looked cleaner than any bus she had taken. She searched the route she knew by habit more than longing: Bengaluru to the belt of towns that thinned into autos, cousin-voices, jasmine in plastic bags, the house where childhood had smelled of rain on red earth and Lakshmi's kitchen.

Options appeared. Dates in neat rows. Seat maps. Prices that pretended to be invitations.

She scanned for something that suited her, midweek later, after the project spit out its current fire, after the release window stopped punishing absences with looks. A Tuesday evening. A Wednesday with buffer and dignity. Her finger hovered. Optimism, brief as AC during load-shedding: maybe family math would bend if she arrived smiling hard enough on a later bus.

Then Saroja's constraint returned, exact as a calendar invite she had not accepted: *before the fifth if you can — better fourth evening.*

The dates that suited Meera arrived too late for being seen, for helping with flowers and Mysore cousins and coffee every twenty minutes, for not becoming the relative who sent a parcel and a carefully worded excuse. She ran the work week in her head without opening the work calendar, mid-project, dependencies named after people, the tightness in her manager's jaw when personal life became an out-of-office. Double bind. No date wore both hats without tearing a seam.

She typed Saturday, 4 April.

# Chapter 4 — Wire Shift

Chappals on the washed step. To his left, beyond the wire, Gowda land began with the same crop and a different accounting of pride. Wedding in sixteen days sat somewhere behind his thoughts like a date circled in someone else's handwriting, Saroja's calls about flowers and cousins, the alliance with Venki's people that made every smile in the house a little tighter, Ananya moving through rooms as if the air itself were being measured. Not dramatically. Not a hole, not a washout. A compression, as if the ground had been asked to do the same work in less space. His stride shortened without instruction from his mind. The stick found earth a fraction sooner than habit expected. He stopped.

Looked at the wire.

The fence ran along the Gowda edge the way it always had, except it did not. Six inches. Eight, maybe. Inward. Toward him. Toward Hegde walking space. A shift a hurried man might miss and a man who had walked this line since his father's funeral would feel in the ankle before he had a word for it. He stepped closer, the mist thinning around his knees. Followed the wire with his eyes: old posts, familiar rust, the lean of one that had been reset after the '19 storm when the whole belt had looked like a broken broom. Then, newer wood. Cleaner cut. He crouched.

Knees complained in the language of age. He ignored them. At the base of the new post, cement. Pale against the red earth. Smooth where a trowel had finished its work, a small apron of grey holding the wood as if permanence could be poured. He put his fingers on it.

Cooler than the surrounding ground.

Not last month's work pretending to be permanent. Fresh. Yesterday's work, or the day before, while he had been in Mandya arguing mildly about the price of wire he had not imagined would be used against him. Someone had waited for the path to be without him. Someone had brought a post and a bag of cement and the quiet confidence of men who move boundaries when the owner's back is turned and the wedding's smile makes loudness expensive.

His father's walk had been wider.

The thought arrived without poetry and without permission. Appa's stick on this earth. Confidence restored itself in the joke. # Chapter 10 — Broken Post

Chappals on the washed step. Dhoti and shirt, morning clothes, field clothes, the uniform of a man who checked his land before the house checked him. Red earth under chappals, packed hard by decades of feet and cart wheels and the stubbornness of routes that existed before anyone needed a sentence to defend them. Since Mandya. Since fresh cement. Since the morning he had pressed his palm against cool grey and felt inheritance narrowing by six inches, eight.

His farmworker was ahead on the path. Raju, young, obedient, following instructions Ramesh had given without fully examining them: use the path demonstratively, rake it, carry loads through it, assert presence. A bundle of cuttings in the other hand. He walked with the deliberate pace of someone performing use, unaware, or aware in the way workers were aware without saying, that performance on contested ground was its own risk.

He checked the wire each morning the way other men checked crop, looking for wrongness without wanting to find it, finding it anyway. Wedding in ten days sat behind his thoughts like a date circled in someone else's handwriting. The field was older than alliance.

Ramesh's vigilance sharpened.

Prasad.

He knew the boy by sight, Venki's side, twenty-two or thereabouts, jeans and shirt even at this hour, the kind of young man who carried family honour in his chest like a stone he had not chosen but could not put down. Hotheaded. Sent, or self-sent, to be present on the path the way Raju had been sent. Proxy warriors.

The narrow section.

Where the wire had moved, six inches, eight, the path compressed until two men could not pass without one yielding. The throat of the dispute. Raju had reached it first, rake still on shoulder, and had not stopped, following instructions, asserting use, making the Hegde side visible in dirt the way Ramesh had asked without examining what asking meant. They converged. Neither slowed. Neither looked at the ground for a place to step aside. Raju's bundle shifted. Prasad's jaw set. Bodies blocking bodies before words arrived.

"This is our side."

"All sides are for walking."

Brief. Hot. The language of ownership deployed like a weapon neither man had invented, echoing veranda arguments, echoing failed meetings, echoing everything that had not worked. Ramesh's stick struck earth. He moved faster than his knee preferred. Fifty metres. Forty. Thirty. Twenty. His voice carried before his body could reach them, authority attempting to arrive before contact.

"Leave it!"

Too late.

Prasad's hands flat on Raju's chest. One shove. One motion. Harder than intention, or as hard as the anger required, Ramesh would never know which, and it would not matter to the file or the wedding or the bruise forming on Raju's shoulder. Damage control without apology, instinctive, the movement of a man who knew this had crossed a line and whose first thought was retreat before witness became record. "Anna—" Raju started.

"Quiet. Can you stand?"

Ramesh helped him up. Raju's weight leaned into him, young body suddenly heavy with pain and shock, breath coming hard through the nose, one hand reaching for the hurt shoulder and stopping short. The broken post lay at an angle, base splintered, wood pale where it had cracked, wire sagging where the post had held it.

Prasad was gone. Pulled away by the older man, figures retreating toward Gowda land, dust settling on their departure. The older man did not look back. Did not apologize.

Ramesh stood with his worker on the path. Broken fence post at an angle. Ramesh's hand on Raju's shoulder. Dust settling.

# Chapter 11 — Notebook Arrows

## S01 — Contradictory Statements

The ceiling fan worked hard and won nothing.

Inspector Priya Naik sat at her desk in the small police station with the FIR on top of a pile that had been taller yesterday and would be taller again tomorrow. One main room. Desk against the back wall. Bench opposite for visitors. Filing cabinet to one side, hot metal even in the morning. Compound glare through the open door. A jeep parked in shade, a constable somewhere, voices from the lane, the machinery of a taluk PS that believed itself busy and was, in the way small stations were always busy with the wrong kind of work.

Professional calm. That was the uniform underneath the uniform.

Ramesh filing after Saroja's objection expired, after a night of silence, after the worker's family made its own arithmetic of dignity versus survival.

What the paper could not capture was everything underneath. The old fight, the new incident, the wedding weather that made every communal file feel like a lit match near dry grass. The wire moved. The meeting failed. The shove. The post.

Something tightened in her chest. Private. Not for the file.

Three years ago, neighbouring taluk. A boundary complaint closed too fast because the queue was long and the families had smiled and promised and the survey marks looked sufficient on paper. Dhoti and shirt, formal in the way village men became formal for stations. Stiff posture, eyes forward, chappals cleaned, the body language of a man who needed a record but did not want trouble. He sat on the bench when she indicated. Accepted tea when the constable brought it and held the cup without drinking, as if warmth might soften something that needed to stay hard.

Always. The word that meant *no document*.

## S02 — Old Family Connection

# Chapter 17 — Cutting Leaf

His fence. His certainty. His *our path* spoken over and over at every gathering, every boundary dispute, every moment when sharing would have cost him face and narrowing gave him a story. # Chapter 18 — Unsent Email

## S01 — The Draft

Saturday evening in Bengaluru had its own weather. Warm air through the window, traffic thinning to a hum, apartment lights multiplying in the buildings across the lane like a second city built from electricity and the loneliness of people who had not planned to spend another weekend alone.

He read it.

Perfect.

The *To:* field was empty.

His finger hovered again. Send. Delete. Send. The trackpad smooth under his thumb. Sending meant Vikram's warmth deployed one more time, the postponed clarity meeting rescheduled into forever, the equity conversation reopened only to be closed again with a smile. She was in the village. Two days ago her text had appeared on his screen: *Things wild here. Tell you later.* He had not pushed. She had not explained. Tonight they were both in storms the other could not enter.

## S02 — Dark Flat

He closed the laptop.

# Chapter 20 — Jasmine in Steel Bowl

# Chapter 21 — Loudspeaker Distortion

The jatre ground was already full by ten.

Pre-monsoon heat sat on Meera's neck. Dust rose from a thousand chappals and settled again on bangle stalls and plastic toys and the cheap cotton flags strung between poles. Loudspeakers on either side of the temple entrance blasted devotional music through amplifiers that cost less than the garlands draped over them. Lakshmi had sent her with a list. Jaggery first, the dark block for the pooja shelf, wrapped in cloth so it would not sweat through the bag. At the jaggery stall she negotiated without embarrassment. The vendor knew city nieces paid city prices unless you showed you lived here now.

"Forty-five," he said.

"Forty. Last time was forty."

"Last time was before the sugar went up."

Children shrieked at a toy-gun booth. An elderly couple moved slowly through the queue toward the temple entrance. Meera had skipped darshan and would skip it again. Bells from inside the temple competed with the loudspeakers and lost. The loudspeakers pulsed. *Harivarasanam* or something like it, distorted into a new shape by cheap speakers and excessive devotion.

"Meera! Over here."

"Pleased to meet you."

Why did I lie?

She did not know.

# Chapter 22 — Empty Guest Mat

He's building to something.

"I know what you meant. Thank you for the tea."

Also precise. The politeness worse than shouting would have been.

The courtyard seemed to hold its humidity differently after those words.

# Chapter 23 — WhatsApp Forward

## S02 — Almost

# Chapter 24 — Dust on the Bag

## S01 — Tamarind and Cart

# Chapter 25 — Bad Knee

# Chapter 26 — Overlapping Footprints

Not empty.

On the shared path.

In the mud.

Footprints.

A brief look between them.

Hegde and Gowda and bride and cousin and worker, all mixed, all indistinguishable in the same mud. Just: walked. Shared.

# Chapter 27 — Crooked Stamp

## S01 — Compromised Between Parties

Tuesday morning the police station compound was bright.

Puddles drying in the sun. The weather had been shifting since Sunday's first shower, rain and clearing alternating, the taluk town holding its breath before monsoon settled in properly. Priya sat at her desk under the slow ceiling fan, its rhythm familiar from every hour she had spent in this room since the path file landed on her desk. The file open in front of her. Stale. Ten days since the statements. Ten days of waiting for escalation that never came.

The station smelled of old paper and wood and the faint ink of forms that had been filled and filed and forgotten. Through the window: compound wall, a constable moving a bike, bright light on drying puddles, the daily commerce of a small police station performing its fiction that the world arrived in legible pieces.

The file wanted drama her notebook had predicted. The village gave her none.

She flipped through the pages.

Hegde version: path exclusive, wire reasonable, shove provoked. Gowda version: shared access, wire provocation, shove defensive. Contradictory on every point that mattered. Who shoved first, whether the path was shared or exclusive, whether the wire was provocation or response. Names and dates and signatures that proved people had been angry enough to come to a police station but not angry enough to agree on what had happened when they got there.

Her notebook with arrows still in the desk drawer. She opened the drawer to reach for the stamp and saw its edge, buff cover, the social-history instinct that had pointed toward connections she couldn't use officially. Arrows drawn in margins. Names linked. Questions that led somewhere the FIR format couldn't follow. She didn't open it. Didn't need to. The arrows were filed with the case whether she looked at them or not.

No new incident since the warning held. The wedding had happened. The shove had aged. Prasad's shoulder healed or didn't, off her desk either way. The village had gone quiet in a way her forms couldn't capture. Temperature dropped without her doing anything except waiting and watching and noting contradictions she couldn't resolve into charges.

Last time, the festival file—

Brief memory, one beat. Three years ago. Two families, a festival boundary, her premature closed notation before tempers cooled. One side escalated to district. Warning on her record. Transfer application stalled in the queue where applications went to wait and die. She couldn't afford closed too early again. Couldn't afford left open too long either. She checked the wording twice in her head before putting pen to paper.

Yesterday's messages had arrived through the channels: word via a Saroja-adjacent relative on the Hegde side, a Gowda messenger on the other. Neither side wanted the police matter to continue. Not formal. Not signed. Not affidavits. Just: word through relatives. The informal communication sufficient for her to recommend closure. Sufficient and thin.

She drafted carefully.

*Compromised between parties. Both sides communicated via relatives declining further action. No further action recommended.*

Each word weighed. Accurate. Thin. Didn't capture what happened. What happened wasn't her job to capture. Only that it stopped. The statements still contradicted each other on the record. No resolution in legal terms. Parties declined pursuit. That was what she could document without fabricating agreement that didn't exist on paper.

She read it once. Changed nothing. The language was correct. It was also empty, the way official language was always empty when the thing it described had happened somewhere forms couldn't reach.

The wedding had happened in the middle of this file. The shove had happened before the wedding. The village had gone quiet after both, in a sequence her forms listed as dates and incident numbers without capturing the temperature change that made yesterday's messages possible. She didn't need to capture it. She needed to not close too early. Not leave open too long. Get the wording right. Stamp the folder. Shelve it.

A constable brought tea. "Tea, ma'am?"

She nodded. He set the cup on the desk edge, waited without comment, then returned to the compound. The fan turned. The tea steamed. The file waited for its mark.

She reached for the CLOSED stamp.

The ink pad was dry. Hadn't been used recently, the station's bureaucratic decay visible in the dusty surface, the red faint even before she pressed. She pressed the stamp to the pad twice, harder the second time, getting what she could. Then to the file cover. Firm. Routine.

It landed crooked.

Partial impression. The C faded at one edge. The D clear. The red mark drying at an angle that would annoy anyone who cared about straight lines. The pad was dry, the angle was what dry pads produced, the partial impression was what this station's tools gave you when you asked them to close a file.

Priya looked at it.

Re-stamp?

She looked at the ink pad again. Dry. Would need fresh ink from the store room. Would need walking. Would need caring about straight lines on a buff folder that would join a shelf of other buff folders that nobody would open unless someone new got transferred in and decided to organize what previous officers had organized badly.

No. It's legible.

Flat. Practical. Move on.

She closed the file cover over the crooked red mark. The constable returned. She handed him the folder without ceremony. He carried it to the shelf against the far wall. The path file joined others, civilian disputes that came and went, documented and shelved, the row of buff folders like a cemetery of arguments the state had witnessed without understanding. He placed it between a property complaint and a domestic matter from last month. Done.

Her desk cleared.

Pen capped. The notebook's edge visible when she pushed the drawer shut, arrows inside, unnamed, filed with everything else she couldn't officially use. Shoulders lower. The desk breathed.

She looked at the empty space where the file had sat for ten days.

Ten days ending in a crooked stamp and a shelf and a desk that could hold the next file, the next contradiction, the next village that would solve itself before she finished the paperwork.

Compound outside: constables moving, a bike starting somewhere, puddles shrinking in bright clearing light, a vendor's voice distant beyond the compound wall. The village was quiet. Not because of her stamp. Her file caught up to its quiet, late. Social peace had already happened somewhere she hadn't seen, on ground her forms didn't map. The state recorded. It did not create.

# Chapter 28 — Two Pages Signed

## S01 — Both Pages

Naveen arrived twenty minutes early and chose the table by the window because he needed to see the rain and not the door.

Grey Bengaluru sky pressed against the glass. Pre-monsoon showers streaking down in thin lines, the street outside washed to a colour between asphalt and regret. Koramangala on a wet Friday: autos splashing through puddles, someone under a broken umbrella, grey of a city that had been promising monsoon for weeks and was finally beginning to mean it. The café was warm against it. Wooden tables, low music, other people's Fridays continuing without regard for what he was here to receive or refuse. He had slept badly, jaw tight since waking, and ordered before sitting so his hands would have something to do besides rehearse sentences he already knew he would not use.

He had been here before. Not often. Once with Meera years ago when she was visiting and they needed a neutral place to argue about something he no longer remembered. The memory was useful only as proof: this table did not belong to Vikram's company culture deck. No glass cabin. No carpet that made every step sound like apology. No shoulder touch that photographed well and delivered nothing.

Vikram came in at eleven-oh-eight with the same smile Naveen had been parsing for four years.

Warm. Immediate. The smile of a man who remembered your name and meant the remembering when he said it. Hand extended, handshake, not hug, professional distance dressed in affection, and Naveen took it because refusing would have been a performance he was not staging today.

"Naveen. Thanks for making the time."

"Yeah."

They ordered. Vikram asked after the team, genuine interest, or the performance of genuine interest that had always been indistinguishable from the real thing, which was worse. Naveen answered in fragments. The release had shipped. Two people were on leave. Normal Friday noise. Vikram nodded as if he were hearing weather reports from a country he also lived in.

They sat across from each other. Not side by side. Not intimate. The table between them wide enough for paper.

Vikram did not rush.

Naveen appreciated that and resented it in equal measure.

"I know this has been hanging over us both, Naveen."

Not an apology. An acknowledgment that admitted a gap without offering to close it. Vikram's voice was measured. Warm. He believed he was being fair. Naveen had watched that belief operate for four years like a climate: constant, comfortable, unrelated to the math underneath.

Then Vikram reached into his bag and slid something across the table.

Two pages.

White. Clean. Legal format, the font of documents that had been to a lawyer and come back with margins and clause numbers and the quiet authority of print. Dated. Signable. Naveen's name already on the signature line, typed where handwriting would eventually go.

He read.

Accelerated vesting schedule. Reduced share count.

Not the 2021 terms. Not the numbers Vikram had spoken in the car after the board deck shipped. *We'll take care of you, Naveen, you know that, early team*, words that had lived in parking lots and corridor touches and nowhere on paper. This was smaller. Visibly smaller. The gap sat in the column where the share count should have been larger, where four years of loyalty should have translated into something that did not require squinting and swallowing.

Fewer. Faster.

He read the clauses without letting himself become a tutorial. He did not need to explain vesting to anyone. He had built the cap table model three times in spreadsheets Vikram never opened. What he needed was simpler and uglier: comparison. The oral promise floated in memory, not exact numbers, never exact, that was part of the cruelty, but near enough. Larger. This document was the same shape with less inside it, like a house plan with two rooms removed and the architect calling it an efficiency upgrade.

He turned the second page. Same structure. Same reduction dressed in legalese that made the insult look like procedure. Accelerated, vest now, not three years from now, so the document could claim generosity while delivering less than the promise that had kept him in a salary forty percent below market and a flat that knew only one person's weight. His name on the line. Dated today. Signable. Real.

Real meant he could not pretend this was fiction. Real meant rent would still get paid and the years would still not come back.

Four years.

Vikram waited without checking his phone. Not fidgeting. Not triumphant. A man who had brought what he could and knew it was not everything and still believed it was something.

"This is what I can do, Naveen. I wanted you to have something real."

Spoken with sincerity. Naveen heard it cleanly. No villainy, no cartoon. Vikram running a company, a promise made in warmth that he could not fully keep, an offer now that existed because Naveen had stayed civil long enough to receive it instead of burning the bridge on a Saturday night with a letter in his drafts folder.

The letter stays in drafts.

He knew it was there. Unsent. The road not taken. *I need to leave before the person I blame becomes me*, perfect words that had never acquired a *To:* field. He had chosen negotiation over rage, the unsent letter to the emails and meetings and this café with rain on the glass. This was where that path led. Not triumph. Settlement.

He could refuse. Get zero. Send the letter after all. Burn everything and call it integrity.

He could sign. Get partial. Live with the scar.

He looked at Vikram. Vikram looked back, warm, waiting, not pretending the document was full measure.

Naveen uncapped his pen.

The scratch was audible.

First page. Signature line. His name in ink, curves he had written ten thousand times on expense forms and NDAs and the paperwork of employment, now on something that closed a wound imperfectly. He did not rush. Did not hesitate. Adult handwriting. Deliberate. The pen moving across the line with the steadiness of a man who had already decided and was only now letting his hand catch up.

He did not thank Vikram. Did not accuse. Did not say *I trusted you* because that sentence belonged to a younger version of this meeting, the version where he sent the draft and walked out into a different weather.

Second page. Same pen. Same steadiness. Both pages signed, original and counterpart, or two copies of one truth, he did not care which; the lawyer had formatted it and the formatting was not the point. The point was: paper now. Partial paper. His.

Done.

He slid the pages back across the table. Vikram took them. Folded them without flourish. The document's thinness visible, four years reduced to two sheets you could hold in one hand.

Vikram exhaled, relief that was not triumph, the breath of a man who had been holding something without knowing whether it would be accepted or returned across the table like an insult.

"We're good?"

Naveen capped the pen. Looked at Vikram directly for the first time since the signing. Eye contact doing the work words would have cheapened.

"We're done."

Not *we're good*. Done. Functional. Scarred. Not friends. Not enemies. The relationship's new form, two pages and a boundary spoken in two syllables.

Vikram nodded. Stood. Brief, maybe another meeting, maybe respect for the space Naveen would need after, and did not offer a handshake on the way out. Just a nod. Gone.

Naveen put the copy into his folder. Closed it. The click sounded loud in the café murmur, or maybe he was only hearing his own ears.

Rain on the windows. Vikram's chair empty across from him. Vikram's coffee cup still on the table, half-finished, abandoned with the ease of a man who had other places to be. Naveen's coffee cooling. The grey Bengaluru day continuing outside without regard for what had just happened inside. A couple at the next table laughed at something on a phone. Life continuing.

Four years. Two pages. Less than was promised. More than nothing.

Pause.

"Shankarappa-avare."

Another pause. The names sitting in the air between them. Not analytical, not *what does he do* or *how does he know*, just the simplest form of the question she had been circling since April.

"Who is he?"

Simple. Direct. Granddaughter asking grandmother. Not investigator. Not journalist. Just: who.

Silence.

Lakshmi did not answer immediately. Rain on tin. Their breathing audible in the quiet, two people in a small room at night, the intimacy coming from smallness and proximity and the fact that Meera was below and Lakshmi was above and that geometry had always meant *I will listen if you will tell*.

Meera waited.

She had learned waiting. Weeks of it, the cost of not grabbing. She did not push. Did not rephrase. Held the question in the air and let it sit.

Lakshmi's hand came to her head.

The gesture from childhood. Palm warm, fingers briefly in her hair, the weight of it familiar from a cheek touched on arrival night, from *sleep now, kanamma*, from every version of love Lakshmi knew how to give without making it heavy.

Lakshmi spoke.

Clear. Quiet. Not whispered. Said, the way you said something you had decided to say and would not unsay.

"He keeps things from becoming police."

There was more.

She heard it before she saw it. The sentence continuing in the air, syntax unfinished, the next word forming—

Lakshmi's mouth opened on the beginning of a phrase. *By—* or something like it, a syllable starting, breath carrying intent toward elaboration, the lips shaping around a consonant that would have opened a door Meera could not unsee—

And closed.

Deliberately. Lips coming together. The next word held inside. Not forgetting. Not losing the thread. Choosing. The jaw set soft but final. The breath held where speech would have continued. Meera watched the muscle at Lakshmi's temple, one small flex, control made visible, and then stillness. The unfinished phrase hanging in the air like smoke from an extinguished match: you could see where fire had been; you could not recover the flame.

The air held unfinished syntax between them.

Meera saw the stop.

The mouth closing. The deliberate silence where a word should have been. She could push. *By what, Ajji, how, who else*, and she knew, reading Lakshmi's face in the dim bulb-light, that pushing would buy her nothing. Lakshmi's eyes did not harden. Did not look away. Held Meera's gaze with the steady opacity of a woman who had decided years ago how much truth a granddaughter could carry and would not recalculate tonight.

She did not push.

She leaned into Lakshmi's hand instead.

Forward slightly. Scalp against palm, the weight of the touch increasing. The sentence received. The suspended syllable received.

Lakshmi's fingers stayed in her hair. Then stillness.

They sat in the unfinished sentence.

Rain outside, onset showers now steady, filling what the next word should have occupied, covering sound, making the room feel sealed and private. Dim bulb. Blanket smell faint, naphthalene and old cotton, the same closed sweetness she had breathed in as a child under stored blankets in the small room down the corridor. Meera on the floor. Lakshmi above. The sentence spoken and the one word suspended and the rest withheld, all of it occupying the room like furniture you could not move without touching.

Somewhere in the house, rubber chappals on stone. Not now. A sound from another summer, arriving without a face attached.

Meera breathed into it. Let it sit in her ribs next to the sentence. *He keeps things from becoming police.* Incomplete. Accurate. Not enough. What she had.

Her eyes stayed open. She did not reach for childhood. Did not name the man with the aligned chappals. Tonight was not for assembling proof.

Lakshmi's hand remained on her head, or the warmth remained on her scalp even when the hand eventually lifted; she could not tell which, only that the touch outlasted the silence. Minutes passed. The rain did not stop. Somewhere in the house Saroja turned in her sleep; Ramesh cleared his throat on the porch once and went quiet.

The bulb flickered once and did not die.

Meera thought of tomorrow's bus. Of Bengaluru. Of Naveen's unread message. Of the path after rain and the empty guest room.

Neither spoke again.

Outside, the monsoon committed a little more of itself to the night.

Inside, the unfinished sentence lived between them. The rain sat with her. The hand's warmth faded slowly.

# Chapter 30 — Phone Glow

## S01 — Come Sooner

Meera woke before the house did.

Short sleep. Last night's loneliness still in her body, warmed slightly by morning, the sentence sitting behind her eyes like something she had read and could not unread. *He keeps things from becoming police.* One suspended syllable. A hand on her head. She had lain on her own bed afterward for an hour without sleeping, the room next to Saroja's snoring, the rain finishing its argument with the roof, the house holding her one last night.

Bag packed by the door since midnight. Clothes, charger, Saroja's medicines she had borrowed and would return next visit. Bus at seven. Saroja had said seven, then six-thirty, then back to seven with the authority of a woman who treated departure schedules the way she treated wedding seating charts: negotiable until they weren't.

Rain had begun. Not the hesitant spatters of yesterday but the monsoon's first real commitment, specks on the veranda, green smell leaking through windows, the village turning the colour of things that had waited too long for water. Morning damp on her skin when she stepped into the corridor. The house stirring in layers. Kitchen first, then voices, then the acceleration of a household sending someone back to the city.

She washed. Dressed for travel, practical kurta, the shoes that could survive bus steps and platform puddles. Carried the bag into the corridor while dawn light filtered grey through rain.

The guest room door stood open.

She passed without stopping. Glanced in. Habit, not pilgrimage.

Folded.

Blankets on the bed, neat, waiting. The room empty since Saturday morning, mat rolled, no naphthalene ghost strong enough to announce absence, just the faint residue of stored cotton and a room between occupants. He had stored them before leaving; now they waited for whoever came next. The small sleep-room where she had arrived in April with shame and city sandals and the feeling of not belonging. Different bag now. Different body.

She did not linger.

The corridor was cool. Lakshmi's door closed. Saroja already moving in the kitchen, vessels, tiffin box, the democracy of departure breakfast that Meera could not eat much of.

Near the front door, the spot where footwear accumulated, the democratic scatter of a household's comings and goings, something was missing.

Bare.

Clean. No chappals aligned with geometric precision. No rubber soles pointing the same direction while everyone else's sandals lay in chaos. Saturday morning. He had left quietly, afternoon rest, house blind, the lane empty, and the spot had stayed empty since. Not punishment. Not exile. Not death. Work-complete. A space where a precise man's precise footwear used to be. She had noticed the alignment on wedding eve. The spot stayed bare.

Saroja met her at the kitchen threshold. Water bottle filled, tiffin box wrapped in cloth, the logistics of love executed before affection found its vocabulary.

"Did you eat?"

"A little."

"Take the tiffin. Don't say no. Bus food is poison. Last time you came you said the same thing and then you ate nothing until Maddur—"

"Yes, Saroja-aunty."

Phone charged, checked twice, Saroja's finger on the battery icon like a diagnostic. Bus time confirmed. Auto number written on a scrap of paper in case the stand was crowded. Umbrella offered and refused. The bag was enough, the rain was not heavy yet, she would get wet at the stand anyway. The practical caring that had been Saroja's mode throughout. Not eloquent, not deep in the way Lakshmi was deep, but consistent and warm and impossible to leave behind without feeling the loss of being looked after.

Lakshmi appeared from the corridor.

Brief. No repetition of last night. Words had been given in the dark; this was body only. She would not elaborate. Would not finish the sentence. Would not say his name.

Her hand came to Meera's cheek.

Same palm. Same warmth. Same dry brief pressure as arrival night. *Sleep now, kanamma*, reversed now: not *you're here* but *you're leaving and you belong anyway*. The loop closing without announcement. Meera received it. Felt the cheek warm where the palm had been after the palm was gone. Wanted to say something and said nothing because Lakshmi's gift had always been the kind that withstood absence better than reply.

Lakshmi's eyes held hers one beat, not searching, not instructing, then released. The back retreated toward the corridor. Grace of non-demand.

Ramesh appeared at the veranda edge.

Brief. Awkward. He was not a words-man, not comfortable in emotional doorways, but present. He had not avoided her departure. He had come. Meera noticed, surprised without showing it, the uncle who had performed patriarch-calm through shame and courtyard humiliation and forty seconds of shared silence on a wet porch while a guest sat distant on the veranda and neither of them named what they had survived.

He looked at her. She looked at him. Rain specked the veranda between them.

"Come sooner next time."

One line. Not a speech. Not an apology. Not an explanation of paths or police files or the weeks that had broken and partially mended something in the house. Just: come back sooner. You belong here. Your presence mattered.

Meera nodded. "I will."

He nodded back, brief, honest, and went back inside. Done. Thirty seconds. The brevity was the honesty.

She stood on the veranda a moment longer than necessary. Standing where the guest had sat with his tea and his bad knee and his admitted doubt, where the courtyard had held speeches and silences and the life of a house that had become, improbably, hers to leave.

The tulsi pot dripped. Rain collected in the courtyard's low corner, first puddle of the season sitting like a small promise. She had walked this veranda dozens of times these weeks. Serving, watching, failing to mediate, learning the cost of presence. The geometry of the house was in her body now. Gate. Lane. Neem at the corner she could not see from here but knew was there.

She picked up her bag.

The house behind her. Courtyard, kitchen, corridor, guest room with its folded blankets waiting. Everything she had arrived not-belonging-to now holding her differently.

Rain specks on her arms at the veranda edge. Bag strap cutting familiar weight. She walked down the lane toward the bus stand, not running, not dragging. A neighbour nodded. Dog under a ledge, dry enough. The lane mud soft where rain had found it. Her sandals, city sandals, cleaner than April, still wrong for this ground, made small sounds she would not hear once the bus engine started.

The house receded. One look, taken, then forward. Gate. Lane. Monsoon air. City-bound and village-held at once.

## S02 — Phone Glow

The bus stand was wet.

Monsoon committed now. Rain steady on the tin awning, puddles forming in the dust that had been dust for months, passengers huddled with bags and umbrellas that would fail in the way all umbrellas failed when the season decided to mean it. Machinery of departure: conductor shouting destinations, drivers checking mirrors, auto drivers offering last-mile rides to people who would refuse them, the diesel-and-upholstery smell of travel beginning. Wet plastic seats inside buses not yet full. Someone eating vada from newspaper, rain-spotted, unbothered.

Meera stood with her bag and Saroja's tiffin and the sentence in her body. Overlapping footprints in memory, a cheek-touch, a nod, a frame incomplete and accurate. Leaving.

Farooq visible from his store doorway, geography of a town small enough that leaving and buying rice shared the same rain. Radio low.

He saw her. Grinned, the grin of someone who had watched her buy dal and argue soap prices and witness something at his counter without being asked to explain it.

"Come back, city girl!"

Shouted across twenty metres. Warm, not oracle, not profound. The man who had sold her rice and called her *city girl* until the name stopped being tease and became recognition: she was both. City and here. Both real.

Her hand went up. Wave returned. Not oracle. Just Farooq. She did not shout back, throat tight, or dignity, or both, but the hand was enough. He understood. He went back to his counter before the bus arrived, which was also enough.

The bus pulled in. Bengaluru, semi-sleeper, the route she had taken down in April when arrival felt like exile and the bag had felt like evidence of a life she was failing to maintain in two places at once. Same route. Different register.

She boarded. Conductor, "Bengaluru?" "Yes." Ticket exchanged. Coins counted. Bag stowed in the hold with a thump that sounded final. Window seat. She had learned to take window seats for village exits because the glass gave you something to look at besides faces, and because rain on glass would matter later without her knowing yet how much.

Interior warming. Bodies settling. Wet clothes smell mixing with diesel. Engine cough. Pause. Then movement.

The bus pulled away.

The village through wet glass: receding. Farooq's store. The stand. The road. The lane to the house invisible from here but present in memory. Guest room, bare threshold, Lakshmi's palm, Ramesh's one line, Saroja's tiffin against her leg. The bus moving regardless of her feelings.

Fields blurred, green deepening with rain, the landscape becoming smear. Taluk town's edge. Then less town. Then distance opening like a mouth.

Phone pinged.

WhatsApp. Ananya.

She opened it.

A photo. Path after rain. Fresh wet ground, mud dark, field edges green, sky grey in the corner the way phone cameras always caught sky. And footprints. New ones. Hegde-side prints. Gowda-side prints. Overlapping. The path ordinary. Shared. Just feet on mud after rain.

She looked at the image in the moving bus.

Phone glow. Screen lit in the slightly dim interior, the path bright on glass, mud and green and prints illuminated from below. Her face lit by the screen. Rain on the window beside her, grey light and streaks, the real village receding behind wet glass while the digital village held steady in her hands.

She typed to Naveen.

*On bus. Tell me later.*

Sent.

She did not look away from the photo immediately. Let the glow sit on her face. Let the bus carry her while the screen held the path steady.

Phone glow illuminating the mud path photo.

Rain on the bus window — committed now, drumming on the roof, streaking the glass beside her face so the screen's light and the grey outside met in her reflection and did not resolve.

Village receding — rain-distorted, already memory, impossible to hold perfectly clear.

Her face lit from below by the screen: the path, the footprints, the green, the ordinary.

The bus moved.

The screen glowed.

The ground held.

She carried it.
