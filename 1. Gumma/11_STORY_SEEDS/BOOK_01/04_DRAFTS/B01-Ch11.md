# Chapter 11 — Notebook Arrows

## S01 — Contradictory Statements

The ceiling fan worked hard and won nothing.

Inspector Priya Naik sat at her desk in the small police station with the FIR on top of a pile that had been taller yesterday and would be taller again tomorrow. One main room. Desk against the back wall. Bench opposite for visitors. Filing cabinet to one side, hot metal even in the morning. Compound glare through the open door — a jeep parked in shade, a constable somewhere, voices from the lane, the ordinary machinery of a taluk PS that believed itself busy and was, in the way small stations were always busy with the wrong kind of work.

Hurt and mischief. Two named parties. A wedding in nine days that both families had mentioned as if the date itself were an argument for leniency — *please, madam, the function*, as if functions were evidence of innocence or guilt.

Thursday morning. Heat already in the room despite the fan's effort. Sweat on forms. Paper softening at the corners where fingers had rested. Dettol smell from yesterday's mopping. Dust on the filing cabinet. The fan's blade blur doing mathematics it could not solve.

Professional calm. That was the uniform underneath the uniform.

She read the FIR again. Ramesh Hegde: complainant. Prasad Gowda: accused. Farmworker injured — bruised shoulder, scraped face, visible, painful, not hospital-worthy but real. Fence post broken. Path between fields. Property damage. Assault. The facts sat on paper with the neatness of things that had happened and could not be unhappened. Late yesterday, the complaint had arrived — Ramesh filing after Saroja's objection expired, after a night of silence, after the worker's family made its own arithmetic of dignity versus survival.

What the paper could not capture was everything underneath — the old fight, the new incident, the wedding weather that made every communal file feel like a lit match near dry grass. The wire moved. The meeting failed. The shove. The post. Each fact a surface symptom of something the FIR form had no column for.

Something tightened in her chest. Private. Not for the file.

Three years ago, neighbouring taluk. A boundary complaint closed too fast because the queue was long and the families had smiled and promised and the survey marks looked sufficient on paper. Six months later: stones. Transfer. Rumour in the PSI's office that she *doesn't handle sensitive land well*. She had not told anyone in this town. She wanted this file documented cleanly. She wanted arrows that met. She wanted paper to do what paper was supposed to do.

Old fight, new incident.

The shorthand arrived without invitation. Her chest tightened again — not sympathy, recognition.

Ramesh arrived at nine.

Dhoti and shirt, formal in the way village men became formal for stations — stiff posture, eyes forward, chappals cleaned, the body language of a man who needed a record but did not want trouble. He sat on the bench when she indicated. Accepted tea when the constable brought it — standard PS hospitality, functional not dramatic — and held the cup without drinking, as if warmth might soften something that needed to stay hard.

Statement-taking began.

His version: unprovoked shove. His worker innocent, on the path as he had always been, doing ordinary work — raking, carrying, the labour of a field that did not pause for family disputes. Gowda-side youth aggressive, hotheaded, pushing without cause. Injury visible. Post broken. Property damage. He described the morning — dawn walk, worker ahead, confrontation at the narrow section, arrival too late. He spoke carefully. Edited. Not lying — selecting. The way people selected when dignity was at stake and the bench opposite a desk was a performance space.

"When was the wire moved?" Priya asked.

"It was always more to our side."

Always. The word that meant *no document*.

She wrote. Pen on paper. The sound of official recording — scratch, pause, scratch. Asked about witnesses — the older Gowda man had been present but would be interviewed separately with Prasad; no neutral third party on the path at contact. Asked about the worker's condition — would he need hospital? No. Would he work tomorrow? Probably not. About whether this had happened before — words, postures, the path becoming a stage for families who could not stop performing ownership.

Ramesh mentioned generations. The path. The use. The families. The way things had been before papers were required for truth. Something in Priya's chest tightened again — recognition without name, the echo of a file that had re-erupted because *generations* was not evidence and never had been.

She noted it. Did not comment.

Ramesh left. Stiff. Satisfied with documentation, unsatisfied with everything else. The door closed behind him with the soft finality of a man who had said what he came to say and would say it again if required.

Twenty minutes. Another case's papers shuffled — a theft report, mundane, almost relief in its simplicity. Tea gone cold in her cup. The fan continuing its losing argument with April. Priya read the theft summary without absorbing it, mind still on *always* and *common*, on two versions of the same morning that could not both be true and could not both be false.

Prasad arrived with an elder relative — Venki's side, measured, managing, the body language of damage control dressed as respect. The boy looked ashamed and scared, jeans and shirt too new for a station bench, hands that would not stay still, eyes that found the floor and stayed there.

Different energy in the room. The same procedure.

Priya reset her face to professional. Same questions. Same pen. Same bench.

Prasad's version: verbal provocation first. Hegde worker blocking the path, aggressive words, *our side*, the posture of men looking for fight. Stumble, not shove. The post was already loose — anyone could see, the wood was old, the base was weak. Accident. Exaggeration. He spoke with the coached carefulness of youth who had been told what to say and was trying to believe it while his hands betrayed him. His eyes found the floor. The elder relative's hand rested on his knee — not comforting, anchoring.

The elder relative added, when Priya looked up from her notes: "This has been settled for years. The path is common."

Common. Settled. Words that meant *no paper*.

Priya pressed. Survey marks? Written agreement? Revenue records covering this specific path usage? A board? A decree? Anything that could translate *generations* into a number a file could hold?

Prasad shook his head. The elder relative spread his hands — a gesture of openness that was also a gesture of emptiness. No document. No board. No survey stone within shouting distance. The path existed in use, in memory, in the feet of both families' workers and elders and children walking to fields for longer than this station had stood.

Both sides — in separate statements, separate bodies, separate edits — had looked at her with the expression of people who had been living inside an arrangement so long they had forgotten it was invisible to outsiders. Shared use, informal, made by feet over decades, maintained by consensus and argument and the occasional shove that became an FIR.

Neither side was fully lying. Both were editing. She could feel the edit in their phrasing — the pauses before *always*, the careful noun choices, the way *common* and *settled* arrived like synonyms for *trust me, madam, we know what we know*.

She dismissed them with the standard warning: no further incidents. The words felt thin in her mouth — the same warning she had given a hundred times, the same warning that would appear in the file as *parties advised*, the same warning that did not reach whatever lived underneath *generations* and *common*. Both families mentioned the wedding again on their way out — as context, as plea, as the clock that made everything urgent and nothing simple. Nine days. As if nine days were a legal category. The elder relative nodded too much. Prasad did not look at her on the way to the door.

Alone at her desk.

The room hotter now. Compound glare sharper. Fan still losing.

She opened her notebook — not the FIR, her notebook, the one where problems became diagrams before they became files, the one that would return in a closure she could not yet see — and drew arrows.

Hegde claim. Arrow.
Gowda claim. Arrow.
Path. Arrow.
Wire. Arrow.
Wedding date. Arrow.
Broken post. Arrow.
Injury. Arrow.

Each line pointing toward a centre that had no label. She tried to connect them. The lines refused to meet. The geometry of a problem that existed in bodies and pride and feet on red earth, not in the cabinet behind her. Wedding date sat in the margin — nine days — a deadline that made every arrow feel heavier without making any arrow clearer.

There's something underneath this that neither side will say.

She could not name it. She was trained to need paper. She had none.

The pen hovered over the empty centre. A sweat drop fell from her temple onto the page and bloomed slightly before she blotted it with her thumb — yellowish paper, blue ink, the small ruin of heat on evidence. Outside, the compound glare continued. A vehicle passed. The fan continued losing.

Notebook with arrows pointing toward a centre that had no label. Priya's pen hovering. Sweat on the page.

## S02 — No Survey Stone

The jeep took them to the path at half past twelve.

Priya sat in the front seat with her notebook on her knee, the constable driving with the familiarity of a man who knew every lane in the taluk and which lanes required slowing for chickens. PSI wanted a report by evening. The file needed ground truth. Ground truth, she was learning again, was not the same as truth — it was what you could write down after walking on red earth in uniform while people watched from windows.

They parked where the lane met the fields. Priya stepped out into full sun — no shade on the path, no mercy, April establishing its daily argument with skin. Hot wind on exposed ground. Dry earth smell. Pre-monsoon pressure in the air, the sense of rain considering arrival and choosing delay.

The path was empty.

Both sides avoiding during police visibility — self-preserving, the wisdom of families who knew that being seen near a dispute during inspection could become interpretation. Priya could feel watching without always seeing watchers. Distant windows. Field edges. The social surveillance of a village performing innocence for the state.

She walked with the constable behind her.

The wire fence first — shifted inward, visible even to her untrained eye. The compression of walking space. New post among old ones, pale wood among dark, the geography of a boundary moved while an owner was in Mandya buying hardware he did not imagine would be used against him. She noted it. Measured the narrow section with her steps — three paces where four had been, or something like that, imprecise because her training was in statements not surveying.

The broken post.

Angled. Base splintered. Still in situ — evidence undisturbed, wood pale at the crack, wire sagging where support had been. She crouched — uniform creasing, knee complaining — and looked at the base. Fresh splintering. Not old rot pretending to be accident. Violence made wood.

She stood. Looked in both directions along the path's length. Red earth packed by feet. Fields on both sides — cane, rows, the ordinary agriculture of a dispute that was not about agriculture.

Survey markers?

She walked fifty metres one way. Nothing. Fifty metres the other. Nothing. No revenue stone. No board with names. No decree carved anywhere. No official geography to ground the file in.

The path existed because feet had made it exist — consensus-wide, not decreed. Made by use. Maintained by argument. Contested by wire.

She walked two hundred metres along its length anyway, because procedure required walking, because files needed the sentence *visited scene*, because her job was to document even when documentation was a photograph of absence. Her boots left prints beside older prints — workers who had stopped using the path since the shove, families who had redirected feet, the path still there, still claimed by use even in disuse.

Fields on both sides. Cane rows. A channel opening clogged with silt. Hot wind in her face. Dust on her boots. The constable followed, patient, local, carrying the knowledge of a place she could not put in the FIR.

"Madam," he said. "Both families have used this since my father's time."

One sentence. Human. Not analysis. The truth of a place where everyone knew and knowledge was not evidence.

Priya wrote in her book: *Shared use, no exclusive documentation, dispute likely pre-existing.*

She added the date. Added her initials. Added the constable's name as witness to the visit. The file would grow. The file would not resolve. She would monitor. She would warn. She could not make peace — only record its absence.

She looked at the path stretching both directions. Made by feet. Contested by pride. Injured by a shove that was now a number in her station and a bruise on a man's face and a wedding clock ticking in nine days. The cane on both sides continued regardless — crop did not pause for FIRs — and the path continued regardless, because paths were older than files.

Pen clicked closed. Notebook shut. The sound small in the hot wind.

She walked back to the jeep. Distant windows visible now that she knew to look — figures half-seen behind glass, families watching her leave, watching her fail to find what would not exist, performing the performance of being inspected.

Hot wind on the path. Both families' distant windows watching her leave.

Unease settled into her professional posture and stayed there for the drive back.
