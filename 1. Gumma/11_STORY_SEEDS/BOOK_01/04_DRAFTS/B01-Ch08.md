# Chapter 8 — Phone Face-Down

## S01 — BP Is Fine

The clinic ceiling fan worked hard and won nothing.

Dr. Suresh Bhat sat at the steel desk between patients with the frosted glass throwing Monday glare into a soft smear across charts and the BP cuff’s coiled tubing. Waiting room murmur: coughs, chair scrapes, someone’s child asking for water in a voice that would become a whine in five minutes. Dettol. Dust. The sweet hair-oil someone always wore to appointments as if fragrance could bargain with illness. His stethoscope was still warm from the last chest — an uncle with gastritis who had spent nine of ten minutes explaining that *the Hegde people* were being difficult, as if Suresh’s earpieces were designed for gossip. Before that, a woman monitoring her mother’s sugar who had mentioned *Gowda pride* while staring at her own hands. Third mention today of “the families.” Wedding weather. Path weather. He was becoming a switchboard without wires, a confessional that offered no absolution and charged two hundred rupees for a consultation that pretended to be about blood.

Mild weariness sat just behind his eyes. Professional neutrality worn thin at the edges like a cuff repeatedly inflated.

He drank lukewarm water from a steel tumbler, called the next name, and arranged his face into the expression that said *I have time* when he did not. Outside, a scooter horn. Inside, the attendant’s flip-flops. The small geography of a taluk clinic that believed it was ordinary and was, except when it wasn’t.

The Gowda nephew entered with the particular posture of young men who had rehearsed a medical sentence in front of a mirror or a scooter’s rearview. Mid-twenties. Shirt buttoned wrong at the collar, corrected as he sat. Eyes that had not slept the sleep they claimed. A faint smell of soap and nervous sweat. He glanced at the certificates on the wall as if Latin letters could authorize confession.

“BP check, doctor. Just routine. Uncle said I should keep checking.”

“Which uncle?”

“Venki-uncle. Not — just, you know. Routine.”

Suresh nodded him into the chair. The boy’s knee was already bouncing — a metronome of unsaid things. Cuff on, Velcro loud in the small room, the sound of medicine beginning. Pump. The dial’s dance under glass. Suresh watched the needle the way he always did, as if numbers could be persuaded into honesty by attention. 132/84. Mild elevation if you wanted drama. Fine if you wanted truth. Not a crisis. Not even interesting as medicine. He deflated the cuff slowly; the boy flinched as if the release were judgment.

“BP is fine,” Suresh said. The boy’s arm hair stood in the fan’s push. “Eat less salt. Sleep more than you’re sleeping. Come back if headaches or if you feel your heart racing without reason. Otherwise you’re all right.”

The nephew did not stand. His bare feet in chappals stayed planted on the cool floor as if roots had grown through rubber.

Hesitation first — the cover story dying politely, mouth opening and closing on air — then the real appointment began, as Suresh had known it would from the knee and the collar and the way “Venki-uncle” had been offered like a password. Stress, the boy said. Cold between the houses since yesterday. A meeting on a veranda that the whole town somehow already knew in outline if not in the detail of untouched cups — phones had done what phones did. Venki-uncle not sleeping, walking the house at night, snapping at small things: salt too much, a chair left out of place, a cousin’s laugh timed wrong. The women walking carefully. The men pretending carpets mattered. *What if the wedding fails?* The question arrived tear-adjacent, genuine, the fear of a young man who had no safe kitchen in which to say it and had chosen a consultation room because doctors were sworn to something that felt like secrecy even when it was only manners and the Medical Council’s ghost. He did not say *path*. He said *tension*. He said *pride*. He said *what if she suffers because of us*.

Suresh listened.

He did not take notes on family politics. Notes became files; files became stories that traveled. He watched the wall calendar’s date — six April — and the fan’s blur, and the boy’s hands twisting each other as if wringing a cloth that would not dry. A fly wrote nonsense on the frosted glare. Ten-minute mark passed without either of them acknowledging clocks. Suresh’s bladder made a small request he ignored. Fifteen minutes. The waiting room’s murmur thickened: a throat cleared pointedly beyond the door, a name called by the attendant and then called again with the irritation of people who believed their fever outranked someone else’s fear. Absorbed despite himself — the professional sin of being human on a schedule. Boundary stretching the way elastic stretched — silently, until the silence itself became a sound. The nephew’s eyes shone; a tear escaped and was shamed away with a knuckle, then another tear that was not shamed in time. Compassion arrived in Suresh’s chest, then compassion’s cousin, fatigue, then the guilt of both, a braid he knew too well from monsoon fevers and midnight calls. Twenty minutes. He heard himself make small doctor-sounds — *hmm*, *I see*, *go on* — that were not wisdom, only proof of presence, the cheapest medicine and sometimes the only one. Twenty-five. The queue pressed against his spine like a hand. Someone in the waiting room laughed at a phone video, incongruous. He could not fix interpersonal politics with a stethoscope. He also could not say *leave* to a tearful boy whose blood pressure was the least true thing in the room.

He closed as he closed such things — kind, generic, the boundary reasserting in sentences that sounded like care and functioned like exit ramps for both of them.

“These things have happened before,” Suresh said. “They usually settle after events pass. You sleep. You eat. You don’t carry your uncle’s nights on your own back. If your BP stays like this, fine. If your sleep doesn’t improve in a week, come. We’ll talk about that — sleep — not the rest. The rest is not my instrument.”

The nephew nodded, calmer by a degree that was not cure and not nothing. Wiped his face with the heel of his hand. Thanked him as if Suresh had prescribed a future with a stamp on it. Stood. The chair scraped. Exited. Door closed with the soft finality of a room returning to its owner.

Thirty seconds alone. Stethoscope off, coiled on the desk like a sleeping animal. Eyes rubbed with thumb and forefinger until lights bloomed behind the lids — orange, then dark. Exhaustion honest and brief — the particular tiredness of having been used correctly for the wrong problem. He sat with his palms flat on steel and felt the fan’s air move the hair on his forearms. Somewhere a pressure cooker whistle from a neighbouring house marked a lunch that was not his. Then the professional mask returning because the next patient was already a shadow on frosted glass, lunch was a theory still believed in somewhere beyond this lane, and the town’s pain had his clinic’s address whether he wanted the mail or not.

He straightened the cuff until the Velcro aligned. Recapped his pen. Called the next name with a voice that did not shake. Became, again, a man with ten minutes to give and a town that needed twenty-five — and would take them, patiently and without receipt, one tearful BP at a time.

## S02 — The Pause

The last patient before he closed for lunch was a schoolteacher with a rash between the toes that was fungal and gratefully ordinary.

No families. No weddings. No paths. Only skin and moisture and the advice about powder and cotton socks that Suresh could give while half his mind was still in the nephew’s wet eyes. He asked the teacher to remove a shoe; the teacher apologized for the smell; Suresh waved the apology away as he had waved a hundred. Looked. Named. Prescribed. Prescription written in the handwriting pharmacists claimed to hate and always understood — the conspiracy of clinics. The teacher thanked him with the briskness of someone late for a fifth-period class and nearly forgot the shoe, hopping once in the doorway before dignity returned. Door closed. Queue clear — the attendant’s broom already making the waiting room’s plastic chairs into a regiment again, scrape and align, scrape and align. Midday past. Glare through frost softening from white to yellow. A faded calendar goddess watched without interest from above the sink. The fan kept running as if motion alone could empty a room of what listening had put into it.

Suresh alone at the steel desk.

Weight returning the moment distraction ended — nephew’s question about a wedding failing, Hegde-side stress last week in an elder’s carefully casual asides about *sleep and blood pressure, doctor, only that*, the pattern of being both families’ soft place to land without being either family’s solution. He held both sides’ pain the way a clinic held both sides’ files: in the same cabinet, separated by alphabet, not by justice. Babies delivered for one house. Elders monitored for the other. Same hands. Same soap. Switchboard. Accidental confessional. Paid for BP. Unpaid for everything that made BP rise. The fan hummed its one note. His lunch tiffin, if he still had a lunch, was a negotiation happening in another building without him.

He thought of saying something to someone who could act. There was no such person in his phone who was also his business. There was only the day, and food, and the body that needed rice even when the mind was full of other people’s uncles. He washed his hands at the corner sink though he had already washed them — ritual as reset — and dried them on the towel that never fully dried in this heat.

He picked up his phone.

Paused.

The pause held longer than logistics required — gravity before a mundane act, thumb hovering, eyes on the dark glass as if an answer might rise from it uninvited. The day’s confidences arranged themselves behind his eyes with nowhere to send them that was not betrayal or futility. A scooter horn outside. The attendant sweeping. His own breath. Then his thumb woke the screen. He typed. A short message — the kind of sentence that moved a household clock without moving history. Sent. The whoosh of departure. He did not watch for the tick that meant read. Did not need to; the ritual was the point, the small claim that his time belonged partly to a life that was not this desk, not this cuff, not this town’s unfinished argument.

He placed the phone face-down on the steel desk.

Deliberate. Final. Glass against metal — a soft click — glow sealed away under plastic and case. The gesture of a man ending a channel. For a second he looked at the blank back of the phone as if it might speak. It did not. He stood, walked the short corridor past the attendant’s nod, pushed open the back door to the step where the clinic’s shadow met the lane’s dust, and took ten seconds of hotter outdoor air that smelled of frying onions and two-stroke oil and the afternoon already planning its weight. A scooter passed, leaving blue smoke. A dog slept in a patch of shade as if shade were permanent. He breathed until his ribs remembered they were not a filing cabinet for other people's nights. Returned.

Waiting room empty. Chairs aligned. A broom leaning. Fan still circling its useless mercy. Phone still face-down on steel — a dark rectangle that might have been anything, to anyone who wanted it to be anything. The day’s confidences sat in his chest with nowhere to go — not solved, not forwarded, only carried — while the clinic held its Monday quiet and Suresh stood a moment longer with his hand on the desk edge, a civilian full of stories that were not medical and not his to end, before he reached for his bag, switched off nothing yet — the fan could run a minute longer — and aimed himself at the ordinary hope of a meal that would not ask him about weddings, paths, or why a fine BP had taken twenty-five minutes to say so.
