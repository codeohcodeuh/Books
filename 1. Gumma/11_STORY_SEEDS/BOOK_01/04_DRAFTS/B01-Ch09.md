# Chapter 9 — Biscuit Tin

## S01 — Equal Size

Saroja had said *come see the temple area* the way she said everything that was not optional — as if Meera's presence were a logistical requirement, like extra chairs or a clean carpet.

Tuesday morning. April heat already working on the road before ten. Meera walked beside her in a cotton churidar Saroja had laid out without discussion — *decent for temple, not like office*, the subtext hanging in the air between them like the dust their slippers raised. The temple committee veranda opened ahead under a neem tree whose shade felt like a salary earned. Plastic chairs in faded colours — red, blue, green gone sun-bleached — arranged in a configuration that implied hierarchy without admitting it. Register books on a table. A pen being passed between hands with the solemnity of a relay baton. Incense residual in the stone. Hot cement in the sun-patches where the neem's mercy did not reach.

Meera expected tedium. She had packed boredom like a snack for the walk.

The elders were already arguing.

Not shouting — worse. Meeting-register voices, self-important, clause-citing, the particular tone of men who had discovered that governance could be performed in plastic chairs and had never recovered from the pleasure. Three of them at the table, one standing with a notebook, another seated with the posture of a judge who had not been consulted about his appointment but had accepted it anyway. A Gowda-side representative Meera did not know — not Venki, someone lower-stakes, polite in a new shirt, firm in a way that suggested he had rehearsed his sentences on the bus.

Saroja found them chairs at the edge. Observer territory. Meera sat where she was told and let the heat find the parts of her the neem missed.

Ramesh was already there.

Stiff in a dhoti that looked pressed against his will. He sat for perhaps three minutes without speaking, without looking at the Gowda rep, without acknowledging the banner sketch someone had begun on the back of a wedding invitation envelope. His jaw held something Meera could not name from this distance — post-meeting weather, the teacup residue, the kind of tightness uncles wore when they had been somewhere important and had not been made important enough. He stood. Left. Quiet. The plastic chair scraped once, a small sound of refusal, and he was gone down the temple steps before anyone could assign him a task.

Meera watched him go. She did not comment. Wrong venue for uncles and paths.

The meeting settled into logistics first — tent size, catering allocation, the shared hall for both families' reception function. Normal coordination. Saroja interjected where lists required it: *What about the carpet? Who's paying for the extra tent?* Her notebook appeared, pen ready, the weapon of a woman who would enforce tomorrow what was merely discussed today. Meera let the practicalities wash over her. Chairs. Vessels. The ordinary machinery of weddings that made families occupy the same space while barely speaking.

Then the banner.

Whose name went first.

The Gowda rep spoke with careful politeness. Groom's side tradition, he said. Equal respect, naturally, but the groom's family name carried the — he searched for a word that would not sound like war — *sequence*. The printer needed to know. The invitation needed to know. People would read it. People always read.

The Hegde-adjacent elder — not Ramesh, someone with the confidence of a man who had served on committees before committees were fashionable — countered without raising his voice. Host-side. Bride's family. Their village. Their temple hall. The bride's people had opened their doors; the banner should acknowledge that first. Tradition was a flexible word when status was at stake.

Meera felt something shift behind her eyes. Not alarm. Recognition.

This was about font size.

The absurdity was so precise it almost hurt. Two families who could not pass each other at a gate without weather, who had failed a meeting on a veranda with untouched cups, who were now negotiating a wedding reception in a temple hall they would share like strangers in a train compartment — and the battleground was typography. Whose name sat above whose. Whether equal size meant equal dignity or merely equal ink.

One elder suggested compromise: both names at equal size. Side by side. Democracy in Devanagari.

Another elder — older, slower, with the timing of a man who had waited his whole committee life for this sentence — shook his head.

"Equal size means nothing if one is on top."

The veranda went quiet for half a breath.

Meera felt her mouth twitch. She turned it into a cough. Saroja glanced at her — *behave* — and returned to scribbling. But something in Meera's chest had cracked open, not into superiority, not into the Bengaluru eye that filed villages as quaint, but into something warmer and more embarrassing: genuine amusement. This was ridiculous. This was life. Adults in plastic chairs under a neem tree fighting over vertical placement while the sun moved across cement and a property-boundary-blessing request from an entirely different family waited in the register like comic subplot furniture.

The chairs rearranged.

Not dramatically — no overturning, no walkouts. Just men standing, shifting, sitting in new configurations as if physical geometry could solve a problem that lived in pride. Scraping on cement. The third rearrangement of the morning. Someone moved to get better shade. Someone else moved to claim the chair that implied seniority. The pen passed again. The banner sketch acquired arrows and question marks.

A separate argument flickered at the edge — whether the wedding function or the boundary-blessing request took precedence on the calendar. Different family entirely. Meera caught fragments: *survey mark*, *temple trust*, *next month*. The committee members loved it. Petty governance as sport. Allocation as identity.

Meera stayed at the edge. No one asked her opinion. Young, city, woman — three reasons to be furniture. She was grateful. She watched the indirection with growing pleasure: Hegde and Gowda speaking through committee members, never to each other, never about the path, never about the shove that had not happened yet, never about anything that mattered in the way this mattered — which was not at all, and therefore completely.

Saroja scribbled faster. Notes that would become enforcement by tomorrow morning. *Equal size* circled. *Top/bottom — defer to printer?* written with the aggression of a woman solving problems the men had invented for sport.

The meeting ended without resolution. Banner names pending. Next meeting scheduled. Life continuing around the dispute the way life always continued around disputes — in registers and plastic chairs and the comedy of people who would fight over five thousand rupees of donation difference and call it tradition.

Meera stood when Saroja stood. The neem rustled. A temple bell somewhere, distant, indifferent. Her detachment had cracked. She could not return to pure superiority after nearly laughing at *equal size means nothing if one is on top*. Something had warmed. Not belonging — not yet — but the first crack in the armour of the city cousin who observed from a safe altitude.

Plastic chairs scraped on cement as the committee rearranged itself for the third time. Saroja furiously scribbling notes she would enforce tomorrow.

## S02 — City Girl

The road back from the temple was all sun.

Meera walked beside Saroja through heat that had decided ten o'clock was still morning and therefore forgivable. Wedding prep lists lived in Saroja's bag and in her mouth — flowers, vessels, garland strings that needed counting before the afternoon, chair covers that someone in Bengaluru would call unnecessary and someone in this lane would call essential. Meera let the logistics flow past like radio in another language. Her skin remembered shade. Her mouth remembered almost laughing. The sentence still lived there — *equal size means nothing if one is on top* — and she did not know if she was allowed to find it funny tomorrow when Saroja was in enforcement mode.

The lane narrowed and widened. A scooter passed. Dogs slept in patches that would become unbearable by noon. Farooq's store appeared where the village admitted commerce — low building, counter visible through the door, shade inside like a promise.

They turned in.

Darkness after sun. Narrow interior, shelves along one wall — soap, oil, spices, buckets, biscuits. Dust on glass where sunlight made a beam-pattern on worn labels. Smell mixed: sweet, dusty, oily.

Farooq was behind the counter.

Middle-aged, beard trimmed, shirt sleeves rolled to elbows that knew work, grin already in place as if he had been waiting for entertainment and Saroja's list was the opening act. His hands moved before his mouth finished greeting — reaching, weighing, the muscle memory of a man who could pack rice and joke simultaneously without spilling either. A biscuit tin sat open on the counter — lid pushed aside, not ceremonially, just always open, the way some doors in villages stayed unlatched because closing them would suggest strangers.

"Saroja-avve," he said. "The list. Let me guess — longer than yesterday."

"Don't guess. Read." Saroja unfolded paper. Business mode. Efficient. Pen behind her ear. "And the committee meeting — you heard?"

"Heard." Farooq began pulling items from shelves with the speed of a man who knew where everything lived without looking, which was most of the time, except when he looked anyway out of habit. "Banner fight?"

"Don't start."

"Who reads banners?" Farooq said, packing rice into a bag with one hand, the other already reaching for the next item on Saroja's list. "Only the printers."

Meera laughed before she could decide not to.

The sound surprised her — genuine, unperformed, escaping the observer posture she had been maintaining since the temple veranda. It came from somewhere below the city cousin's armour, somewhere that had nearly laughed at font size and had been converted into warmth instead of superiority. Farooq looked up. Not startled. Interested. His grin widened by a millimetre, the way shopkeepers widened grins when they found a new category of customer.

"City girl laughs at our fights, ah?"

The label landed without hostility. *City girl* — naming her as outsider, naming her as watcher, naming her as someone who stood at counter-ends and observed. Meera felt seen and simultaneously invited, as if the naming were a door left ajar rather than a wall. She could watch. She could also sit. The shop was neutral ground in a way the temple committee had not been; Farooq owed tabs to both families, joked about both families, supplied both families with soap and rice and the ordinary necessities that made feuds expensive. His side was the counter. His loyalty was transaction.

"Something had to be funny," she said. Minimal. Not clever. Just true.

"Tell them what I said." Farooq tied a bag with a twist that would survive a scooter journey. "Both names. Same size. Who cares. The wedding will happen, the food will happen, and in six months nobody will remember which name was on top unless someone kept the invitation to show their grandchildren." He glanced at Saroja without stopping his hands. "She will keep the invitation."

"I will keep everything," Saroja said. "That's the problem."

Farooq laughed. Items checked, prices murmured, bags filling. Meera stood at the counter-end, less detached now. Heat pressed against the door. Inside, shade and Tuesday commerce.

Farooq slid the biscuit tin toward her without ceremony.

"Take."

She reached across the counter. Dry biscuit, sweet, slightly warm where the tin had sat in a sun-patch near the door — the warmth of counter-level hospitality, not oven warmth, the warmth of things offered openly. She ate it. Small acceptance. Body saying yes to inclusion in a joke she had not earned and had been offered anyway.

Saroja counted change on the counter — coins aligned, notes folded, the arithmetic of a woman who would not be shortchanged by affection or distraction. Farooq's grin held. Sunlight found a jar of pickles and turned it amber. Dust motes in the beam. Street sounds filtered through — a child, a horn far away, the village continuing.

Meera chewed. Watched Saroja finish. Watched Farooq pack the last bag with the competence of a man who would remember *city girl* the way shopkeepers remembered everything — not as judgment, as category, as the beginning of a rapport that might matter later or might not, depending on how many times she walked through his door between now and the wedding.

They paid. They left. The sun hit again immediately.

Behind them on the counter: the biscuit tin lid, open. Farooq's grin. Saroja counting change one final time, lips moving on numbers that would become tomorrow's enforcement. Sunlight on dusty jars.
