# Chapter 28 — Two Pages Signed

## S01 — Both Pages

Nav arrived twenty minutes early and chose the table by the window because he needed to see the rain and not the door.

Grey Bengaluru sky pressed against the glass — pre-monsoon showers streaking down in thin lines, the street outside washed to a colour between asphalt and regret. Koramangala on a wet Friday looked like every other wet Friday: autos splashing through puddles, someone under a broken umbrella waiting for something, the particular grey of a city that had been promising monsoon for weeks and was finally beginning to mean it. The café was warm against it: wooden tables, low music, the murmur of other people's Fridays continuing without regard for what he was here to receive or refuse. Espresso machine hissing somewhere behind the counter. Coffee smell strong enough to cover nervousness if nervousness had a smell. He had slept badly — jaw tight since waking, time checked twice on the auto ride over — and ordered before sitting so his hands would have something to do besides rehearse sentences he already knew he would not use.

He had been here before. Not often — once with Meera years ago when she was visiting and they needed a neutral place to argue about something he no longer remembered. The memory was useful only as proof: this table did not belong to Vikram's company culture deck. No glass cabin. No carpet that made every step sound like apology. No shoulder touch that photographed well and delivered nothing.

The table felt neutral in the way only places that belonged to neither party could feel neutral. Rain on windows. A folder in his bag — empty for now, ready. A pen he had brought from home because he did not trust café pens for things that mattered. He set the pen beside the saucer. Waited. Watched rain streaks merge and separate on the glass like thoughts he was not having.

Nervous determination. The meeting was today. Three days since Vikram's assistant email — neutral café, half-day if you need it, let's close the loop. Close the loop. As if four years of trust were a cable that could be coiled and put away.

Vikram came in at eleven-oh-eight with the same smile Nav had been parsing for four years.

Warm. Immediate. The smile of a man who remembered your name and meant the remembering when he said it. Hand extended — handshake, not hug; professional distance dressed in affection — and Nav took it because refusing would have been a performance he was not staging today.

"Nav. Thanks for making the time."

"Yeah."

They ordered. Vikram asked after the team — genuine interest, or the performance of genuine interest that had always been indistinguishable from the real thing, which was somehow worse. Nav answered in fragments. The release had shipped. Two people were on leave. Normal Friday noise. Vikram nodded as if he were hearing weather reports from a country he also lived in.

They sat across from each other. Not side by side. Not intimate. The table between them wide enough for paper.

Vikram did not rush.

Nav appreciated that and resented it in equal measure.

"I know this has been hanging over us both, Nav."

Not an apology. An acknowledgment — the kind that admitted a gap without offering to close it, that saw the problem and would still only bring what fit in his hands. Vikram's voice was measured. Warm. He believed he was being fair. Nav had watched that belief operate for four years like a climate: constant, comfortable, unrelated to the math underneath.

Then Vikram reached into his bag and slid something across the table.

Two pages.

White. Clean. Legal format — the font of documents that had been to a lawyer and come back with margins and clause numbers and the quiet authority of print. Dated. Signable. Nav's name already on the signature line, typed where handwriting would eventually go.

He read.

Accelerated vesting schedule. Reduced share count.

Not the 2021 terms. Not the numbers Vikram had spoken in the car after the board deck shipped — *we'll take care of you, Nav, you know that, early team* — words that had lived in parking lots and corridor touches and nowhere on paper. This was smaller. Visibly smaller. The gap sat in the column where the share count should have been larger, where four years of loyalty should have translated into something that did not require squinting and swallowing.

Fewer. Faster.

He read the clauses without letting himself become a tutorial. He did not need to explain vesting to anyone — he had built the cap table model three times in spreadsheets Vikram never opened. What he needed was simpler and uglier: comparison. The oral promise floated in memory — not exact numbers, never exact, that was part of the cruelty — but near enough. Larger. This document was the same shape with less inside it, like a house plan with two rooms removed and the architect calling it an efficiency upgrade.

He turned the second page. Same structure. Same reduction dressed in legalese that made the insult look like procedure. Accelerated — vest now, not three years from now — so the document could claim generosity while delivering less than the promise that had kept him in a salary forty percent below market and a flat that knew only one person's weight. His name on the line. Dated today. Signable. Real.

Real was the word that hurt. Real meant he could not pretend this was fiction. Real meant rent would still get paid and the years would still not come back.

Four years.

Vikram waited without checking his phone. That, too, was a kind of respect — or a kind of theatre. Nav could not tell anymore and had stopped needing to. The warmth was real. The math was real. Both could be true and still not add up to what he deserved.

Vikram waited. Not fidgeting. Not triumphant. A man who had brought what he could and knew it was not everything and still believed it was something.

"This is what I can do, Nav. I wanted you to have something real."

Spoken with sincerity. Nav heard it cleanly — no villainy, no cartoon. Vikram running a company, a promise made in warmth that he could not fully keep, an offer now that existed because Nav had stayed civil long enough to receive it instead of burning the bridge on a Saturday night with a letter in his drafts folder.

The letter stays in drafts.

He knew it was there. Unsent. The road not taken — *I need to leave before the person I blame becomes me* — perfect words that had never acquired a *To:* field. He had chosen negotiation over rage — the unsent letter to the emails and meetings and this café with rain on the glass. This was where that path led. Not triumph. Settlement.

He could refuse. Get zero. Send the letter after all. Burn everything and call it integrity.

He could sign. Get partial. Live with the scar.

He looked at Vikram. Vikram looked back — warm, waiting, not pretending the document was full measure.

Nav uncapped his pen.

The scratch was audible.

First page. Signature line. His name in ink — curves he had written ten thousand times on expense forms and NDAs and the ordinary paperwork of employment, now on something that closed a wound imperfectly. He did not rush. Did not hesitate. Adult handwriting. Deliberate. The pen moving across the line with the steadiness of a man who had already decided and was only now letting his hand catch up.

He did not thank Vikram. Did not accuse. Did not say *I trusted you* because that sentence belonged to a younger version of this meeting — the version where he sent the draft and walked out into a different weather.

Second page. Same pen. Same steadiness. Both pages signed — original and counterpart, or two copies of one truth, he did not care which; the lawyer had formatted it and the formatting was not the point. The point was: paper now. Partial paper. His.

Done.

He slid the pages back across the table. Vikram took them. Folded them without flourish. The document's thinness visible — four years reduced to two sheets you could hold in one hand.

Vikram exhaled — relief that was not triumph, the breath of a man who had been holding something without knowing whether it would be accepted or returned across the table like an insult.

"We're good?"

Nav capped the pen. Looked at Vikram directly for the first time since the signing. Eye contact doing the work words would have cheapened.

"We're done."

Not *we're good*. Done. Functional. Scarred. Not friends. Not enemies. The relationship's new form — two pages and a boundary spoken in two syllables.

Vikram nodded. Stood. Brief — maybe another meeting, maybe respect for the space Nav would need after — and did not offer a handshake on the way out. Just a nod. Gone.

Nav put the copy into his folder. Closed it. The click sounded loud in the café murmur — or maybe he was only hearing his own ears.

Rain on the windows. Vikram's chair empty across from him. Vikram's coffee cup still on the table — half-finished, abandoned with the ease of a man who had other places to be. Nav's coffee cooling. The grey Bengaluru day continuing outside without regard for what had just happened inside. A couple at the next table laughed at something on a phone. Life continuing. That was the point, maybe — that life continued after incomplete settlements, that you signed and the city kept raining and other people's Fridays kept being Fridays.

Four years. Two pages. Less than was promised. More than nothing.

He had chosen this.

## S02 — Tell You Later

The café continued.

Other patrons talked. Music low. Espresso machine. Rain on glass — same streaks, or new ones; he had stopped tracking. Vikram's chair stayed empty. Nav sat with the folder closed on the table beside his cup, the pages inside real now, the pen capped, the morning's tension released into something that was not joy and not grief but the particular flatness that followed an irreversible adult decision.

Post-signing numb.

His coffee had gone cold. The smell changed — stale where it had been strong, the café warmth settling into afternoon without asking whether he was ready for afternoon. He did not order another. Did not leave immediately. There was nowhere urgent to be — half-day taken, office notified, the team running without him the way teams ran when you were early enough that your absence did not stop releases. He had earned the right to sit, and sitting was what the afternoon required.

He wanted to tell someone.

Meera would get this — the partial and the dignity of it, the not-zero and the not-enough, the choice that was not vindication and not surrender. She was in the village. Packing, probably. Saroja's voice in the background calling her name from the kitchen — he imagined it without detail, the way you imagined weather in a place you had never visited at the hour it was happening. Phone buried in a bag while the house prepared for her departure. Friday afternoon in a village four hundred kilometres away, where bus timings and tiffin boxes mattered more than blue ticks on a Bengaluru screen.

He opened his phone anyway.

Meera would get this.

*Got something. Tell you later.*

Sent.

Delivered. Blue ticks. He watched the screen the way you watched a door you had already closed — knowing what was on the other side and knowing you could not walk through it from here. The message sat in their thread below her last ping — *things wild here*, days ago, unanswered by him then, unanswered by her now. Asynchronous. Their normal. Usually tolerable. Today: the particular loneliness of having news and no audience.

No typing indicator. No reply.

She's packing.

Not neglect. Logistics. Lives at different speeds this week — his with two signed pages in a folder, hers with bags and bus timings and a grandmother's room to sit in one last time. He refreshed once. Stopped himself. Put the phone face-down on the table. She would see it later. Just not now.

The screen that didn't buzz — absence where notification should be. He had done something irreversible and no one in this room knew.

Alone with the news.

Same week the village had got its own imperfect resolution — he did not know details, only the temperature of her texts over the past days, *things calmed*, *path quiet*, the shorthand of someone reporting weather from a country he could not enter. Different costumes. Same scar shape. Partial paper here. Partial peace there. Bodies before paper in the village; paper arrived in the city, but reduced. He did not labour the parallel. It arrived in his chest without thesis — recognition as body-knowledge, not argument.

Same week. Different paper.

He was not the fool who had believed without question. Not the hero who had burned everything down. The adult who had negotiated and signed and said *we're done* and meant it.

Still in drafts.

The resignation letter would never leave his drafts folder now. That choice was permanent — confirmed in the pen-scratch and the folder-close and the rain running down glass he did not wipe. He would go back to work. Or home. Or somewhere. The afternoon ahead. The grey sky. The partial thing in the folder that was also, insultingly, also: enough to continue.

Signed two pages beside a finished coffee cup. Phone face-down — message sent, read receipt glowing faintly, no reply. Rain on glass continuing or slowing; he had stopped noticing which. The grey Bengaluru afternoon. Somewhere four hundred kilometres away his cousin was folding clothes into a bag and not looking at her phone.

He chose this.

The letter stayed in drafts. The pages were signed. And the rain did not care either way.
