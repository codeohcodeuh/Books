# Chapter 24 — Dust on the Bag

## S01 — Tamarind and Cart

Saturday morning Meera took the bus to town with Saroja's list on her phone and nothing else on her mind — or so she told herself. The list was real. The errand was real. Everything else was background she had learned not to touch.

Dust wind. Hot grit on the window glass. The taluk town arriving with its usual Saturday commerce — auto-rickshaws at the stand, vendors under awnings, the bus depot smell of diesel and sweat and frying oil from a cart near the depot entrance. Errand mode. Practical. Shankarappa absent four days now; the household tense with rumor-heat she felt at the edges without investigating — Saroja's phone face-down at meals, Ramesh on the porch too long, the guest room door closed and empty. Today: rice, dal, soap. Normal.

Farooq's store sat where it always sat — counter facing the door, bus stand visible through the glass, the geography of a town small enough that arrival and departure shared the same dust. Shelves of soap and dal and spice packets; rice sacks stacked against the wall; the radio low somewhere in the back. She already knew this place — warm counter, no gossip to her face, provisions stacked with the care of a man who knew his regulars. She liked him. He made town errands less lonely.

The household had sent her because Saroja's knee was acting up and Ramesh had not offered. Rumor-heat at home — phones at meals, porch silences, the empty guest mat visible every time she passed the corridor — but today the list was the list: practical before mysterious.

"City girl buying our rice now, ah?"

Farooq smiled from behind the counter. She smiled back. Easy rapport — the continuity of a relationship that had started with turmeric and patience.

"Ten kilo or five? Saroja didn't specify."

"Ten. And the dal — the Toor, not the Moong. She'll send it back if you give me Moong."

"Your aunt is particular."

"My aunt is Saroja."

He laughed. Another customer stood at the far end of the counter — an older woman buying matches and something in a small bottle, the ordinary cover of a Saturday morning. Meera waited while Farooq served them, checked her list on the phone screen, counted rice bags in her head against what the kitchen actually consumed. Ten kilo rice. Two kilo dal. Soap — the brand Saroja liked, not the cheaper one that smelled wrong. She argued briefly about the soap price — not because she cared about two rupees but because arguing was part of the errand's texture, the proof that she lived here now.

"You're not even bargaining properly," Farooq said. "Your aunt would have taken ten rupees off."

"I'm learning."

The door opened.

A man entered — fifty or thereabouts, standard shirt the color of every standard shirt on every bus, dusty shoes, canvas bag on his shoulder. The kind of bag everyone carried between towns — neither new nor memorable, anonymity as default. Dust on the bag and the shoes suggesting long travel. He did not approach the counter immediately. Waited near the door, eyes on the middle distance, patient in the way of someone who had done this before.

The other customer paid and left — change counted, door chime if there was a chime, the store returning to two people and Meera.

The traveler approached Farooq.

Meera was still at the counter — her rice being weighed on the old scale, or about to be bagged — close enough to hear everything without straining.

"The tamarind cutting went well; he should know the cart is ready."

One sentence. Spoken clearly — not whispered, not secret, but delivered in a rhythm that was wrong for conversation. Rehearsed. Precise. Meant for Farooq the way a prescription was meant for a pharmacist. No greeting. No context. No follow-up. Delivered, not discussed.

Farooq's face paused — micro-second, eyes flicking to the man, recognition that was not surprise. Reception. A nod, small and economical.

"Good."

One word. Period. The transaction complete — not commercial, not social. Communicative. Something crossing the counter that was not rice or dal.

The traveler did not buy anything. Did not sit. Did not browse. Did not pretend to need soap or matches as cover. Turned toward the door. Walked toward the bus stand visible through the glass — thirty meters, the geography of a town small enough that arrival and departure could happen in under a minute. Sixty seconds total, maybe less. Entry to exit without wasted movement. Meera counted without meaning to — one one-thousand, two one-thousand — and he was already at the door, already gone from the store's small world.

Meera's body registered before her mind: that wasn't natural.

The store smelled of rice dust and soap boxes and the ordinary commerce of provisions — and underneath that, for a second, something else: the aftertaste of a sentence that did not belong to shopping.

Tamarind. Cart.

Courtyard vocabulary echoing — the tree that fell in '88, the path measured in cart-widths, terms she had heard once in a courtyard and carried since without fully understanding. *Tamarind cutting.* *Cart ready.* The words sat in her body like stones from a language lesson she had not signed up for. She could not be sure the sentence connected to the path dispute. But the delivery-mode was wrong for gossip, wrong for small talk, wrong for anything except communication from A to B. And Farooq had received it without surprise — which meant he had received them before.

Farooq was already back to normal — wrapping her rice in plastic, face reset, banter-voice returning as if the pause might have been imagined if you had not been watching closely. The store smelled of rice dust and soap boxes again — ordinary covering whatever had passed through it.

"Two kg dal also?"

"Two kg. Toor."

"Good."

Same word. Different register. She watched his hands tie the bag. Steady. Ordinary.

"Thank you, Farooq."

He smiled. She walked out with plastic handles cutting slightly into her palm, provisions complete, list checked off on her phone. The bell above the door if there was a bell. Heat and dust wind hitting her face like a ordinary wall.

The traveler was already gone from view — toward the bus stand, dust on his bag already blending with town dust, anonymous within seconds of arriving.

Three things now.

She did not say it aloud. Her body knew — Shankarappa, Gayatri, now this. A pattern forming without permission.

## S02 — Hundred Footprints

Outside, she looked toward the bus stand.

The traveler: not visible. Saturday bustle — people with bags, buses loading and unloading, the ordinary churn of departure. A bus pulled away toward the highway, diesel smoke trailing; another idled with its door open. She could not know which one he had taken. Or whether he had walked somewhere else entirely. The system did not leave traces for observers who were not meant to observe.

Footprints in the dust — a hundred of them, overlapping, none distinguishable. Everyone had that bag. Everyone had those shoes. The canvas that might have been his already indistinguishable from every other canvas that passed through this stand on every other Saturday. Meera had seen Shankarappa arrive dusty at this same stand — anonymous bag, the same infrastructure carrying people who did not announce themselves. The traveler had echoed that mode without copying it. Design, not coincidence.

Which one?

She stood at the edge of the stand and scanned and found nothing anomalous in a field of ordinary. A woman argued with an auto driver about fare. A boy sold peanuts from a tray. Buses came and went. The anonymity was not a failure of attention. It was architecture.

If I ask—

The thought broke off. To go back into Farooq's store and say *who was that?* would expose that she had noticed. That she had seen the pause. That she had been watching closely enough to build a pattern — Shankarappa, Gayatri, now this. Asking would make her a grabber. Rohan's probe had felt wrong in her body for the same reason: grabbing at something not offered, pulling into visibility what had been functioning in quiet.

Grabbing breaks things.

She chose not to ask Farooq. Not from fear. Not from politeness. From the same instinct that had closed the door on the journalist — if she reached for this, it might break. If she waited... she did not know what waiting gave. But grabbing was wrong. The watching-cost registered as complicity and shame together: to ask would expose her watching; to stay silent was to participate in concealment again. Same complicity as with the journalist. Same refusal to chase as when the guest left. Now this — stillness as decision.

She could have gone back. Three steps to the store door. One question. *Who was that man?* Farooq would have reset his face and answered with something easy and the system would have kept moving and she would have marked herself as someone who grabbed. She did not go back.

She turned away from the bus stand.

Rice in one hand. Dal and soap in the other. Provisions complete. List checked off on her phone. The world unchanged on the surface — same dust wind, same hot grit, same town returning to its Saturday shape.

Except now she knew: something had moved through Farooq's store that was not for her to see. And it had moved perfectly.

Bus stand behind her. Dust settling in the wind. The bag the traveler carried already gone into the anonymous stream of every other bag that ever passed through. A hundred footprints in the dust — all the same. None his. None hers.

She walked toward the auto stand and did not look back. A question planted in her body — unnamed, unasked — carried the way she carried Rohan's refusal and the empty guest mat: quietly, without grabbing.
