# Chapter 1 — Visitor Parking

The rice was still warm enough to eat without reheating.

Meera took the plate from the microwave, felt the heat through ceramic, and set it on the kitchen counter where evening light had gone thin and yellow. She did not bother with a proper seat. Hip against the edge. Spoon in hand. Work clothes still on — soft kurta for office days that did not need a jacket, jeans that had held their shape through a long Wednesday. Outside, Malleshwaram released the day's heat from old walls and tar. The window screen held no breeze. The ceiling fan turned after seven with no real air behind it.

She had been looking forward to this exact nothing: leftover rice, phone face-down later, alone without performing competence for anyone.

The flat had learned her habits long before anyone else had.

Her handbag always landed on the kitchen chair instead of the hook beside the door. Shoes waited neatly against the wall because untidy shoes bothered her more than an untidy dining table. The dining table had long ago surrendered to other work: laptop charger, two unopened courier envelopes, a library book due back three days earlier, a ceramic bowl holding loose coins and hair ties.

The silence after work had its own routine. Microwave first. Wash the lunch box before the sambar dried into something that required effort. Fill the steel bottle from the purifier. Tonight qualified as ordinary.

She liked that about Wednesdays.

They belonged to nobody.

No family video calls that stretched politely past an hour. No team catch-ups pretending not to be meetings. No weekend plans waiting to be made or declined. Wednesday evenings asked nothing except that they be allowed to become night.

She crossed to the sink and rinsed the spoon she had used to stir the rice before changing her mind and taking another from the drawer instead. The first one had felt too small. The second felt right. She noticed herself doing it and almost laughed. Tiny preferences multiplied when no one else was around to witness them.

Outside, somebody rolled shut the neighbourhood bakery's metal shutter. The sound travelled up the street in one long scrape. Somewhere in the next block a pressure cooker released steam in three quick sighs.

She looked once toward the phone where it rested beside the salt jar, screen black, charging cable bent gently against the counter. She could still put it in the bedroom. She had promised herself she would. Evenings had become easier since making that rule. Fewer pointless arguments about parking. Water supply. Dogs. Lift maintenance.

She left the phone where it was.

First bite. A little dry. Oil at the edges. Fine.

It buzzed once against the counter.

She chewed. Did not look. The group could wait. Everything in every group could wait. That was the rule she had made for evenings, and she had kept it for almost a week — discipline, by her measure.

Another buzz. Then a third, close enough that the vibration travelled into the plate and made a soft ring against the spoon.

Meera swallowed and kept eating. Traffic murmured beyond the compound wall. A pressure cooker two floors above hissed and finished. She wanted them to be enough.

The fourth buzz brought a preview that slid across the glass before she meant to see it.

*Unauthorized vehicle in Resident Parking Slot B-2*

She put the spoon down.

Not yet, she told herself. She wiped her fingers on a tissue, delaying by half a breath the thing that had already arrived. Then she pulled the cable free and unlocked the phone.

The apartment WhatsApp group was a climbing stack. Messages over messages. Asha's name at the head of most of them, the way Asha's name always found the head of things.

The group had existed for years and had accumulated the sediment of apartment life. Water timings. Lift repairs. Power cuts. Notices about pest control. A photograph of a dead pigeon with three different suggestions for whom to call.

There were rules about what could be posted, and rules about the rules.

Asha usually enforced both.

Meera had muted the group twice. The first time, she missed a maintenance shutdown and spent the next morning without water. The second time, she discovered that muting did not prevent people from tagging you. After that she left notifications on. URGENT usually meant somebody's driver had blocked the gate. Please note meant nothing was urgent. Requesting everyone's cooperation meant Asha was irritated.

Meera rarely contributed.

Mostly she read.

Sometimes the group was useful — a late pharmacy, a loose tile, the lift stuck between floors before the guard reached the panel. Other times it was simply there, a second layer of building noise.

Meera knew whose messages would arrive before opening them. Asha's would be complete sentences, punctuation intact. Mr. Rais from 3B sent voice notes regardless of whether the matter required a voice note. The Doshi family used the group as if it were a family chat.

She had also never left the group.

There was comfort in knowing that if something happened in the building, someone would notice. That was what the group was for. Someone noticing.

Her thumb moved down the screen.

Tonight, someone had noticed the car.

*Resident Parking Slot B-2 — vehicle without resident sticker. Photo attached. Timestamp 6:48 p.m.*

*Who parked this?*

*Second reminder. Number plate visible.*

Meera scrolled with her thumb, the rice cooling beside her hand.

The photo loaded slowly on her network, then all at once: the painted B-2 on the pillar, a strip of evening concrete, a white hatchback angled a little wrong. The plate number sharp under whatever light Asha had used. Caption neat as a filled form.

Slot B-2.

Meera's thumb stopped.

She knew the car before she let herself know it.

The small scratch near the rear bumper was from the basement pillar on the day Divya had insisted she could park without Meera's help. She had reversed twice, stopped, opened the door, looked at the distance, and declared the pillar "aggressively close."

Meera had laughed so hard she had to sit down.

Divya still told the story as if the pillar had moved.

The faded sticker on the windshield was another thing she had refused to remove. It had come from a weekend trip two years ago, when the two of them had driven to Tumakuru on a Friday evening and spent most of Saturday arguing about whether they were lost. Divya had wanted to follow the road signs. Meera had trusted Google Maps. They had eventually discovered that both methods could be wrong in different ways.

Divya had bought the sticker at a roadside shop because she liked the colours.

"You keep everything," Meera had told her.

"You keep people," Divya had replied.

She had said it casually, while trying to peel the backing off the sticker.

Meera had pretended not to hear.

That was Divya's particular talent. She could say something that sounded like a joke and leave it sitting somewhere behind you until much later.

The car had been in Meera's slot since that morning because Divya had been running late for the airport train.

She had arrived at the door with the trolley bag half open, one sandal strap twisted, and her phone trapped between her shoulder and ear.

"Just till evening, na?"

Meera had looked at the empty resident slot visible through the balcony and shrugged.

"Take it."

"You're sure?"

"It's a parking space, Divya."

"Exactly. You say that now. Tomorrow you'll tell me I've ruined your life."

"I'll survive."

Divya had grinned, pressed the spare key into her palm and hurried toward the lift.

Three minutes later she had come back because she'd forgotten her passport.

Meera had stood in the doorway holding the key and laughing.

"Go," she'd told her. "Before you miss the train."

"Call me when you get home."

"I'll be home before you."

"Then send me a photo of the car."

"Why?"

"So I know it's safe."

"You mean so you know I haven't sold it."

"Same thing."

Then the lift doors had closed.

Meera had gone back inside, put the spare key on the kitchen counter, and forgotten about it.

She looked again at the photograph on her phone.

The car sat exactly where she had left it.

Slightly crooked.

Divya would have laughed.

She had parked it that morning.

She pinched the screen. Zoomed. Released. Plate unchanged. The painted B-2 unchanged. Timestamp offering no alternate morning in which she had been wiser.

More messages arrived while she stared.

*Please identify.*

*Please move the vehicle to Visitor Parking and obtain a temporary overnight visitor parking pass from Security.*

*@Meera — is this yours?*

Her name sat in the chat like a sticker on a parcel nobody wanted to claim.

Facts lined up. They did not argue.

The rice waited, half-eaten, grains going stiff at the rim. She did not pick up the spoon again.

Her thumb hovered over reply.

*Sorry — getting a visitor parking pass now.*

Too thin. She deleted.

*It's my friend's — temporary —*

Already a defence. Deleted before it became a sentence.

In the group, blue ticks appeared under Asha's photo. People were looking. Names she knew from Dasara sweets and AGM attendance and the hellos of people who shared a lift and little else.

Heat pressed at the window glass. The fan kept its useless circle. On the counter the plate cooled by degrees she would feel later with the back of her hand.

Meera stood with the phone warm in her palm and the meal unfinished, her name still bright on the screen beside the slot.

---

She left the plate where it was.

Phone in pocket. Flat door shut with more care than the situation deserved. The stairwell smelled of dust, phenyl, old paint. Tube light on the second landing flickered once and held. Her slippers slapped concrete in a rhythm that felt too loud, as if the building were listening for the person attached to B-2.

The lift was faster, technically, but Meera took the stairs when she was upset. The lift made people stand facing one another in a rectangle and pretend not to listen. The stairs allowed her to hear herself think.

On the first landing, somebody had left a pair of children's sandals outside the door — one upright, one on its side. Meera had seen them there for three days. They belonged to someone else's small chaos.

On the next floor, rasam with too much coriander; the third-floor auntie cooked early and kept her kitchen window open.

Near the ground floor, the association noticeboard held layer on layer of pasted warnings until the corners had thickened. The latest notice had Asha's initials at the bottom.

The building was full of things nobody quite moved. Meera knew most of them without thinking. That was what happened when you lived somewhere long enough. The same woman sweeping the corridor at six. The same uncle pressing the lift button twice. The same arguments about parking every few months.

Usually, they belonged to somebody else.

She reached the ground floor.

The security cabin was visible through the glass doors, the guard leaning back in his chair with a newspaper folded across his lap. Outside, a scooter coughed to life. Someone's child ran past the entrance with a cricket bat held like a sword.

For one second, Meera felt the familiar relief of coming home.

Then she remembered why she was going downstairs.

She pushed open the door.

Divya's hatchback. Still. Slightly crooked. The painted B-2 on the pillar faded to the colour of old teeth. Heat rose off the concrete in a slow sheet. Somewhere a scooter reversed with a complaining whine. The air smelled of dust and hot metal and the faint sweetness of something rotting in a drain the association had been meaning to fix since last monsoon.

Asha was already there.

Phone in hand, screen angled toward the plate, posture of a person completing documentation rather than starting a quarrel. She looked up when Meera's slippers reached the slot. No smile. No sneer. Only the tight composure of someone who had been correct since morning and saw no reason to stop.

"Meera. Thank you for coming down."

Behind Asha, near the scooter row, an uncle from the third floor stood with his hands linked behind his back. Watching. Not commenting. Meera knew his face from festival sweets and from the way he always pressed the lift button twice. She had never disliked him. She disliked, suddenly and precisely, that he was here.

She nodded once and went to the driver's door as if speed itself could shrink the audience. The handle bit heat into her palm. She reached for the spare key she was certain she had dropped into her pocket upstairs —

Empty.

Another pocket. Empty. Kurta side pocket: phone, nothing else. She patted once more, uselessly, hoping physics might revise itself.

Of course.

Divya's spare was in the bag on the kitchen chair, dropped there that morning while Meera was already late for a stand-up. She saw it with insulting clarity: strap hanging, side pocket, key under a lip balm and a folded bill from the Lakshmi Provision Stores. The morning had been all hurry and kindness. The evening had turned both into evidence.

Asha did not step into her path. She also did not make space as if space were a gift. She remained where she was, phone still lit, the small circle of light finding the number plate and then the painted B-2. Sweat had made a faint shine at Asha's temple. Even correctness had a body.

"This car needs a pass," Asha said. "Visitor registration. Photos. You understand."

Meera's mouth found something short. "I understand. Friend's car. Airport. I'll get a pass."

"Acknowledgement helps," Asha said, mild as water left too long in a bottle.

Meera could have said that Divya was gone, that her slot had been empty, that she would not make a habit of it. Each sentence arrived pre-weighed and too heavy. In committee she was always a step behind. She heard herself offer only the logistics again, thinner: "I'm moving it."

"Yes," Asha said. "Please."

Exact, and not unkind.

The uncle shifted his weight from one slipper to the other. Looked at the slot number. Looked at the hatchback. Looked away toward the gate, where a guard was arguing with a delivery boy about a parcel that would not fit on a bike. Said nothing. Meera felt that if he had joked or scolded it might have been easier than this — a man who would remember and not intervene.

Meera's phone buzzed against her hip, the group still chewing. She did not take it out. Above them a child's voice called for water and was answered. Life continued in layers that did not know about Slot B-2.

"Key's upstairs," Meera said. The sentence came out flatter than pride preferred. "Two minutes."

Asha's flashlight clicked brighter. The beam found the painted B-2 and held it — a white number inside a hard circle of light. The light caught a moth, lost it. The number remained.

"I'll wait," Asha said.

No cruelty in it. Only the calm of a person who had nowhere else she needed to be more than here.

Meera turned toward the stairs. Her slippers found the first step. Then the second. The hatchback remained slightly crooked, innocent of committee language. Heat breathed from the wall beside her hand.

The climb was longer than the descent. Air trapped in the stairwell, thick with cooking from somebody's delayed dinner. Behind her the flashlight stayed on the bay, and every step stretched a small wrong.

She did not look back; she did not need to. The light on the number followed her up the concrete.

---

The spare was exactly where she had pictured it. Two apartment keys. One office access fob. A tiny enamel kingfisher whose blue had chipped away over the years until only one wing still remembered the colour.

She came back down, the key fisted in her hand. She unlocked Divya's car under Asha's still-bright phone, and drove first to the security cabin by the gate. Registration number. Flat number. A few photographs. The guard wrote out a temporary overnight visitor pass without hurry and pointed at the dashboard beneath the windshield. She thanked him, tucked the pass beneath the windshield, and drove the hatchback to one of the marked Visitor Parking spaces near the compound wall, the ones nobody preferred because the Gulmohar dropped sticky litter and birds used the mirrors as a perch.

Then it was done. Slot B-2 empty behind her. Asha still in the lot with her phone, finishing the record. Meera locked the car, walked back without hurrying, and took the stairs because waiting for the lift would have meant standing still in fluorescent light with her own face.

Upstairs, the flat had not moved to meet her.

Nothing had happened here. The fan still turned at the same speed. The kitchen light still threw its small yellow pool across the counter. Her handbag was still on the chair.

For a moment she stood in the doorway and looked at everything as if she had returned to somebody else's home.

The silence was different now.

An hour ago, it had been what she wanted. Now she could hear the refrigerator cycling on and off. From the neighbouring flat came the television laughter, only now it sounded too close.

She walked to the kitchen and touched the plate. Cold. She had known it would be. Still, the small confirmation disappointed her.

Her phone was in her pocket.

She took it out, locked it, placed it face-down. Then picked it up again.

She opened the group.

Nobody had said anything about her.

That should have helped.

It didn't.

She scrolled upward, past the photograph, past Asha's reminder, past her own name. Her name looked strangely formal there, as if somebody had entered it into a register.

She closed the chat.

There was nothing wrong with the flat.

That was the problem.

Everything was exactly where she had left it.

Only she had returned different.

The rice sat on the counter, dull and cold, a skin forming at the rim.

Her phone had not rested.

Asha's new message sat at the top of the group like minutes from a meeting Meera had not agreed to attend.

*Update: Visitor vehicle has been moved to Visitor Parking and registered with Security. Residents are reminded that overnight visitor vehicles must be registered and display a valid visitor pass. Timestamp 8:14 p.m.*

Below it, read receipts multiplied. Names from Dasara. Names from the complaint about generator noise. Blue ticks stacking without replies, people who had seen enough and agreed by looking.

Meera opened a reply box.

*Noted. It was a one-time situation —*

Delete. Already defensive.

*Sorry for the inconvenience. Won't happen again. The car belongs to a friend who —*

Too much. Delete.

*Moved.*

Too clipped. Sounded angry. Or guilty. Delete.

She typed a fourth beginning — *Thanks for flagging* — and erased it before the second word finished. Not on a Wednesday. Not for free.

The ticks continued. The fan turned its slow circle. Night heat sat in the rooms like a held breath. A neighbour's television laughed on cue.

She turned the phone face-down on the counter.

Light leaked through the case, thin and blue-white, the chat still alive underneath, ticks still arriving in a world she had decided not to watch. She scraped the cold rice into the bin. The spoon chimed once against steel. A few grains clung to the plate; she rinsed them without ceremony and left the plate in the rack.

Then the flat held only the fan and the small glow that would not quite go dark. The blue light kept gathering beneath the phone case, asking for nothing except that she look.
