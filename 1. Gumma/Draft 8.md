# Chapter 1 — Visitor Parking

The rice was still warm enough to eat without reheating.

Meera took the plate from the microwave, felt the heat through ceramic, and set it on the kitchen counter where evening light had gone thin and yellow. She did not bother with a proper seat. Hip against the edge. Spoon in hand. Work clothes still on, the soft kurta she wore for office days that did not require a jacket, jeans that had held their shape through a long Wednesday. Outside, Malleshwaram released the day's heat from old walls and tar. The window screen held no breeze. The ceiling fan turned after seven with no real breeze behind it.

She had been looking forward to this exact nothing: leftover rice, phone face-down later, alone without performing competence for anyone.

First bite. A little dry. Oil at the edges. Fine.

Her phone lay face-up beside the salt, screen dark, charging cable still attached from when she had dropped her bag an hour earlier. She had meant to put it in another room. She never did.

It buzzed once against the counter.

She chewed. Did not look. The group could wait. Everything in every group could wait. That was the rule she had made for evenings, and she had kept it for almost a week, which, for her, counted as discipline.

Another buzz. Then a third, close enough that the vibration travelled into the plate and made a soft ring against the spoon.

Meera swallowed and kept eating. Traffic murmured beyond the compound wall. A pressure cooker two floors above hissed and finished. She wanted them to be enough.

The fourth buzz brought a preview that slid across the glass before she meant to see it.

*Unauthorized vehicle in Resident Parking Slot B-2*

She put the spoon down.

Not yet, she told herself, and wiped her fingers on a tissue, delaying by half a breath the thing that had already arrived. Then she pulled the cable free and unlocked the phone.

The apartment WhatsApp group was a climbing stack. Messages over messages. Asha's name at the head of most of them, the way Asha's name always found the head of things.

*Resident Parking Slot B-2 — vehicle without resident sticker. Photo attached. Timestamp 6:48 p.m.*

*Who parked this?*

*Second reminder. Number plate visible.*

Meera scrolled with her thumb, the rice cooling beside her hand.

The photo loaded slowly on her network, then all at once: the painted B-2 on the pillar, a strip of evening concrete, a white hatchback angled a little wrong. The plate number sharp under whatever light Asha had used. Caption neat as a filled form.

Slot B-2.

Meera's thumb stopped.

She knew the car before she let herself know it. The small scratch near the rear bumper from a basement parking scrape Divya still told as comedy. The faded sticker on the windshield Divya had never peeled. The way it sat: Divya always parked a little crooked and laughed when anyone mentioned it.

She had parked it that morning.

She pinched the screen. Zoomed. Released. Plate unchanged. The painted B-2 unchanged. Timestamp offering no alternate morning in which she had been wiser.

More messages arrived while she stared.

*Please identify.*

*Please move the vehicle to Visitor Parking and obtain a temporary overnight visitor parking pass from Security.*

*@Meera — is this yours?*

Her name sat in the chat like a sticker on a parcel nobody wanted to claim.

She checked the photo's time against the morning she remembered: Divya in the doorway with a trolley bag, late for the airport train, spare key pressed into Meera's palm, a quick *just till evening, na*, and her own resident slot empty and convenient and, Meera had thought, the sort of small wrong that dissolved by dinner.

Facts lined up. They did not argue.

The rice waited, half-eaten, grains going stiff at the rim. She did not pick up the spoon again.

Her thumb hovered over reply.

*Sorry — getting a visitor parking pass now.*

Too thin. She deleted.

*It's my friend's — temporary —*

Already a defence. Deleted before it became a sentence.

In the group, blue ticks appeared under Asha's photo. People were looking. Names she knew from Dasara sweets and AGM attendance and the hellos of people who shared a lift and little else.

Heat pressed at the window glass. The fan kept its useless circle. On the counter the plate cooled by degrees she would feel later with the back of her hand.

Meera stood with the phone warm in her palm and the meal unfinished, her name still bright on the screen beside the slot.

---

She left the plate where it was.

Bag in pocket. Flat door shut with more care than the situation deserved. The stairwell smelled of dust, phenyl, old paint. Tube light on the second landing flickered once and held. Her slippers slapped concrete in a rhythm that felt too loud, as if the building were listening for the person attached to B-2.

The slot was exactly where the photo had said.

Divya's hatchback. Still. Slightly crooked. The painted B-2 on the pillar faded to the colour of old teeth. Heat rose off the concrete in a slow sheet. Somewhere a scooter reversed with a complaining whine. The air smelled of dust and hot metal and the faint sweetness of something rotting in a drain the association had been meaning to fix since last monsoon.

Asha was already there.

Phone in hand, screen angled toward the plate, posture of a person completing documentation rather than starting a quarrel. She looked up when Meera's slippers reached the slot. No smile. No sneer. Only the tight composure of someone who had been correct since morning and saw no reason to stop.

"Meera. Thank you for coming down."

Behind Asha, near the scooter row, an uncle from the third floor stood with his hands linked behind his back. Watching. Not commenting. Meera knew his face from festival sweets and from the way he always pressed the lift button twice. She had never disliked him. She disliked, suddenly and precisely, that he was here.

She nodded once and went to the driver's door as if speed itself could shrink the audience. The handle bit heat into her palm. She reached for the spare key she was certain she had dropped into her pocket upstairs —

Empty.

Another pocket. Empty. Kurta side pocket: phone, nothing else. She patted once more, uselessly, hoping physics might revise itself.

Of course.

Divya's spare was in the bag on the kitchen chair, dropped there that morning while Meera was already late for a stand-up. She saw it with insulting clarity: strap hanging, side pocket, key under a lip balm and a folded bill from the Lakshmi Provision Stores. The morning had been all hurry and kindness. The evening had turned both into evidence.

Asha did not step into her path. She also did not make space as if space were a gift. She remained where she was, phone still lit, the small circle of light finding the number plate and then the painted B-2. Sweat had made a faint shine at Asha's temple. Even correctness had a body.

"This car needs a pass," Asha said. "Visitor registration. Photos. You understand."

Meera's mouth found something short. "I understand. Friend's car. Airport. I'll get a pass."

"Acknowledgement helps," Asha said, mild as water left too long in a bottle.

As if the noise had simply arrived.

Meera could have said that Divya was gone, that her slot had been empty, that she would not make a habit of it. Each sentence arrived pre-weighed and too heavy. In committee she was always a step behind. She heard herself offer only the logistics again, thinner: "I'm moving it."

"Yes," Asha said. "Please."

Exact, and not unkind.

The uncle shifted his weight from one slipper to the other. Looked at the slot number. Looked at the hatchback. Looked away toward the gate, where a guard was arguing with a delivery boy about a parcel that would not fit on a bike. Said nothing. Meera felt that if he had joked or scolded it might have been easier than this neutral attention, the attention of a man who would remember and not intervene.

Meera's phone buzzed against her hip, the group still chewing. She did not take it out. Above them a child's voice called for water and was answered. Life continued in layers that did not know about Slot B-2.

"Key's upstairs," Meera said. The sentence came out flatter than pride preferred. "Two minutes."

Asha's flashlight clicked brighter. The beam found the painted B-2 and held it, a white number inside a hard circle of light, a parking bay turned into a stage with one prop and an audience of two. The light caught a moth, lost it. The number remained.

"I'll wait," Asha said.

No cruelty in it. Only the calm of a person who had nowhere else she needed to be more than here.

Meera turned toward the stairs. Her slippers found the first step. Then the second. The hatchback stayed where it was, crooked, innocent of committee language. Heat came off the wall beside her hand.

The climb was longer than the descent. Air trapped in the stairwell, thick with cooking from somebody's delayed dinner. Behind her the flashlight stayed on the bay, and every step stretched a small wrong.

She did not look back; she did not need to. The light on the number followed her up the concrete.

---

The spare was exactly where she had pictured it: cold metal, side pocket, under the lip balm.

She came back down, the key fisted in her hand. She unlocked Divya's car under Asha's still-bright phone, and drove first to the security cabin by the gate. Registration number. Flat number. A few photographs. The guard wrote out a temporary overnight visitor pass without hurry and pointed at the dashboard beneath the windshield. She thanked him, tucked the pass beneath the windshield, and drove the hatchback to one of the marked Visitor Parking spaces near the compound wall, the ones nobody preferred because the Gulmohar dropped sticky litter and birds used the mirrors as a perch.

Then it was done. Slot B-2 empty behind her. Asha still in the lot with her phone, finishing the record. Meera locked the car, walked back without hurrying, and took the stairs because waiting for the lift would have meant standing still in fluorescent light with her own face.

Upstairs, the flat had not moved to meet her.

The rice sat on the counter, dull and cold, a skin forming at the rim. She shut the door and stood with her back against it for a count of three, then put the key in the bag as if returning an object to its place could return an hour.

Her phone had not rested.

Asha's new message sat at the top of the group like minutes from a meeting Meera had not agreed to attend.

*Update: Visitor vehicle has been moved to Visitor Parking and registered with Security. Residents are reminded that overnight visitor vehicles must be registered and display a valid visitor pass. Timestamp 8:14 p.m.*

Below it, read receipts multiplied. Names from Dasara. Names from the complaint about generator noise. Blue ticks stacking without replies, people who had seen enough and agreed by looking.

Meera opened a reply box.

*Noted. It was a one-time situation —*

Delete. Already defensive.

*Sorry for the inconvenience. Won't happen again. The car belongs to a friend who —*

Too much. Delete.

*Moved.*

Too clipped. Sounded angry. Or guilty. Delete.

She typed a fourth beginning — *Thanks for flagging* — and erased it before the second word finished, that did not cost something she did not want to pay on a Wednesday.

The ticks continued. The fan turned its slow circle. Night heat sat in the rooms like a held breath. A neighbour's television laughed on cue.

She turned the phone face-down on the counter.

Light leaked through the case, thin and blue-white, the chat still alive underneath, ticks still arriving in a world she had decided not to watch. She scraped the cold rice into the bin. The spoon chimed once against steel. A few grains clung to the plate; she rinsed them without ceremony and left the plate in the rack.

Then the flat held only the fan and the small glow that would not quite go dark. Blue light kept stacking under the plastic, unread.

# Chapter 2 — The Hallway Promise

Naveen stood three metres from Vikram's glass cabin with his phone open to notes he had rewritten twice that morning and once in the auto on the way in.

Bullet points. Clean. Professional-casual. *Equity structure — ask, don't accuse. Vesting clarity. Timeline that isn't "soon." Calendar hold before we leave the conversation.* The words looked like a person who had slept. He had not slept well. Outside the corridor windows the March glare sat white on the glass. Inside, the AC hummed. Carpet under his shoes, slightly too soft. Down the hall a printer coughed out a page and went quiet.

He had asked for a quick sync. Vikram had said, earlier in the week, *catch up*. Today was the catch-up. Naveen's pulse knew it before his face did.

The cabin was empty when he arrived. Vikram's jacket hung on the chair inside, and a laptop open to slides — the board deck, probably. Through the glass the open-plan floor looked calm: heads, monitors, keyboards. Meera was at her desk in the far cluster, headphones on, frowning at a ticket. She did not look up. Good. He did not want an audience for hope.

A colleague from growth stood at the printer with a sheaf of papers, scrolling his own phone between page grabs. Close enough to see a conversation. Not close enough, Naveen told himself, to hear one. Public enough that pressing would become a story. He had still come.

Eleven twenty-two. He refreshed nothing on his notes. The notes did not need refreshing. They needed a person on the other side who would stay still long enough to answer them.

The cabin door opened.

Vikram came out moving already, phone in one hand, the other lifting in greeting before his eyes had fully found Naveen. The smile arrived on schedule: warm, quick, the smile of a man who remembered names and kept loyalty liquid. His shirt looked pressed. His watch caught the glare and threw it.

"Hey — yes, I wanted to catch you too," Vikram said, as if Naveen had not been the one waiting with a script in his palm.

Naveen's prepared opener shortened in his mouth. "Got a minute? On the ESOP thing — just want to understand the structure, lock a proper time on calendar—"

Vikram's hand landed on his shoulder.

Warm. Firm. Naveen felt the heat of it through cotton.

"Absolutely," Vikram said. "Let's lock it."

Naveen almost believed him.

Then, still smiling, still touching: "After the board deck ships next week. Once that's out of the way we can sit properly. You know how it is — this week's a wash. Back-to-backs. You of all people get it."

The hand stayed a beat longer than the sentence needed. Vikram's body had already angled toward the cabin again. His tone did not cool. The deferral arrived wrapped in the same heat as the promise.

"Next week," Naveen said, and heard how little it contained. He had three follow-ups ready. None of them fit in a corridor with a colleague at a printer and Vikram's phone lighting up against his thigh.

"Next week," Vikram echoed, brighter. "You've been patient. You're early team, Naveen."

At the printer, the growth colleague glanced over, saw the shoulder, the smile, the performance of care, and looked back at his papers with the mild envy of a person who had not been patted. Appearance filed. Content unread. Meera, far across the floor, might have seen the same silhouette through glass and desks: two men, one hand, a conversation that looked like mentorship from ten metres away.

Vikram squeezed once, released, and turned.

His back crossed the carpet, passed through the glass door, became a shape behind glare and slides. The cabin sealed with a soft corporate click. Naveen stood with his phone still open to talking points he had not used, the shoulder warm where a hand had been, the warmth already leaving.

Same cadence.

2021. Night. Vikram's old car smelling of coffee and new plastic, the city sliding past in streaks. Streetlight over the dashboard. Naveen still high on a pilot that had nearly failed and then hadn't. Vikram's voice easy, intimate, the voice of a founder before the vocabulary of decks. *You're early team. When we raise, we take care of our people. I'll make sure you're looked after.* Hand on shoulder then too. Same pressure. Same brightness. Same *after* tucked inside the *yes*, so neatly you could miss it if you wanted the yes enough.

Series A had come in 2023. Parties. Photos. A cake with the company logo slightly wrong. The paper had not come. Naveen had told himself timing. Timing had stretched until it filled the corridor.

He closed the notes app without saving anything new. There was nothing new to save. Somewhere a Slack notification pinged from a pocket that was not his. His own shoulder cooled by degrees he could measure only because he was standing still enough to notice.

He walked back toward the open floor carrying nothing he could show on a calendar. Only the fading print of a hand.

---

At his desk the tickets refused to become English.

Naveen opened one about a latency spike. Closed it. Opened Slack. Closed Slack. The corridor replayed: Vikram's angle of departure, the colleague's glance, the glass door sealing. Eleven-forty. Eleven-fifty. He drank water from a bottle that tasted of plastic and office. His notes app sat unused under other apps, a fossil of preparation.

People moved around him with the noon hunger of open plan. Someone microwaved something that smelled of sambar. Someone laughed too loudly into a headset. Ordinary Thursday. His body had not received the memo.

Meera pulled one headphone off when he stopped beside her monitor. Her screen showed a sprint board in mid-argument with itself, red tickets, a dependency named after a person who was on leave. She looked tired in the precise way of people who had slept and still owed the night something.

"Lunch?" he said.

She glanced at the clock, at him, at the hallway that led past Vikram's glass. Whatever she had seen from her desk that morning sat behind her eyes without announcing itself. "You look like a person who needs coffee more than food."

"Both. Neither. Come."

"Five minutes. Let me park this comment so it doesn't look like I vanished mid-war."

He waited. She typed three lines with the economy of someone who had learned that long comments were where blame hid. Then she stood, took her phone, left the headphones on the desk like a promise to return to a different kind of noise.

They took the stairs because the lift meant standing still with other bodies and the risk of Vikram's voice arriving bright from a different floor. Down one level the café breathed its noon crowd. Laptops, salads in plastic, other people's deadlines braided low. They ordered without ceremony, the way they had ordered since the month they both started, when this table by the window had been theirs before it belonged to anyone. Two coffees. No food. Naveen almost added a sandwich and didn't. Appetite was elsewhere.

The table came free. Cups arrived with a soft ceramic knock. Steam lifted, thinned, began to fail.

Naveen wrapped his hands around his cup and did not drink. Meera slid his saucer a centimetre toward him without looking, the way she always did when his elbows wandered. A habit from their first year, when standups ran long and lunch was the only place a sentence could finish.

"Had the chat with Vikram," he said, keeping it light, keeping it like a status update you could put in a standup. "The equity thing."

Meera blew once across her coffee and waited. She had always been good at waiting without filling.

"He said absolutely. Let's lock it. After the board deck next week."

She tilted her head the way she did when a requirement sounded complete and wasn't. "Did he say no?"

The question landed exactly where he had hoped it would not. Clean. Practical. A blade made of common sense.

"No," Naveen said. "But—"

He stopped. The *but* had nowhere factual to go. Vikram had not refused. Vikram had affirmed. Vikram had called him early team with the warmth of a man who believed his own myth in real time. The wound was the shoulder used as punctuation. Saying that aloud sounded like a person inventing injury for sport.

Meera watched him. Not unkind. "Then what happened exactly?"

Naveen looked at the window glare, at a scooter flashing past outside with a delivery bag strapped on, at the two cups between them still full, steam now only a rumour.

"He said the same thing in 2021," he said, and his voice dropped without his deciding it. "Car. Same words. Same—" He touched his own shoulder once, a small stupid gesture, then let his hand fall to the table. "Same hand. *We'll lock it. You're early team.* Then nothing on paper."

Meera's face changed. Not much. A shift around the eyes, the mouth holding still as if a laugh or a curse had been considered and rejected. She had seen the hallway from her desk; now she had the rhyme.

Neither of them said *he's lying*. The word was too clean for what had happened. 

Silence held between them. Spoons. A laugh from the next table about someone's wedding. The espresso machine's short violence. Their coffees cooled.

Meera's phone lit on the table beside her wrist. A WhatsApp preview, apartment group, Asha's name half-visible in the stack, the blue of a world that enforced itself with screenshots. She dismissed it with one thumb, no comment, eyes returning to Naveen. He saw it. She did not offer it. He did not ask. Same silence, untranslated.

"I don't know what you do with that," she said finally. Not advice. An admission of limit, offered without apology.

"Neither do I."

She held his gaze.

He almost said *maybe I'm overreading*. He did not. She had already seen his face when he named 2021. 

Meera turned her cup a quarter-turn on the saucer. "Next week," she said, testing the phrase the way a loose tooth asks to be tested.

They had a name for it between them, never written down, from the month Vikram first used the phrase and Meera had looked at Naveen across this same table and said, without smiling, *There it is. Next week.*

"Next week," Naveen agreed, and neither of them smiled.

A tray clattered somewhere behind the counter. Someone called a name that was not theirs. The world continued its Thursday with insulting competence.

"Next week," still in the air between them. The cups stayed full.

# Chapter 3 — Ticket PDF

Meera set her bag on the chair by the door, the same chair that had held Divya's spare key two nights ago, and kicked off her slippers. The flat smelled faintly of the morning's coffee and of rooms that had held heat all day without complaint. Fan on high, blades chopping the air into motion that was not coolness. Through the window, Bengaluru's Friday evening was a continuous hush of tyres and someone's television laughter a floor above. On the sill, a basil plant she had bought after a bad sprint and forgotten to water properly sat brown at the edges, still trying.

Her phone lay face-up on the counter where she had dropped it when she came in.

Three missed calls. Amma. Timestamps marching through the afternoon like a complaint filed in triplicate. 2:14, during the architecture review she could not leave. 4:02, during the one-on-one where she had nodded at problems that were not hers. 6:41, watched arrive on the desk during standup, blue and insistent, while she let it die into voicemail with the cowardice of people who loved their mothers and still needed ten minutes of unmarked air.

Week nearly done. Nothing in the fridge worth inventing into dinner. She filled a glass from the filter and drank standing up, performing the evening, hoping missed calls might pass if she did not look.

She put the phone face-down.

Washed her hands. The apartment group had been quieter today, Asha on generator hours, polite thumbs-ups, and Meera had scrolled past with the thin pride of surviving Wednesday's parking slot. Naveen's face at the café sat behind her ribs without asking to be unpacked. Manageable.

The phone buzzed against laminate.

Fourth. Or a message that would become a fourth if ignored. The counter transmitted the vibration into her hip where she leaned.

Meera turned it over, saw Amma, exhaled through her nose, and called back before her body could invent another chore.

Saroja answered on the first ring.

"Finally," Saroja said, not unkind, already moving at logistics speed. "I thought maybe you fell into one of those office holes and forgot you have a family. Are you home? Eat something. Don't tell me you're skipping meals again."

"I'm fine, Amma."

"Fine is what you say when the rice is finished and you're drinking water for dinner. Listen. Ananya's things are moving fast. Pallavi is asking about who is coming from our side, as if I'm a booking agent. Lakshmi keeps saying your name like I'm hiding you in a cupboard. When are you coming?"

Meera leaned her hip harder against the counter. The phone warmed against her ear. Fan blades strobed the wall with soft, repeating shadow. Outside, a scooter braked too late and someone swore without heat.

"I'll look at dates this weekend, Amma."

"This weekend." Saroja repeated it the way she repeated recipes that had gone wrong. Not shouting, correcting. "Putti, the wedding is not a cloud. It has a date. Before the fifth if you can — better fourth evening. Flowers. Chikmagalur cousins. Coffee every twenty minutes. Ananya is expecting you. You are the one from our—"

"Amma."

"—side. Don't Amma me through the important part. Ananya is expecting you. She asked twice. Once in the morning, once when they were measuring for the entrance thing. Lakshmi asked this morning, *Is Meera coming or only sending parcel?* As if my daughter is a courier service. I told her you're coming. Don't make me a liar in my own mother's house."

Ananya would notice absence. Lakshmi would notice with the quiet accuracy of women who counted bodies without counting aloud. Meera's father would not come, that fact had hardened years ago, and so the duty of being seen fell where it always fell.

She stared at the stove clock. 8:41. Numbers indifferent. A magnet held a takeaway menu she never used.

"I have a project mid-stretch," she said. "If I leave too early, the release—"

"Everyone has a project. The wedding does not wait for Jira." Saroja's voice climbed half a note, then softened. "Come. Be seen. Help your cousin. Ramesh-mama is in a mood — land. Don't ask me. House needs hands who can smile and not argue. You were always good at that."

Ramesh-mama. Impossible. Land thing. Meera let it pass the way she let most village weather pass when she was in Malleshwaram with a fan and a glass of water: noted, unopened, filed under Uncles And Weddings. She said, "Mm," and meant nothing by it except that she had heard a noise shaped like information and would not chase it down a phone line on a Friday.

"Before the fifth," Saroja pressed. "Book something. Don't do that thing where you look and look and then the tickets are three thousand and you blame the universe, which is the same as blaming me when I'm the one calling."

"I don't blame you for ticket prices."

"You blame timing. Timing is also me, apparently." A breath, shorter. Then warmer, the voice that still undid her when she was not careful: "Just come, putti. Lakshmi will make the lemon rice you pretend you don't miss. Sleep in your old room. Let the house see your face before all the strangers arrive."

Meera's mouth found the sentence before her plans finished their committee meeting.

"I'll book tonight, Amma."

Silence for half a second. Surprise, or satisfaction disguised as it.

"Good," Saroja said, already shifting to the next cell. "Send me the screenshot. Not tomorrow morning when you've talked yourself into Monday. Tonight. And tell that office of yours you're going for family. They can survive without you for a few days. The world survived before product managers."

"Okay."

"Okay is not a screenshot."

"I'll send it."

"Love you. Eat something that is not water. You sound like fluorescent lights."

"Love you, Amma."

The call ended with Saroja's small click, the sound of a person who had gotten what she needed and still had three other calls to make. Meera lowered the phone.

Her own mouth, offering *tonight* like a gift. She set the phone on the counter face-up this time, as if honesty of position could substitute for the booking she had not yet made.

The stove clock read 8:58.

---

By 9:30 she had moved the problem to the bed, as if geography could make obligation softer.

Laptop open on a pillow that still held the dent of last night's poor sleep. Kurta exchanged for an old T-shirt. Fan on high, its round shadow crossing the wall in steady arcs. Phone dark beside her knee, screen asleep, Saroja's satisfaction temporarily fed. The bedroom held the day's heat in the mattress and the curtains. Laptop fans whirred; the machine added its own small summer to her thighs.

The booking site loaded with cheerful colours and a stock photo of a bus that looked cleaner than any bus she had taken. She searched the route she knew by habit more than longing: Bengaluru to the belt of towns that thinned into autos, cousin-voices, jasmine in plastic bags, the house where childhood had smelled of rain on red earth and Lakshmi's kitchen.

Options appeared. Dates in neat rows. Seat maps. Prices that pretended to be invitations.

She scanned for something that suited her, midweek later, after the project spit out its current fire, after the release window stopped punishing absences with looks. A Tuesday evening. A Wednesday with buffer and dignity. Her finger hovered. Optimism, brief as AC during load-shedding: maybe family math would bend if she arrived smiling hard enough on a later bus.

Then Saroja's constraint returned, exact as a calendar invite she had not accepted: *before the fifth if you can — better fourth evening.*

The dates that suited Meera arrived too late for being seen, for helping with flowers and Chikmagalur cousins and coffee every twenty minutes, for not becoming the relative who sent a parcel and a carefully worded excuse. She ran the work week in her head without opening the work calendar, mid-project, dependencies named after people, the tightness in her manager's jaw when personal life became an out-of-office. Double bind. No date wore both hats without tearing a seam.

She typed Saturday, 6 April.

The seat map bloomed. A price sat there, not yet cruel, the cruelty of interstate travel. She imagined the bus: evening departure, AC that would fail near Maddur, a seatmate with snacks, village air at night smelling of something green and burnt. Saroja fussing on the road before Meera could arrange her face. Lakshmi's lemon rice waiting. She erased the date, backspace clicking soft in the hot room.

Tried Sunday the seventh, morning bus. Too late for the smile-and-not-argue window. Saroja would call from the kitchen with hurt sharpened into practicality. Ananya would have already started missing her in the pointed way of brides who counted faces. Lakshmi would say nothing and mean everything. Erased.

Typed 6 April again. Selected a seat near the front because she hated the smell of the toilet at the back. Hovered over confirm. Read the small print about changes and cancellations. Her finger lifted. She erased a second time, slower, as if delay were a conversation she was still allowed to have with herself.

When she searched again, same route, same Saturday, the price had risen by a little. Not a catastrophe. Enough to feel like a comment. Time punishing the luxury of hesitation.

Third time.

Saturday 6 April. Evening bus. Arrival that would put her on the house road after dark, bag strap cutting her shoulder, dogs barking at a stranger who was not a stranger. Her finger completed the confirm before her pride finished its minority report. A spinner. A pause in which the fan seemed louder. Then green checkmarks. Thank you for your booking.

Her email pinged soft in the hot room.

Confirmation. PDF attached. She opened it with the caution of someone opening a verdict.

Her name in clean letters. The date. Seat number. Boarding point. The route spelled out like a sentence she could not edit in Google Docs. And there, in the tidy typography of companies that had learned how to end negotiations: **non-refundable**.

She thought, briefly, of lemon rice, and of Ramesh-mama in a mood about land, and of Ananya measuring entrances, and then she stopped thinking in lists.

She stared until the screen's glow felt like a second climate on her face. Phone dark beside the laptop, no missed calls left to hide behind, no fourth buzz to postpone the irreversible.

He did not take notes on family politics. Notes became files; files became stories that travelled. The boy's hands twisted each other as if wringing a cloth that would not dry. A tear escaped and was shamed away with a knuckle. Suresh made the small doctor-sounds that meant presence without promise. He could not fix this with a stethoscope. He also could not say *leave*.

Thirty seconds alone. Stethoscope off. The next patient already a shadow on frosted glass.

He picked up his phone.

Paused.

Thumb hovering. Scooter horn outside. Attendant sweeping. Then the screen woke. He typed a short message — a sentence that moved a household clock without moving history. Sent. He did not watch for the tick that meant read.

# Chapter 9 — Biscuit Tin

The elders were already arguing.

Not shouting. Worse. Meeting-register voices, self-important, clause-citing. Three of them at the table, one standing with a notebook, another seated like a judge. A Marigowda-side representative Meera did not know, not Venki, polite in a new shirt.

Saroja found them chairs at the edge. Meera sat where she was told and let the heat find her under the neem.

Ramesh was already there.

Stiff in a dhoti that looked pressed against his will. He sat for perhaps three minutes without speaking, without looking at the Marigowda rep, without acknowledging the banner sketch on the back of a wedding invitation envelope. He stood. Left. The plastic chair scraped once, and he was gone down the temple steps before anyone could assign him a task.

Meera watched him go. She did not comment. Wrong venue for uncles and paths.

The meeting settled into logistics first: tent size, catering, the shared hall. Saroja interjected where lists required it: *What about the carpet? Who's paying for the extra tent?* Her notebook appeared, pen ready. Meera let the practicalities wash over her. Chairs. Vessels. Families in the same space, barely speaking.

Then the banner.

Whose name went first.

The Marigowda rep spoke with careful politeness. Groom's side tradition, he said. Equal respect, naturally, but the groom's family name carried the, he searched for a word that would not sound like war, *sequence*. The printer needed to know. The invitation needed to know. People would read it. People always read.

The Hiregowda-adjacent elder, not Ramesh, someone with the confidence of a man who had served on committees before committees were fashionable, countered without raising his voice. Host-side. Bride's family. Their village. Their temple hall. The bride's people had opened their doors; the banner should acknowledge that first. Tradition was a flexible word when status was at stake.

Something shifted behind Meera's eyes.

This was about font size.

The absurdity was so precise it hurt. Two families who could not pass each other at a gate without weather, who had failed a meeting on a veranda with untouched cups, now fighting over typography. Whose name sat above whose.

One elder suggested compromise: both names at equal size. Side by side. Democracy in Devanagari.

Another elder, older, slower, with the timing of a man who had waited his whole committee life for this sentence, shook his head.

"Equal size means nothing if one is on top."

The veranda went quiet for half a breath.

Meera's mouth twitched. She turned it into a cough. Saroja glanced at her, *behave*, and returned to scribbling — but not before her pen paused mid-air, caught by the same sentence. Adults in plastic chairs under a neem tree, fighting over vertical placement while the sun moved across cement.

The chairs rearranged.

Not dramatically, no overturning, no walkouts. Just men standing, shifting, sitting in new configurations as if physical geometry could solve a problem that lived in pride. Scraping on cement. The third rearrangement of the morning. Someone moved to get better shade. Someone else moved to claim the chair that implied seniority. The pen passed again. The banner sketch acquired arrows and question marks.

A separate argument flickered at the edge, whether the wedding function or the boundary-blessing request took precedence on the calendar. Different family entirely. Meera caught fragments: *survey mark*, *temple trust*, *next month*. The committee members loved it. Petty governance as sport. Allocation as identity.

Meera stood when Saroja stood. The neem rustled. A temple bell somewhere, distant, indifferent.

Plastic chairs scraped on cement. Saroja furiously scribbling notes she would enforce tomorrow.

The road back from the temple was all sun.

Meera walked beside Saroja through heat that had decided ten o'clock was still morning and therefore forgivable. Wedding prep lists lived in Saroja's bag and in her mouth, flowers, vessels, garland strings that needed counting before the afternoon, chair covers that someone in Bengaluru would call unnecessary and someone in this lane would call essential. Meera let the logistics flow past like radio in another language. Her skin remembered shade. Her mouth remembered almost laughing. They turned in.

Darkness after sun. Narrow interior, shelves along one wall, soap, oil, spices, buckets, biscuits. Dust on glass where sunlight made a beam-pattern on worn labels. Smell mixed: sweet, dusty, oily.

Farooq was behind the counter.

Middle-aged, beard trimmed, shirt sleeves rolled to elbows that knew work, grin already in place as if he had been waiting for entertainment and Saroja's list was the opening act. His hands moved before his mouth finished greeting, reaching, weighing, the muscle memory of a man who could pack rice and joke simultaneously without spilling either. A biscuit tin sat open on the counter, lid pushed aside, not ceremonially, just always open, the way some doors in villages stayed unlatched because closing them would suggest strangers.

"Saroja-avve," he said. "The list. Let me guess, longer than yesterday."

"Heard." Farooq began pulling items from shelves with the speed of a man who knew where everything lived without looking, which was most of the time, except when he looked anyway out of habit. "Banner fight?"

"Don't start."

"Who reads banners?" Farooq said, packing rice into a bag with one hand, the other already reaching for the next item on Saroja's list. "Only the printers."

Meera laughed before she could decide not to.

The sound surprised her, genuine, unperformed, escaping the observer posture she had been maintaining since the temple veranda. Farooq looked up. Not startled. Interested. His grin widened by a millimetre.

"City girl laughs at our fights, ah?"

The label landed without hostility. *City girl*. Invited, not cornered. The shop was neutral ground; Farooq owed tabs to both families, supplied both with soap and rice. His loyalty was transaction.

"I will keep everything," Saroja said. "That's the problem."

Farooq slid the biscuit tin toward her without ceremony.

"Take."

She reached across the counter. Dry biscuit, sweet, slightly warm where the tin had sat in a sun-patch near the door, the warmth of counter-level hospitality, not oven warmth, the warmth of things offered openly. She ate it.

Sunlight found a jar of pickles and turned it amber. Dust motes in the beam. Street sounds filtered through, a child, a horn far away, the village continuing.

They paid. They left. The sun hit again immediately.

Saroja was already on the next list before the lane widened — banner names still pending, and tomorrow she would enforce whatever the printer had not yet decided. Behind them on the counter: the biscuit tin lid, open. Farooq's grin held. Meera's mouth still held the sentence she was not sure she was allowed to find funny tomorrow.

# Chapter 10 — Broken Post

Ramesh left before the stove finished its argument with Saroja.

Chappals on the washed step. Dhoti and shirt, morning clothes, field clothes, the uniform of a man who checked his land before the house checked him. Walking stick in the right hand because the left knee remembered last year's harvest and liked to announce itself in the cool hour before heat claimed the morning, even when the morning was not cool, only dry, only April, only the hour when dawn pretended gentleness and then betrayed you by eight.

Saroja's voice from the kitchen, something about tiffin, something about Raju already gone ahead, something he did not answer because answers before patrol were invitations to delay. Meera still asleep. Ananya's wedding lists on the table like a second crop requiring harvest. Ten days. The number sat in his chest without changing what his feet did.

Hot wind already moving across the fields. Not cooling. Just moving. Carrying dust and the pre-monsoon pressure that made everything feel charged and unfinished, sky pale, mist burned off. Birds worked the margins. A distant pump coughed and failed and coughed again. Somewhere a dog barked once and thought better of it. His field gate opened with the squeal he had been meaning to fix since last monsoon.

The path between fields.

Red earth under chappals, packed hard by decades of feet and cart wheels. To his right, Hiregowda cane, his cane, stood in rows that would need water soon. To his left, beyond the wire, Marigowda land began with the same crop and a different accounting of pride. Between them, the path: narrow enough to keep a man honest, or it had been, until the wire moved.

Since Mandya. Since fresh cement. Since the morning he had pressed his palm against cool grey and felt inheritance narrowing by six inches, eight.

His farmworker was ahead on the path. Raju, young, obedient, following instructions Ramesh had given without fully examining them: use the path demonstratively, rake it, carry loads through it, assert presence. Make the Hiregowda side visible in the dirt. The kind of assignment elders gave young men when words had failed and pride needed a body to carry it. Raju had a rake over one shoulder. A bundle of cuttings in the other hand. He walked with the deliberate pace of someone performing use, unaware, or aware in the way workers were aware without saying, that performance on contested ground was its own risk.

Ramesh watched Raju's back. Guilt sat beside vigilance. He had sent the man into this.

Since the wire, these walks had become patrols. Since the tea meeting, waiting. He checked the wire each morning the way other men checked crop, expecting fresh insult and hoping not to find it. Wedding in ten days. The field was older than alliance.

Movement at the far end of the path.

Figures approaching from the Marigowda side. Two, one young, walking fast, anger in the set of shoulders and the speed of feet; one older, behind, slower, trying to moderate with pace alone, failing because youth moved faster than caution.

Ramesh's vigilance sharpened.

Prasad.

He knew the boy by sight, Venki's side, twenty-two or thereabouts, jeans and shirt even at this hour, hotheaded. Sent, or self-sent, to be present on the path the way Raju had been sent. Proxy warriors.

The narrow section.

Where the wire had moved, six inches, eight, the path compressed until two men could not pass without one yielding. The throat of the dispute. Raju had reached it first, rake still on shoulder, and had not stopped, following instructions, asserting use, making the Hiregowda side visible in dirt the way Ramesh had asked without examining what asking meant. Prasad came from the other end with speed that was its own argument.

They converged. Neither slowed. Neither looked at the ground for a place to step aside. Raju's bundle shifted. Prasad's jaw set. Bodies blocking bodies before words arrived.

"This is our side."

"All sides are for walking."

Brief. Hot. The language of ownership deployed like a weapon neither man had invented, echoing veranda arguments, echoing failed meetings, echoing everything that had not worked. Ramesh's stick struck earth. He moved faster than his knee preferred. Fifty metres. Forty. Thirty. Twenty. His voice carried before his body could reach them, authority attempting to arrive before contact.

"Leave it!"

Too late.

Prasad's hands flat on Raju's chest. One shove. One motion. Harder than intention, or as hard as the anger required, Ramesh would never know which, and it would not matter to the file or the wedding or the bruise forming on Raju's shoulder. Raju's feet lost the earth they expected. His heel caught the uneven ground where the wire's move had narrowed the path. His shoulder found the fence post at the path's edge, old wood, weathered, leaning slightly from years of use and weather and the leaning of men who had argued here before, and the post took his momentum and snapped at the base with a sound like bone giving way.

Splinter. Crack. The worker went down hard, shoulder first, then hip, then the side of his face finding red earth that did not care about sides or weddings or generations.

Dust kicked up from the impact, red earth, dry, fine, rising in a small cloud that caught the early light and then fell back. Settled on Raju's shirt. On Prasad's frozen hands. On the splintered wood.

Three seconds of absolute silence.

Ramesh stopped running ten metres away. The world narrowed to red earth and a man on the ground and a post at a wrong angle and blood, not much, scrape not wound, on Raju's cheek where dirt had found skin. His own breath loud in his ears. Hot wind continuing, indifferent. Birds pausing, then resuming. The rake lay where Raju had dropped it, tines in dirt, handle pointing at the sky like an accusation.

Prasad stood frozen.

Terror at his own hands. The boy had not meant, or had meant and was now horrified by the meaning made flesh. A young man who had pushed too hard and was watching the consequence arrive in someone else's body, in wood splintered at the base.

The older Marigowda farmworker reached Prasad. Hands on his arm. Pulling back. Damage control without apology, instinctive, the movement of a man who knew this had crossed a line and whose first thought was retreat before witness became record. He murmured something Ramesh would never hear, *enough*, or *come*, or a name, and Prasad moved at last, feet finding obedience, body leaving while mind still stood in the three seconds of silence.

Ramesh crouched beside Raju.

Hand on the shoulder, gentle, protective, too late. The shoulder would bruise deep. The face was scraped, blood drying in a line from temple to jaw, dirt embedded where skin had met earth. Not critical. No hospital. Visible. Painful. The kind of injury that would be seen and asked about and would require a story the families were not ready to tell.

"Anna—" Raju started.

"Quiet. Can you stand?"

Ramesh helped him up. Raju's weight leaned into him, young body suddenly heavy with pain and shock, breath coming hard through the nose, one hand reaching for the hurt shoulder and stopping short. The broken post lay at an angle, base splintered, wood pale where it had cracked, wire sagging where the post had held it.

Prasad was gone. Pulled away by the older man, figures retreating toward Marigowda land, dust settling on their departure. The older man did not look back. Did not apologize.

Ramesh stood with his worker on the path. Broken fence post at an angle. Ramesh's hand on Raju's shoulder. Dust settling.

---

They walked slowly.

Ramesh took Raju's weight on his left side, matching the limp that had begun in the bruised shoulder, each step a small negotiation between pride and pain. Path to road. Road to compound gate. The morning had become fully hot while they were on the ground; heat found the scrape on Raju's face and made it sting. No one spoke. The house appeared ahead, courtyard wall, gate, tulsi pot, the shape of a home that would soon become a witness.

"Anna, leave it."

Raju said it quietly. Stoic. Definitive. One sentence, complete. 

Ramesh did not respond. He kept walking. The words sat in him like stones.

The courtyard. Saroja at the door, she had been somewhere inside, heard the slow footsteps, come to look. Her face changed in sequence: recognition, alarm, calculation. The wedding arriving in her eyes before the injury fully registered. She gasped. Hand to mouth.

"Don't go to police before the wedding. Please."

One plea. Quiet. Desperate. Not a speech about family honour, just the immediate arithmetic of alliance and ceremony and ten days that could not survive a station visit. She looked at Raju's face, blood drying, cheek scraped, and her hand went from her mouth to her heart to Ramesh's arm.

Ramesh did not answer.

He guided Raju inside. Seated him on the low stool in the front room where workers were sometimes given tea after a long day, the same room where alliances were discussed over cups and where violence was not supposed to arrive before breakfast. Saroja followed, already calling for turmeric, cloth, ice if they had it, the home remedies that were the first line of defence when clinics and stations lived in a different category of problem.

A neighbour woman appeared, summoned by voice or instinct, the village's secondary medical system, and began applying yellow paste to Raju's scraped cheek with the brisk tenderness of someone who had treated worse and would treat worse again and did not need the story to do the work.

Meera was in the corridor.

Ramesh saw her peripherally, cotton from yesterday's errands, confusion on her face, the posture of a woman who had heard the commotion and stopped at a distance because the room told her this was not her question to ask. She saw Raju's face. The bruise forming on the shoulder visible where his shirt had been pulled aside for the paste. She filed it. She did not approach. She did not speak.

Ramesh sat in the chair across from Raju and waited for his hands to stop shaking.

Time passed. The house continued around the crisis, vessels in the kitchen, voices lowered in the way voices lowered when children might hear, someone bringing water in a steel tumbler, Saroja hovering with the restless energy of a woman who could fix garland strings and seating charts but could not fix this. The neighbour finished the paste and murmured something about rest. Raju nodded. Stoic. Already returning to the economy of *leave it*.

Ramesh's phone sat on the table where he had left it that morning, dark screen, silent, the object that should have rung by now if the other side understood what had happened.

He looked at it. Waited.

Venki. An apology. An explanation. Someone from the Marigowda side acknowledging that a line had been crossed.

The phone did not ring.

No call came.

Minutes accumulated. The morning moved toward mid-morning. Saroja brought tea Ramesh did not drink. Someone mentioned breakfast and the mention died in the room. Raju sat with turmeric on his face and pain in his shoulder, the dignity of a man who would not ask for compensation.

Ramesh looked at the broken fence post, in his mind or leaning against the courtyard wall, he could not tell which anymore. Wood at a wrong angle, base splintered, the thing he had touched that dawn now destroyed.

Broken fence post. The crack that could not be un-cracked.

# Chapter 11 — Notebook Arrows

Hurt and mischief. Two named parties. A wedding in nine days that both families had mentioned as if the calendar itself were evidence.

Inspector Priya Naik read the FIR again. Ramesh Hiregowda: complainant. Prasad Marigowda: accused. What the paper could not capture was everything underneath — the old fight, the new incident, the wedding pressure.

Something tightened in her chest. Private. Not for the file.

Three years ago, neighbouring taluk. A boundary complaint closed too fast. Someone had been hurt later. She would not close this one for convenience.

Ramesh arrived at nine.

Dhoti and shirt, formal in the way village men became formal for stations. Stiff posture, eyes forward, chappals cleaned, the body language of a man who needed a record but did not want trouble. He sat on the bench when she indicated. Accepted tea when the constable brought it and held the cup without drinking, as if warmth might soften something that needed to stay hard.

Statement-taking began.

His version: unprovoked shove. His worker innocent, on the path as he had always been, doing ordinary work. Raking, carrying, the labour of a field that did not pause for family disputes. Marigowda-side youth aggressive, hotheaded, pushing without cause. Injury visible. Post broken. Property damage. He described the morning. Dawn walk, worker ahead, confrontation at the narrow section, arrival too late. He spoke carefully. Edited. Not lying. Selecting. The way people selected when dignity was at stake and the bench opposite a desk was a performance space.

"When was the wire moved?" Priya asked.

"It was always more to our side."

Always. The word that meant *no document*.

She wrote. Pen on paper. Scratch, pause, scratch. Asked about witnesses. The older Marigowda man had been present but would be interviewed separately with Prasad. No neutral third party on the path at contact. Asked about the worker's condition. Would he need hospital? No. Would he work tomorrow? Probably not. About whether this had happened before. Words, postures, the path becoming a stage for families who could not stop performing ownership.

Ramesh mentioned generations. The path. The use. The families. The way things had been before papers were required for truth. Something in Priya's chest tightened again.

She noted it. Did not comment.

Ramesh left. Stiff. Satisfied with documentation, unsatisfied with everything else. The door closed behind him with the soft finality of a man who had said what he came to say and would say it again if required.

Twenty minutes. Then Prasad arrived with an elder relative.

Different energy in the room. The same procedure.

Priya reset her face to professional. Same questions. Same pen. Same bench.

Prasad's version: verbal provocation first. Hiregowda worker blocking the path, aggressive words, *our side*, the posture of men looking for fight. Stumble, not shove. The post was already loose. Anyone could see, the wood was old, the base was weak. Accident. Exaggeration. He spoke with the coached carefulness of youth who had been told what to say and was trying to believe it while his hands betrayed him. His eyes found the floor. The elder relative's hand rested on his knee. Not comforting. Anchoring.

The elder relative added, when Priya looked up from her notes: "Settled, madam. Common path."

Common. Settled. Words that meant *no paper*.

Priya pressed. Survey marks? Written agreement? Revenue records covering this specific path usage? A board? A decree? Anything that could translate *generations* into a number a file could hold?

Prasad shook his head. The elder relative spread his hands. A gesture of openness that was also a gesture of emptiness. No document. No board. No survey stone within shouting distance. The path existed in use, in memory, in the feet of both families' workers and elders and children walking to fields for longer than this station had stood.

Both sides, in separate statements, separate bodies, separate edits, had looked at her with the expression of people who had been living inside an arrangement so long they had forgotten it was invisible to outsiders. Shared use, informal, made by feet over decades, maintained by consensus and argument and the occasional shove that became an FIR.

Neither side was fully lying. The pauses before *always*, the careful noun choices, the way *common* and *settled* arrived like synonyms for *trust me, madam, we know what we know*.

She dismissed them with the standard warning: no further incidents. The words felt thin in her mouth. Both families mentioned the wedding again on their way out. As context, as plea, as the clock that made everything urgent and nothing simple. Nine days. As if nine days were a legal category. The elder relative nodded too much. Prasad did not look at her on the way to the door.

She opened her notebook. Not the FIR, her notebook, the one where problems became diagrams before they became files, the one that would return in a closure she could not yet see. She drew arrows.

Hiregowda claim. Arrow. Marigowda claim. Arrow. Path. Arrow. Wire. Arrow. Wedding date. Arrow. Broken post. Arrow. Injury. Arrow.

Each line pointing toward a centre that had no label. She tried to connect them. The lines refused to meet. Wedding date sat in the margin. Nine days. A deadline that made every arrow feel heavier without making any arrow clearer.

She was trained to need paper. She had none.

The pen hovered over the empty centre. A sweat drop fell from her temple onto the page and bloomed slightly before she blotted it with her thumb. Yellowish paper, blue ink, the small ruin of heat on evidence. Outside, the compound glare continued. A vehicle passed. The fan continued losing the fight against the heat.

The jeep took them to the path at half past twelve.

Priya sat in the front seat with her notebook on her knee, the constable driving with the familiarity of a man who knew every lane in the taluk and which lanes required slowing for chickens. PSI wanted a report by evening. The file needed ground truth. Ground truth, she was learning again, was not the same as truth. It was what you could write down after walking on red earth in uniform while people watched from windows.

They parked where the lane met the fields. Priya stepped out into full sun. No shade on the path, no mercy, April establishing its daily argument with skin. Hot wind on exposed ground. Dry earth smell. Pre-monsoon pressure in the air, the sense of rain considering arrival and choosing delay.

The path was empty.

Both sides avoiding during police visibility. Self-preserving, the wisdom of families who knew that being seen near a dispute during inspection could become interpretation. Priya could feel watching without always seeing watchers. Distant windows. Field edges. The social surveillance of a village performing innocence for the state.

She walked with the constable behind her.

The wire fence first. Shifted inward, visible even to her untrained eye. The compression of walking space. New post among old ones, pale wood among dark, the geography of a boundary moved while an owner was in Mandya buying hardware he did not imagine would be used against him. She noted it. Measured the narrow section with her steps. Three paces where four had been, or something like that, imprecise because her training was in statements not surveying.

The broken post.

Angled. Base splintered. Still in situ. Evidence undisturbed, wood pale at the crack, wire sagging where support had been. She crouched, uniform creasing, knee complaining, and looked at the base. Fresh splintering. Not old rot pretending to be accident. Violence made wood.

She stood. Looked in both directions along the path's length. Red earth packed by feet. Fields on both sides. Cane, rows, the agriculture of a dispute that was not about agriculture.

Survey markers?

She walked fifty metres one way. Nothing. Fifty metres the other. Nothing. No revenue stone. No board with names. No decree carved anywhere. No official geography to ground the file in.

Fields on both sides. Cane rows. A channel opening clogged with silt. Hot wind in her face. Dust on her boots. The constable followed, patient, local, carrying the knowledge of a place she could not put in the FIR.

"Madam," he said. "Both families have used this since my father's time."

One sentence. Human. Not analysis.

Priya wrote in her book: *Shared use, no exclusive documentation, dispute likely pre-existing.*

She added the date. Added her initials. Added the constable's name as witness to the visit. The file would grow. The file would not resolve. She would monitor. She would warn.

Pen hovered. Notebook still open on her knee. Nine days to the wedding. Both families' distant windows watching her leave — and the centre of her diagram still blank.

# Chapter 12 — Aligned Sandals

Friday evening. Wedding eve.

The week between police and wedding eve had been a cold war dressed as preparation.

Thursday: complaint filed; Meera helping Saroja with garland strings, Ramesh returning tight-lipped from the station, path topic avoided at dinner. Friday: vendor lists, chair counts, muhurtham timing, both families polite-cold in shared spaces. Saturday: errands in town, flowers and sweets, the path visible from the road, empty, wire still up. Wednesday: seating chart arguments resolved by Saroja's notebook. Thursday: last full prep day, Ananya's wedding-night room packed, tension everywhere but no new incident. The Hiregowda courtyard had been transformed. Rice. Sambar. Sweet. Pickle. Fried items in newspaper cones. A feast laid out to say *we are fine* louder than any speech. Women in the kitchen still bringing replacements for emptied vessels. Meera circulated. Festive churidar. Saroja had insisted, *not office clothes, not black, not mourning colours, we are not mourning, we are marrying*. Helping with serving, exchanging pleasantries she had learned to deliver without meaning all of them. *Congratulations. Looking beautiful. So happy for you.* The phrases of wedding-eve fluency, spoken into a room that needed fluency to keep from examining what lay underneath.

The path-violence sat in her memory like a bruise on Raju's face she had not been given the story for. Ramesh had come back from the station twice and returned each time tighter, the way men returned from places where paper recorded what bodies had done. Families strained. Tonight was truce. Fragile, necessary, peace that existed because tomorrow required it and because both sides had invested too much rice and garland string to detonate now.

# Chapter 28 — Two Pages Signed

She stood on the veranda a moment longer than necessary. Standing where the guest had sat with his tea and his bad knee and his admitted doubt, where the courtyard had held speeches and silences and the life of a house that had become, improbably, hers to leave.

The tulsi pot dripped. Rain collected in the courtyard's low corner, first puddle of the season sitting like a small promise. She had walked this veranda dozens of times these weeks. Serving, watching, failing to mediate, learning the cost of presence. The geometry of the house was in her body now. Gate. Lane. Neem at the corner she could not see from here but knew was there.

She picked up her bag.

The house behind her. Courtyard, kitchen, corridor, guest room with its folded blankets waiting. Everything she had arrived not-belonging-to now holding her differently.

Rain specks on her arms at the veranda edge. Bag strap cutting familiar weight. She walked down the lane toward the bus stand, not running, not dragging. A neighbour nodded. Dog under a ledge, dry enough. The lane mud soft where rain had found it. Her sandals, city sandals, cleaner than April, still wrong for this ground, made small sounds she would not hear once the bus engine started.

The house receded. One look, taken, then forward. Gate. Lane. Monsoon air. City-bound and village-held at once.

---

The bus stand was wet.

Monsoon committed now. Rain steady on the tin awning, puddles forming in the dust that had been dust for months, passengers huddled with bags and umbrellas that would fail in the way all umbrellas failed when the season decided to mean it. Machinery of departure: conductor shouting destinations, drivers checking mirrors, auto drivers offering last-mile rides to people who would refuse them, the diesel-and-upholstery smell of travel beginning. Wet plastic seats inside buses not yet full. Someone eating vada from newspaper, rain-spotted, unbothered.

Meera stood with her bag and Saroja's tiffin and the sentence in her body. Overlapping footprints in memory, a cheek-touch, a nod, a frame incomplete and accurate. Leaving.

Farooq visible from his store doorway, geography of a town small enough that leaving and buying rice shared the same rain. Radio low.

He saw her. Grinned, the grin of someone who had watched her buy dal and argue soap prices and witness something at his counter without being asked to explain it.

"Come back, city girl!"

Shouted across twenty metres. Warm, not oracle, not profound. The man who had sold her rice and called her *city girl* until the name stopped being tease and became recognition: she was both. City and here. Both real.

Her hand went up. Wave returned. Not oracle. Just Farooq. She did not shout back, throat tight, or dignity, or both, but the hand was enough. He understood. He went back to his counter before the bus arrived, which was also enough.

The bus pulled in. Bengaluru, semi-sleeper, the route she had taken down in April when arrival felt like exile and the bag had felt like evidence of a life she was failing to maintain in two places at once. Same route. Different register.

She boarded. Conductor, "Bengaluru?" "Yes." Ticket exchanged. Coins counted. Bag stowed in the hold with a thump that sounded final. Window seat. She had learned to take window seats for village exits because the glass gave you something to look at besides faces, and because rain on glass would matter later without her knowing yet how much.

Interior warming. Bodies settling. Wet clothes smell mixing with diesel. Engine cough. Pause. Then movement.

The bus pulled away.

The village through wet glass: receding. Farooq's store. The stand. The road. The lane to the house invisible from here but present in memory. Guest room, bare threshold, Lakshmi's palm, Ramesh's one line, Saroja's tiffin against her leg. The bus moving regardless of her feelings.

Fields blurred, green deepening with rain, the landscape becoming smear. Taluk town's edge. Then less town. Then distance opening like a mouth.

Phone pinged.

WhatsApp. Ananya.

She opened it.

A photo. Path after rain. Fresh wet ground, mud dark, field edges green, sky grey in the corner the way phone cameras always caught sky. And footprints. New ones. Hiregowda-side prints. Marigowda-side prints. Overlapping. The path ordinary. Shared. Just feet on mud after rain.

She looked at the image in the moving bus.

Phone glow. Screen lit in the slightly dim interior, the path bright on glass, mud and green and prints illuminated from below. Her face lit by the screen. Rain on the window beside her, grey light and streaks, the real village receding behind wet glass while the digital village held steady in her hands.

She typed to Naveen.

*On bus. Tell me later.*

Sent.

She did not look away from the photo immediately. Let the glow sit on her face. Let the bus carry her while the screen held the path steady.

Phone glow illuminating the mud path photo.

Rain on the bus window — committed now, drumming on the roof, streaking the glass beside her face so the screen's light and the grey outside met in her reflection and did not resolve.

Village receding — rain-distorted, already memory, impossible to hold perfectly clear.

Her face lit from below by the screen: the path, the footprints, the green, the ordinary.

The bus moved.

The screen glowed.

The ground held.

She carried it.
