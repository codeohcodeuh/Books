# Chapter 15 — Muddy Sandals

## S01 — You'll Go Back

Ananya heard about the boast before she saw Meera.

Morning grapevine. Saroja's kitchen voice, lowered but not lowered enough, the cousin's name attached to words that made Ananya's new-wife stomach tighten: *won't give one inch*, Gowda guests, vehicle, Ramesh still asleep and therefore not present for the damage he would have to inherit when he woke. Karthik's family would know by now. Cold again. The wedding's laugh-cry already felt like something that had happened to different people in a different village. Married into the middle. Processing post-wedding position while the ground shifted underneath.

She found Meera at the hall door.

Phone in hand. A brief glance at the screen, a ping from Bengaluru that Meera looked at and then pocketed without replying, Nav or office or the life that still existed in the city. Determination in the set of her jaw. City sandals clean on the stone. Meera walked out toward the path where cousins from both sides had gathered at her invitation, and Ananya followed at a distance because new-wives followed at distances until someone told them otherwise, anxious hope mixing with the particular guilt of watching someone you liked walk toward a problem you could not solve.

Overcast. Humid. Ground damp from morning moisture that had not quite become rain. Wet earth smell, field grass, open air without enclosure. Sky flat and grey, covering everything, nothing clear today. Ananya walked from house toward path, five minutes of lane and field edge, her churidar catching damp at the hem where grass had transferred moisture. She knew Meera had organized this gathering. Young people from both sides, informal, an attempt to clear air that the drunk boast had poisoned. Anxious hope: maybe Meera could do this. Maybe reasonable conversation among the younger generation could find what elders couldn't.

Path / fields edge: four, maybe five young bodies in a loose circle. Standing. No one seated. Comfort absent, which meant this was not yet a conversation so much as a confrontation wearing polite clothes. Meera in the centre of the loose formation, open-handed gestures, reasonable voice carrying in the flat afternoon light. Ananya arrived partway through. Hung back at the edge, did not enter the circle, positioned as observer with guilt already forming. The disputed path visible twenty metres beyond the group. Not centered, but present, the object around which all talk circled without touching. Wet grass underfoot. Overcast sky flattening shadows. Field sounds. Distant birds, a tractor somewhere not close enough to matter. The privacy of an open place that was not private at all because the village always listened.

Meera had chosen the village for this moment, chosen the damp field and the cousins and the attempt that Ananya now watched with anxious hope curdling into dread. *She's going to try*, Ananya thought. *Maybe she can.* The hope felt naive even as she held it. Post-boast, post-wedding, post-everything that should have taught them both that goodwill was costume. But Meera was smart. Meera cared. Ananya wanted, selfishly, for caring to be enough.

Meera speaking: "What happened last night — that was wrong. Nobody here chose this fight. We're the same generation. We don't have to inherit—"

City-clarity. Articulate. Trying to build common ground. Acknowledging the boast without naming the cousin. "Nobody here chose this fight." Reasonable, true, landing on crossed arms and ground-gazing without purchase. Ananya watched Meera's open-handed gestures. Inclusive, modern, the body language of someone who had learned in conference rooms that transparency dissolved conflict. It did not dissolve here. The field edge held its damp silence. The path visible beyond the group like a third party waiting to be invited into a conversation that kept circling it without touching.

Hegde cousin. Twenty-five, defensive, loyal to Ramesh in the way young men were loyal to uncles who would one day be them. Arms still crossed despite Meera's tone. Jaw set. Not hostile yet, but guarded, the posture of a man who had been told since adolescence that the path was blood and the Gowda side was temporary weather. Gowda-side youth looking at his feet, recently shamed by shove and boast and the accumulated humiliation of being Gowda in Hegde territory even when the territory had promised alliance. Second Gowda youth quieter, watching, assessing whether this conversation was trap or opportunity and deciding, from the set of his mouth, trap. Second Hegde cousin following the first, less vocal, along for the ride. Present because Meera had asked and because refusing would require explaining refusal to elders who wanted the young people to "talk it out" without specifying what talking out would produce.

Body language cold. Nobody relaxing despite good words. Ananya read the room before Meera did. Cringe approaching, sensing the knife before it was drawn. Hopeful becoming uneasy.

Meera pushed slightly. The over-reach forming.

"We can work this out. We're not our fathers. We can figure this out without—"

Ananya felt the cringe before the sentence finished. The room tightening, the cousins not relaxing but hardening, Meera's reasonable voice becoming the wrong instrument for this particular music. *She's going to try*, Ananya had thought approaching. *She doesn't see it coming.* Now: *he's about to—*

Easy for you to say.

The Hegde cousin's voice. Direct, not raised, not cruel, the worst combination. Cut through Meera's reasonable register like a blade through cloth.

"You live in Bangalore. You'll go back."

Silence.

The sentence sat in the damp air. Named. True. The knife. Standing named. Transience exposed. The worst combination: direct, not cruel, accurate. Meera's face did not collapse. She was too composed for that. But something behind her eyes went still, the stillness of a person receiving a fact they had known privately and had hoped nobody would say publicly.

Gowda youth nodded.

Looked at the Hegde cousin. For the first and only time today, both sides united: Meera was not from here enough to mediate. Not land. Not years. Not the accumulated cost of presence this dispute required. Ananya frozen on Meera's behalf. Devastated, watching someone she liked fail publicly. The nod was small. It did not need to be large. It ended the mediation more completely than shouting would have.

Meera had no response.

Because it was true. She couldn't say *I live here*. Couldn't say *I'll stay*.

"I'm trying to help," Meera said anyway. Quiet, not defensive, the sentence of a person who had nothing left except honesty that confirmed defeat.

The Hegde cousin didn't smile. Didn't need to. The words hung beside *you'll go back* like a second nail in the same board. Meera's defeat visible. Silence stretching.

Ananya should say something.

What would she say?

The guilt sat beside the cringe. Witness who did not intervene, new-wife who was married into the middle and still did not know which middle permitted her voice. She stayed at the edge. Empty hands. Regret accumulating. Should have helped. Couldn't. What would intervention even look like?

Silence stretched further.

The loose circle dissolved without declaration. People stepping back, looking at phones, drifting in directions that suggested the conversation had died and no one would bother to bury it. No dramatic exit. Quiet failure. Passive ending. Social dispersal.

Meera stood in the middle of what had been a circle.

Alone now. Others drifting. Hegde cousins toward the lane, Gowda youth toward the path's other side, bodies moving away from the centre as if the centre had become embarrassing. Clean city sandals in damp field mud. The stain climbing the straps where she had not noticed herself sinking, leather darkening, mud in creases, the ground claiming what the city had kept pristine. Ananya watched the mud climb.

She approached.

Finally. Beside Meera. Both looking at nothing in particular. Field edge, overcast sky, the path visible and unimproved, disputed object present at scene edge but not centered. Wordless solidarity beginning. Space between them: everything unsaid.

Regretful. Guilty. Ananya approaching with empty hands. The space between them already holding the walk home, the sentence she would find, the gate where mud would be seen and not named.

## S02 — Kind Silence

They walked side by side.

Path to lane. Lane toward the house. Neither rushing. Footsteps on damp ground. Two sets, steady, unhurried, equal pace, neither ahead nor behind. Silence between them that was not accusation and not congratulation. Just: two women moving through aftermath without a script.

Ananya had watched Meera fail from the edge of a circle she hadn't entered. Guilt lived in that fact. Close enough to have spoken, unclear what speech would have accomplished, married into the middle without yet knowing whether the middle granted permission or only obligation. The lane smelled of wet earth and evening approaching. A dog barked once and stopped. Someone in a nearby house ran a vessel. Domestic sound, ordinary, indifferent to the humiliation occurring in the lane's dust.

Ananya wanted to say *you tried* and knew it was not right. Trying was the problem, trying without the years this ground required, trying as visitor rather than resident. Searching for honest comfort. Weighing sentences. "You tried" would be worse. Would congratulate the attempt rather than witness the failure. Meera didn't want that. Meera wanted someone not to say it, and Ananya knew without being told. The mud on Meera's sandals made a soft sound with each step. Leather meeting damp lane, the ground's slow claim on city footwear.

They're right that you'll leave. They're wrong that it means you don't care.

She said it while walking, not stopping, side-by-side. Less intensity, more generosity. Kind truth. Warmth without a lie. Not healing. Just human after public humiliation.

Meera didn't respond.

Didn't need to. The sentence landed. Didn't fix anything. Saw her. Enough. Not healed. Acknowledged. Reception without reply. Meera too proud to cry, deflated not broken, processing in silence that accepted Ananya's offering without requiring more words. Her shoulders dropped a fraction. Not relief, but the body acknowledging it had been seen.

Walking continued. Lane unspooling toward village. Overcast light flat on both faces. Humidity on skin. Distant sounds of the house. Vessel, voice, ordinary afternoon pretending the morning's grapevine was not still moving through both families like weather. House gate approaching. Iron and habit, threshold back into the family that had produced the cousin's knife and the Gowda nod and the failure Meera would carry in her sandals now ruined.

Gate.

They arrived. Meera's sandals visibly muddy. Stain on straps, mud in the creases where leather met sole, the contrast between city footwear and field ground now complete and undeniable. Ananya looked at them. Meera looked at them. Neither said anything about it. Eyes met briefly. Moved on. Brief gate-pause. Small, quick, domestic, not monumental. Shared knowledge without vocabulary. The stain visible in flat afternoon light, dark against clean leather that would never be clean again in the way it had been this morning.

Ananya touched Meera's elbow once, light, the way you touched someone when words had already done what they could.

They entered the gate. Went inside.

The silence between them was kind but not healing. The ground stain remained. City walked into ground it couldn't yet claim.

Behind them the lane held their footprints in damp dust. Two sets, side by side, already softening at the edges where the ground reclaimed even the evidence of passage. Ananya did not look back. Meera did not look back. The house received them with its ordinary sounds. Someone cooking, someone arguing softly about tomorrow's vessels, the path dispute continuing its underground life in kitchens and corridors while the field edge meeting became another failure added to the pile.
