# Chapter 10 — Broken Post

## S01 — The Shove

Ramesh left before the stove finished its argument with Saroja.

Chappals on the washed step. Dhoti and shirt, morning clothes, field clothes, the uniform of a man who checked his land before the house checked him. Walking stick in the right hand because the left knee remembered last year's harvest and liked to announce itself in the cool hour before heat claimed the morning, even when the morning was not cool, only dry, only April, only the particular hour when dawn pretended gentleness and then betrayed you by eight.

Saroja's voice from the kitchen, something about tiffin, something about Raju already gone ahead, something he did not answer because answers before patrol were invitations to delay. Meera still asleep. Ananya's wedding lists on the table like a second crop requiring harvest. Ten days. The number sat in his chest without changing what his feet did.

Hot wind already moving across the fields. Not cooling. Just moving. Carrying dust and the pre-monsoon pressure that made everything feel charged and unfinished, sky pale, mist burned off, birds starting their work-song as if someone had switched them on. A distant pump coughed and failed and coughed again. Somewhere a dog barked once and thought better of it. His field gate opened with the squeal he had been meaning to fix since last monsoon.

The path between fields.

Red earth under chappals, packed hard by decades of feet and cart wheels and the stubbornness of routes that existed before anyone needed a sentence to defend them. To his right, Hegde cane, his cane, stood in rows that would need water soon. To his left, beyond the wire, Gowda land began with the same crop and a different accounting of pride. Between them, the path: narrow enough to keep a man honest, or it had been, until the wire moved.

Since Mandya. Since fresh cement. Since the morning he had pressed his palm against cool grey and felt inheritance narrowing by six inches, eight.

His farmworker was ahead on the path. Raju, young, obedient, following instructions Ramesh had given without fully examining them: use the path demonstratively, rake it, carry loads through it, assert presence. Make the Hegde side visible in the dirt. The kind of assignment elders gave young men when words had failed and pride needed a body to carry it. Raju had a rake over one shoulder. A bundle of cuttings in the other hand. He walked with the deliberate pace of someone performing use, unaware, or aware in the way workers were aware without saying, that performance on contested ground was its own risk.

Ramesh watched Raju's back. Guilt sat beside vigilance. He had sent the man into this.

Since the wire, these walks had become patrols. Since the tea meeting, waiting. He checked the wire each morning the way other men checked crop, looking for wrongness without wanting to find it, finding it anyway. Wedding in ten days sat behind his thoughts like a date circled in someone else's handwriting. The field was older than alliance.

Movement at the far end of the path.

Figures approaching from the Gowda side. Two, one young, walking fast, anger in the set of shoulders and the speed of feet; one older, behind, slower, trying to moderate with pace alone, failing because youth moved faster than caution.

Ramesh's vigilance sharpened.

Prasad.

He knew the boy by sight, Venki's side, twenty-two or thereabouts, jeans and shirt even at this hour, the kind of young man who carried family honour in his chest like a stone he had not chosen but could not put down. Hotheaded. Sent, or self-sent, to be present on the path the way Raju had been sent. Proxy warriors.

The narrow section.

Where the wire had moved, six inches, eight, the path compressed until two men could not pass without one yielding. The throat of the dispute. Raju had reached it first, rake still on shoulder, and had not stopped, following instructions, asserting use, making the Hegde side visible in dirt the way Ramesh had asked without examining what asking meant. Prasad came from the other end with speed that was its own argument.

They converged. Neither slowed. Neither looked at the ground for a place to step aside. Raju's bundle shifted. Prasad's jaw set. Bodies blocking bodies before words arrived.

"This is our side."

"All sides are for walking."

Brief. Hot. The language of ownership deployed like a weapon neither man had invented, echoing veranda arguments, echoing failed meetings, echoing everything that had not worked. Ramesh's stick struck earth. He moved faster than his knee preferred. Fifty metres. Forty. Thirty. Twenty. His voice carried before his body could reach them, authority attempting to arrive before contact.

"Leave it!"

Too late.

Prasad's hands flat on Raju's chest. One shove. One motion. Harder than intention, or exactly as hard as the anger required, Ramesh would never know which, and it would not matter to the file or the wedding or the bruise forming on Raju's shoulder. Raju's feet lost the earth they expected. His heel caught the uneven ground where the wire's move had narrowed the path. His shoulder found the fence post at the path's edge, old wood, weathered, leaning slightly from years of use and weather and the leaning of men who had argued here before, and the post took his momentum and snapped at the base with a sound like bone giving way.

Splinter. Crack. The worker went down hard, shoulder first, then hip, then the side of his face finding red earth that did not care about sides or weddings or generations.

Dust kicked up from the impact, red earth, dry, fine, rising in a small cloud that caught the early light and then fell back. Settled on Raju's shirt. On Prasad's frozen hands. On the splintered wood.

Three seconds of absolute silence.

Ramesh stopped running ten metres away. The world narrowed to red earth and a man on the ground and a post at a wrong angle and blood, not much, scrape not wound, on Raju's cheek where dirt had found skin. His own breath loud in his ears. Hot wind continuing, indifferent. Birds pausing, then resuming, as if they had been waiting to see if the silence would become speech. The rake lay where Raju had dropped it, tines in dirt, handle pointing at the sky like an accusation.

Prasad stood frozen.

Terror at his own hands. The boy had not meant, or had meant and was now horrified by the meaning made flesh. A young man who had pushed too hard and was watching the consequence arrive in someone else's body, in wood splintered at the base.

The older Gowda farmworker reached Prasad. Hands on his arm. Pulling back. Damage control without apology, instinctive, the movement of a man who knew this had crossed a line and whose first thought was retreat before witness became record. He murmured something Ramesh would never hear, *enough*, or *come*, or a name, and Prasad moved at last, feet finding obedience, body leaving while mind still stood in the three seconds of silence.

Ramesh crouched beside Raju.

Hand on the shoulder, gentle, protective, too late. The shoulder would bruise deep. The face was scraped, blood drying in a line from temple to jaw, dirt embedded where skin had met earth. Not critical. Not hospital. Visible. Painful. The kind of injury that would be seen and asked about and would require a story the families were not ready to tell.

"Anna—" Raju started.

"Quiet. Can you stand?"

Ramesh helped him up. Raju's weight leaned into him, young body suddenly heavy with pain and shock, breath coming hard through the nose, one hand reaching for the hurt shoulder and stopping short. The broken post lay at an angle, base splintered, wood pale where it had cracked, wire sagging where the post had held it. Physical evidence. Injury. A world that had been verbal until this moment.

Prasad was gone. Pulled away by the older man, figures retreating toward Gowda land, dust settling on their departure. The older man did not look back. Did not apologize.

Ramesh stood with his worker on the path. Broken fence post at an angle. Ramesh's hand on Raju's shoulder. Dust settling.

## S02 — No Call Comes

They walked slowly.

Ramesh took Raju's weight on his left side, matching the limp that had begun in the bruised shoulder, each step a small negotiation between pride and pain. Path to road. Road to compound gate. The morning had become fully hot while they were on the ground; heat found the scrape on Raju's face and made it sting. No one spoke. The house appeared ahead, courtyard wall, gate, tulsi pot, the ordinary geometry of a home that would soon become a witness.

"Anna, leave it."

Raju said it quietly. Stoic. Definitive. One sentence, complete. The voice of a man who understood that police meant escalation, retaliation, a wedding destroyed.

Ramesh did not respond. He kept walking. The words sat in him like stones.

The courtyard. Saroja at the door, she had been somewhere inside, heard the slow footsteps, come to look. Her face changed in sequence: recognition, alarm, calculation. The wedding arriving in her eyes before the injury fully registered. She gasped. Hand to mouth.

"Don't go to police before the wedding. Please."

One plea. Quiet. Desperate. Not a speech about family honour, just the immediate arithmetic of alliance and ceremony and ten days that could not survive a station visit. She looked at Raju's face, blood drying, cheek scraped, and her hand went from her mouth to her heart to Ramesh's arm as if touch could redirect what had already happened.

Ramesh did not answer.

He guided Raju inside. Seated him on the low stool in the front room where workers were sometimes given tea after a long day, the same room where alliances were discussed over cups and where violence was not supposed to arrive before breakfast. Saroja followed, already calling for turmeric, cloth, ice if they had it, the home remedies that were the first line of defence when clinics and stations lived in a different category of problem.

A neighbour woman appeared, summoned by voice or instinct, the village's secondary medical system, and began applying yellow paste to Raju's scraped cheek with the brisk tenderness of someone who had treated worse and would treat worse again and did not need the story to do the work.

Meera was in the corridor.

Ramesh saw her peripherally, cotton from yesterday's errands, confusion on her face, the posture of a woman who had heard the commotion and stopped at a distance because the room told her this was not her question to ask. She saw Raju's face. The bruise forming on the shoulder visible where his shirt had been pulled aside for the paste. She filed it. She did not approach. She did not speak.

Ramesh sat in the chair across from Raju and waited for his hands to stop shaking.

Time passed. The house continued around the crisis, vessels in the kitchen, voices lowered in the way voices lowered when children might hear, someone bringing water in a steel tumbler, Saroja hovering with the restless energy of a woman who could fix garland strings and seating charts but could not fix this. The neighbour finished the paste and murmured something about rest. Raju nodded. Stoic. Already returning to the economy of *leave it*.

Ramesh's phone sat on the table where he had left it that morning, dark screen, silent, the object that should have rung by now if the other side understood what had happened.

He looked at it. Waited.

Venki. An apology. An explanation. Someone from the Gowda side acknowledging that a line had been crossed.

The phone did not ring.

No call came.

Minutes accumulated. The morning moved toward mid-morning. Saroja brought tea Ramesh did not drink. Someone mentioned breakfast and the mention died in the room. Raju sat with turmeric on his face and pain in his shoulder, the dignity of a man who would not ask for compensation.

Ramesh looked at the broken fence post, in his mind or leaning against the courtyard wall, he could not tell which anymore. Wood at a wrong angle, base splintered, the thing he had touched that dawn now destroyed.

This is no longer about meters.

The thought arrived without permission. This is about what kind of people we are.

He did not decide about police. Saroja's plea on one side. Raju's *leave it* on the other. Venki's silence as answer. The wedding in ten days. The worker's blood drying on his cheek. The post broken. The path still there, still contested.

Broken fence post. The crack that could not be un-cracked. Dread settled into his chest and stayed.
