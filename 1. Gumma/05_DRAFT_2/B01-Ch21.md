# Chapter 21 — Loudspeaker Distortion

## S01 — The Wrong Question

The jatre ground was already full by ten.

Pre-monsoon heat sat on Meera's neck. Dust rose from a thousand chappals and settled again on bangle stalls and plastic toys and the cheap cotton flags strung between poles. Loudspeakers on either side of the temple entrance blasted devotional music through amplifiers that cost less than the garlands draped over them. The song arrived in pieces, the singer's voice breaking when the volume peaked, a feedback ghost under every chorus. Meera had learned to walk through that noise the way you learned to walk through wedding crowds: head slightly down, purpose in your hands, body negotiating gaps between aunties and teenagers and vendors who called prices into the distortion as if volume could compete with volume.

Lakshmi had sent her with a list. Jaggery first, the dark block for the pooja shelf, wrapped in cloth so it would not sweat through the bag. Then thread, the red kind Saroja preferred for tying around the lamp. Domestic errands wearing festival clothes: cotton kurta, hair pinned up, the practical shoes that could survive dust and temple steps. Saroja had wanted to come and had been pulled into helping with the school stall; Meera had the list and the freedom of a morning that was half obligation and half holiday.

At the jaggery stall she negotiated without embarrassment. The vendor knew city nieces paid city prices unless you showed you lived here now.

"Forty-five," he said.

"Forty. Last time was forty."

"Last time was before the sugar went up."

They settled at forty-two. The block went into her cloth bag, heavy and faintly sticky through the wrapping. Thread from the next row, three packets, one spare. Errands done. The obligation satisfied before pleasure, which was how Lakshmi liked things arranged.

She browsed. Glass bangles clicked under her fingers, green and gold, the colors of every jatre she had ever attended as a child visiting relatives, now strange and familiar at once. A stall sold fried kodubale in newspaper cones; the smell of hot oil mixed with camphor from the temple direction and jasmine from garland sellers. Incense smoke threaded through the dust. Children shrieked at a toy-gun booth. An elderly couple moved slowly through the queue toward the temple entrance. Meera had skipped darshan and would skip it again. Bells from inside the temple competed with the loudspeakers and lost. The loudspeakers pulsed. *Harivarasanam* or something like it, distorted into a new shape by cheap speakers and excessive devotion.

Near a flower stall, marigold heaps, rose petals in steel bowls, a woman passed at the edge of Meera's vision. Steel bowl in one hand. Profile that might have been familiar or might have been any woman buying flowers for a home altar. Meera's attention snagged for half a second. Was that—

The crowd swallowed her. No eye contact. No pause. Gone before Meera could decide if she had seen anything at all.

Malathi found her by the biscuit stall, waving over the heads of teenagers clustered around a ring-toss game.

"Meera! Over here."

Ananya's teacher looked festival-bright in a green saree, bangles loud when she moved. Beside her stood a man Meera did not know, mid-thirties, casual shirt, no notebook visible today, the comfortable posture of someone who could stand in any crowd and look like he belonged there. Observer's eyes. Pleasant face. Hair neat in the way of people who worked in offices but had dressed down for a village fair.

"Meera, this is Rohan-ji. He's doing a features piece on village dispute-resolution methods, for a Kannada magazine. I told him you're the one who actually lives here now."

"Pleased to meet you," Rohan said. Kannada accented but clean; English-educated voice underneath. He smiled the way journalists smiled, open, interested, already sorting you into categories without making you feel sorted.

"Pleased to meet you."

They stood in a pocket of slightly thinner crowd near the stall edge, people flowing around them like water around a stone. The loudspeakers dropped to a lower register for a moment, and Meera could hear Malathi's bangles again.

Malathi drifted toward the biscuit display, and Meera understood without being told: *you talk, I'll look*. Innocent buffer. A door Meera had not known was there, now open.

Rohan asked easy questions first. How long had she been in the village? Did she miss city life? What did she think of the jatre food? Which stall had the best kodubale? He listened like listening was his profession, which it was, head tilted, follow-up questions timed to show he had heard the previous answer. Meera answered honestly where honesty was safe. The heat. The dust. The way festivals here were louder and smaller at the same time than anything in Bangalore. The adjustment of learning which aunties to greet and which prices to argue about. He laughed at the right moments. She found herself comfortable, charmed in the low-grade way you got charmed by someone who made you feel interesting without making you feel exposed.

Then he pivoted.

"I hear your family had that path thing? And apparently some elder spoke the actual old terms?" Direct. No wind-up. "That's amazing. Do you know who he was? This kind of living memory, it's incredible."

Meera's shoulders lifted before her mind caught up. Jaw tightening. The comfortable pocket of conversation gone, replaced by something alert and closed.

The courtyard speech had traveled further than the courtyard. Grapevine. Magazine research. Hunting.

"Do you know who he was?" Rohan repeated, genuine enthusiasm in it, not malicious, not predatory in the cruel sense. Professional hunger. The hunger of someone who had found a thread and wanted to pull it into print. His eyes were bright with it. He really did find these patterns interesting. That made her refusal feel stranger to her. He was not a villain asking for secrets; he was a man who valued what she had witnessed, and her body still said no.

"Just an uncle-type," Meera heard herself say. Controlled voice. Casual. "He knew family history. It's not that unusual here."

She was lying. She knew exactly how unusual. The courtyard, the terms, the silence after, Shankarappa's cup placed afterward with that particular precision. Her body had decided before her mind agreed: this man should not have it.

Rohan smiled. Genuine. Curious. He knew she had just closed the door. She could see him know, in the slight shift of his attention, the way a journalist noted refusal without pushing because pushing would end the interview.

"Family memory is beautiful," he said easily. "No one values it enough."

She nodded. He nodded. Malathi returned with a packet of biscuits, Parle-G, the safe choice, and Rohan asked her about the school stall near the temple, whether Ananya had helped with the rangoli, and the conversation moved to food and crowds and the price of coconut burfi. Meera answered on autopilot, burfi price, stall location, the teacher's daughter's enthusiasm. Her body still closed while her mouth performed normal. The probe was over. Its residue stayed in her body, shoulders still high, jaw still set.

Malathi noticed nothing. That was the gift of festival noise.

They parted near the temple side. Malathi and Rohan continued into the fair, his head turning once, not quite a look back, more a note taken. Meera stood with the jaggery and thread in her bag and the loudspeaker drowning whatever the conversation had meant in distorted devotional music.

Why did I lie?

She did not know.

## S02 — Not Amplified

The afternoon heat peaked while the crowd thinned.

Families who had come early began drifting toward buses and autos. Stalls started discounting unsold stock. Meera found shade under a stall overhang at the jatre's edge, the festival continuing around her without her full participation.

She stood with her errand bag and tried to understand her own behavior.

Why did I lie?

Family loyalty, maybe. Small-town protectiveness. Her own desire to be the one who understood it, not some Kannada magazine features writer collecting living memory like specimens. Paranoia. All of these. None of them certain.

The realization came separately, heavier: she had not just watched. She had actively concealed. After the boast she had withheld from Ramesh. After Gayatri she had not asked Lakshmi. Now this, a lie to a stranger with a notebook in his mind if not his hands. She had chosen a side without knowing what the sides were. She had closed a door Rohan would remember, without being asked, without knowing why. The not-knowing was the worst part.

What am I protecting?

Two people. Two quiet interventions. A feeling she could not prove. A pattern she suspected but could not name. You could not explain why you protected something you could not describe. If she could name it, she could evaluate her choice. But it was formless, and formlessness meant her lie sat in her body without justification, only instinct.

The loudspeakers peaked, a devotional song climbing toward its chorus, the cheap amplifier straining, and then broke. Feedback screech, three seconds, raw and physical. Everyone winced. A child covered both ears and started crying until his mother shushed him. An elderly man near Meera muttered something about the committee's equipment budget. Then the screech cleared and the song continued as if nothing had happened, devotion resuming in the same broken register as before.

Some things only work when they're not amplified.

The thought arrived half-formed during the screech and could not keep hold of. She did not know if she meant the prayer or the pattern.

She would not tell anyone about Rohan. Not Saroja. Not Lakshmi. Not Malathi, who had meant well and could not have known what she was introducing. The closed door was Meera's alone to carry.

A vendor packed up unsold toys. An auto driver slept on his seat with a handkerchief over his face. The jatre continued its slow afternoon dismantling.

Across the thinning fair, Saroja waved from near the auto stand, arm raised, the gesture that meant *come, we're leaving, your father is waiting*. Meera walked toward her family group, festival-normalcy ready to swallow the probe's residue. Ananya was eating something sticky on a stick. Ramesh looked hot and tired and unaware that anything had happened at a biscuit stall near the temple. The unease went under the surface, not away.

Jaggery and thread in her bag. The closed-door feeling alongside both.

Loudspeaker distortion fading back into song behind her. The sound covering everything, including what she almost understood.

Some things only work when they're not amplified. She did not know what she meant by that yet.
