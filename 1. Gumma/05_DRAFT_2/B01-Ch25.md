# Chapter 25 — Bad Knee

## S01 — I Cannot Prove It

Sunday morning smelled of wet earth.

The first pre-monsoon shower had come overnight, not enough to flood anything, enough to change the air. Meera stood in the guest room doorway and breathed it in. The oppressive heat of the last week had broken. Dust that had coated everything since the departure sat darker now, settled, beaten down. Birds louder than they'd been in days. Water still dripping from the tile edges somewhere in the courtyard, a steady irregular percussion that made the house feel rinsed.

She was looking for the umbrella.

Saroja had said it was behind the cot, *the black one, not the broken one, the one with the handle that doesn't stick*, and Meera had slept badly enough that morning logistics felt like a kindness. Something to do with her hands. She moved the pillow. Checked behind the trunk. Pulled the bedsheet aside and looked in the gap between cot and wall. No umbrella.

The cot's far side, then. She crouched, felt along the wall, found fabric and pulled. Yes, the black one, handle intact, the nylon still smelling faintly of last monsoon's mildew. She shook it once. Closed it again. Carried it toward the corridor without knowing yet if she would need it. The rain had stopped. The earth was still drinking.

The house was waking in its usual Sunday rhythm. A vessel clinked in the kitchen. Someone coughed behind a closed door. Saroja's voice, indistinct, giving instructions about dal. Meera walked through the corridor and out toward the courtyard, the umbrella hooked over her arm because she had found it and did not yet have a reason to put it down.

Outside, the compound held puddles that caught morning light. The veranda tiles were still damp from the shower. She had come for tea, or for air, or for the kind of movement that kept thought from settling where she didn't want it. She expected empty seating. Shankarappa gone five days now, since the quiet departure, since the empty guest mat and the rumor heat that had peaked while he was away. An empty chair where his tea had been.

She stopped.

He was there.

Shankarappa. Seated at the far end of the veranda the way he had been seated in the courtyard mediation, not performing arrival, not claiming space. Tea in hand. Steam rising in the cool air. As if he had never left. As if leaving and returning were both within his ordinary vocabulary and neither required announcement.

No *I'm back.* No look toward the door to see who had noticed. He was simply present again.

Her body knew before her mind arranged the knowledge.

He's here.

Relief arrived without permission, warm, physical, immediate. The five-day absence compressed into something her chest recognized before she could name it. She had cared more than she knew. She did not gasp. Did not exclaim. She stopped, registered, and after a moment moved to sit.

Not next to him. Not across from him in confrontation distance. Nearby, three or four meters, earshot but not conversation-distance. The gap was respect. She set the umbrella against the veranda pillar and sat on the damp tile edge, the surface cool through her salwar. From here she could see the courtyard, wet earth, a crow picking at something near the wall, the laundry line empty because rain had come and Saroja would rehang everything later.

Lakshmi passed through the courtyard with tea things, a tray already empty, cups collected. She had already served him. Her face was calm in the way that said she had known. Not triumph. Not I-told-you-so. Just: knowing. *He'll be back or he won't.* He came back. She glanced once toward the veranda, Meera seated, Shankarappa seated, the morning ordinary, and continued toward the kitchen without comment.

From the kitchen doorway, Saroja saw him.

Meera caught it in passing: Saroja's hands still on the doorframe, the instruction she had been about to call out dying in her throat. Not surprise exactly. Something loosening. Her shoulders dropped a fraction before she turned back inside. The house had been carrying a weight Saroja would never have named aloud, and now it was lighter by a degree only a woman managing a household would feel.

Meera watched Shankarappa's hands.

The bad knee. Worse than the courtyard evening when she had first noticed the absent rubbing gesture. Stiff. He rubbed it with that same maintenance, harder now. Five days on buses, on foot, on whatever surfaces had carried him, the knee had paid. The travel had cost him something visible.

Don't grab.

The instinct from the closed door and the laundry line and the town errand where she had learned to wait instead of interrogate. With this man, you didn't grab. You waited. She chose to sit. Chose to stay. Chose to be present near him without demanding purpose. For Meera, urban, verbal, used to directness, this was hard. Anyone watching from the courtyard would see: the guest and the niece, sitting in silence.

The minutes accumulated.

Birds. Dripping tiles. Wet earth smell rising from the courtyard. Insects starting again in the warmed air. A child somewhere behind the house, voice indistinct. Shankarappa's knee-rub continuous, not foregrounded, just present, the body doing its maintenance while the mind did whatever work minds did in silence.

She could have asked where he went. Could have said she was worried. Could have filled the silence with the questions the village had been forwarding through phones and porches and the traveler message she had carried home from town. She didn't. Waiting was the act.

Shankarappa finished his tea. Set the cup down, not precisely this time, not the careful placement from the evening when he had set tea and walked away without looking back, just placed, the absence of ceremony its own signal. This wasn't a function-moment. This was personal.

"The rain came. Good."

Normal comment. To the air, or the courtyard, or his own knees. Not turned toward her. She happened to be close enough to hear.

She held still.

"I was thinking about what I said." Pause. "The terms I spoke, they are what I was told. I believe they are accurate." Another pause, longer. "I cannot prove them beyond my telling. Chandrappa and Mallappa are dead. The tree is gone. I carry what was given to me. It may be imperfect."

He rubbed his knee.

That's it. No more. The admission hung in wet air. He didn't retract. He didn't weaken. He said: I believe AND I can't prove. Both held simultaneously.

Meera received it without words. She had none.

He's saying he might be wrong.

Not weakness. Not retraction. Something harder than certainty. Certainty would have been a weapon. This was an offering.

The silence after stretched. Not empty, full of morning continuing. Birds. Dripping. The distant kitchen sound. His hand on his knee. Her breath steady because she had decided to keep it steady. The admission sitting between them like something placed on the tile between two seated people: visible, unclaimed, offered.

This was not a man with documents. Not a court witness. Not someone who could produce proof on demand. This was a man carrying memory, imperfect memory, and choosing to carry it anyway. And saying so. No savior. No proof. No answer that would end the village's fever with a single verified sentence. Then something else underneath the disappointment. Respect. This was harder than confidence. This was more honest.

What kind of proof is a person?

The question formed without finishing. It would sit in her for days.

Shankarappa rubbed his bad knee, the gesture continuous, absent, the body maintaining itself while the mind had done its harder work. The admission hung between them. Wet earth smell. Birds louder than they'd been in days. Tea cooling in the cup he had just set down. Morning continuing around them, a crow on the compound wall, a child somewhere behind the house, the drip of water from a tile edge, Saroja's voice calling for someone to bring the laundry in before the sun hardened it.

Nothing resolved. Something deepened.

She didn't respond. She received. Settled into the receiving the way you settled into shade when the sun had been too long on your neck.

## S02 — Forty Seconds

By ten o'clock the morning had warmed.

Meera moved from the veranda to the back porch, the courtyard edge where laundry sometimes hung and where Ramesh kept his station when he wasn't hiding in back rooms. She carried the admission with her the way you carried something fragile: not displayed, not discussed, just present. Post-admission quiet. Disappointment and respect held together in the same hand.

The wet courtyard dried in patches. Puddles shrinking. Tile edges still dripping in places, already less. The rain-relief continuing, earth warming, domestic sounds resuming, the house finding rhythm again. Saroja had rehung laundry on the line. White against green. Ordinary.

She stood near the porch rail. A cup of tea she hadn't made sat on the ledge, someone else's morning, still warm enough to steam slightly. Morning tasks available but not urgent. Processing.

Ramesh emerged from the back rooms.

He glanced toward the veranda first, brief, involuntary, where Shankarappa remained a distant figure. Seated still. Knee-rubbing visible even at distance, the small repetitive motion of a man maintaining his body while the house maintained its uncertainty around him. Ramesh's face moved through its sequence: surprise, then something older, shame from the courtyard where he had thrown a dare and a quiet man had answered with terms that made him smaller, then relief he would not name.

He did not approach Shankarappa. Did not speak to him. Did not cross the courtyard toward the veranda where the guest sat in his returned ordinary. Turned instead toward the porch where Meera was.

He stopped.

She stopped, whatever she had been doing, which was nothing visible, which was the point.

They hadn't planned this. Neither had prepared words. Co-presence accidental. Unplanned. The back porch overlooking wet courtyard, garden edge, domestic space separate from Shankarappa's veranda, architecturally distant, essential. Shankarappa visible as a shape on another part of the house. Rubbing his knee. Not looking toward them. Not participating. His presence was the stabilizer. His attention was elsewhere.

Forty seconds. Maybe a minute.

They didn't speak.

Both looked at the wet courtyard, puddles drying, leaf-litter dark against tile, the ordinary aftermath of rain. Ramesh stood beside her. Not uncomfortably close. Just: in the same air. The silence said what language couldn't: we survived the same weather while he was gone. Nothing more. Nothing less. No path talk. No apology. No naming Shankarappa. No reference to the last two weeks.

Meera chose to stay in the silence rather than fill it.

She could have said: *He's back.* Could have said: *Are you okay?* Could have referenced anything from the fortnight that had aged them both. She didn't. The not-speaking was the act. Stillness over words. Second patience beat of the morning. The grammar she was learning.

Ramesh's shoulder near hers. Not touching. One brief moment where their eyes might have met, she wasn't sure, then both looking back at the courtyard. The drip from a tile edge. A bird. The drying earth. Somewhere inside, Saroja moved a vessel. The house continuing around them.

Meera thought of the shove she hadn't seen and the terms she had heard and the guest visible at distance who had admitted he might be wrong. None of it belonged in this silence. The silence was its own weather.

Ramesh moved.

Went inside after forty seconds, no goodbye, no comment, no look back. The beat was presence. Shared weather. Surviving the same storm. That was all. But it was something.

She noted it without analyzing. Not reconciliation. Coexistence. A tick. A seed for a warmth that wouldn't arrive for days yet but needed this moment to be possible. The kind of small mark you almost didn't notice until later when you needed proof that two people could stand in the same air after everything.

That was something.

The house atmosphere had changed. Rumor-heat cooling, shoulders lowering in rooms she couldn't see. Not because he had done anything since sitting down. Because he was sitting. On the veranda. Rubbing his knee.

It's already calmer.

Brief observation. The house breathed differently. Lighter. People less tense. He hadn't fixed the path. Hadn't addressed Ramesh. Hadn't explained where he'd been. He sat.

What kind of authority works by sitting?

She was no longer asking *who is he?*, which could be answered by a document, a name, a history file. She was asking *what IS this?*, which could only be answered by watching, over years, what this kind of presence did. What kind of memory was carried with admitted doubt and still held weight. A harder question. A better one. The right one forming gradually, not as eureka, just as the next shape the wondering took.

Shankarappa remained visible at distance, veranda figure, knee-rubbing continuous, barely moving. Meera and Ramesh already separated without goodbye. The porch empty of Ramesh now. Meera still on it, carrying disappointment-respect like two weights balanced on the same scale.

Courtyard returning to ordinary rhythm. Drying puddles. Warming earth. Domestic sounds resuming, vessel, voice, the flap of a wet sari hung to finish drying, Saroja calling for turmeric from the store room. The village out there somewhere, already slightly calmer. Because someone came back. Someone who sits and rubs his knee and says: I might be wrong.

Somehow, impossibly, that was enough.
