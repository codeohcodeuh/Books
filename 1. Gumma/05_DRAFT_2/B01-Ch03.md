# Chapter 3 — Ticket PDF

## S01 — Three Missed Calls

The kitchen light made everything look later than it was.

Meera set her bag on the chair by the door, the same chair that had held Divya's spare key two nights ago, and kicked off her slippers. The flat smelled faintly of the morning's coffee and of rooms that had held heat all day without complaint. Fan on high, blades chopping the air into motion that was not coolness. Through the window, Bengaluru's Friday evening was a continuous hush of tyres and someone's television laughter two floors down. On the sill, a basil plant she had bought after a bad sprint and forgotten to water properly sat brown at the edges, still trying.

Her phone lay face-up on the counter where she had dropped it when she came in.

Three missed calls. Amma. Timestamps marching through the afternoon like a complaint filed in triplicate. 2:14, during the architecture review she could not leave. 4:02, during the one-on-one where she had nodded at problems that were not hers. 6:41, watched arrive on the desk during standup, blue and insistent, while she let it die into voicemail with the particular cowardice of people who loved their mothers and still needed ten minutes of unmarked air.

Week nearly done. Nothing in the fridge worth inventing into dinner. She filled a glass from the filter and drank standing up, performing the evening as if missed calls were a weather system that might pass if she did not look at the sky.

She put the phone face-down.

Washed her hands. The apartment group had been quieter today, Asha on generator hours, polite thumbs-ups, and Meera had scrolled past with the thin pride of surviving Wednesday's bay. Nav's face at the café sat behind her ribs without asking to be unpacked. Ordinary residue. Manageable.

The phone buzzed against laminate.

Fourth. Or a message that would become a fourth if ignored. The counter transmitted the vibration into her hip where she leaned.

Meera turned it over, saw Amma, exhaled through her nose, and called back before her body could invent another chore.

Saroja answered on the first ring, as if she had been holding the phone in a grip that denied the possibility of further delay.

"Finally," Saroja said, not unkind, already moving at logistics speed. "I thought maybe you fell into one of those office holes and forgot you have a family. Are you home? Eat something. You sound like you haven't eaten since Tuesday."

"I'm fine, Amma."

"Fine is what you say when the rice is finished and you're drinking water for dinner. Listen. Ananya's things are moving fast. Pallavi is asking about who is coming from our side, as if I'm a booking agent. Lakshmi keeps saying your name like I'm hiding you in a cupboard. When are you coming?"

Meera leaned her hip harder against the counter. The phone warmed against her ear. Fan blades strobed the wall with soft, repeating shadow. Outside, a scooter braked too late and someone swore without heat.

"I'll look at dates this weekend, Amma."

"This weekend." Saroja repeated it the way she repeated recipes that had gone wrong. Not shouting, correcting. "Beta, the wedding is not a cloud. It has a date. You need to be here before the fifth if you can. Better fourth evening, then you can help. There is so much. Flowers. The cousins from Mysore who will arrive early and expect coffee every twenty minutes. Your father won't come, you know that, so you are the only one representing properly from our—"

"Amma."

"—side. Don't Amma me through the important part. Ananya is expecting you. She asked twice. Once in the morning, once when they were measuring for the entrance thing. Lakshmi asked this morning, *Is Meera coming or only sending parcel?* As if my daughter is a courier service. I told her you're coming. Don't make me a liar in my own mother's house."

Guilt arrived the way Saroja always sent it: not as a weapon brandished, but as furniture already in the room, rearranged for maximum contact. Ananya would notice absence. Lakshmi would notice with the quiet accuracy of women who counted bodies without counting aloud. Meera's father would not come, that fact had hardened years ago, and so the duty of being seen fell where it always fell.

She stared at the stove clock. 8:41. Numbers indifferent. A magnet held a takeaway menu she never used.

"I have a project mid-stretch," she said. "If I leave too early, the release—"

"Everyone has a project. The wedding does not wait for Jira." Saroja's voice climbed half a note, then softened. "I'm not asking you to move permanently. Come. Be seen. Help your cousin not feel like the city forgot her. And your Ramesh-mama is being impossible these days, some land thing, I don't know, he's in a mood, so the house needs extra hands who can smile and not argue. You can do that. You were always good at that."

Ramesh-mama. Impossible. Land thing. Meera let it pass the way she let most village weather pass when she was in HSR with a fan and a glass of water: noted, unopened, filed under Uncles And Weddings. She said, "Mm," and meant nothing by it except that she had heard a noise shaped like information and would not chase it down a phone line on a Friday.

"Before the fifth," Saroja pressed. "Book something. Don't do that thing where you look and look and then the tickets are three thousand and you blame the universe, which is the same as blaming me when I'm the one calling."

"I don't blame you for ticket prices."

"You blame timing. Timing is also me, apparently." A breath, shorter. Then warmer, the voice that still undid her when she was not careful: "Just come, kanna. Lakshmi will make the lemon rice you pretend you don't miss. Sleep in your old room. Let the house see your face before all the strangers arrive."

Meera's mouth found the sentence before her plans finished their committee meeting.

"I'll book tonight, Amma."

Silence for half a second. Surprise, or satisfaction disguised as it.

"Good," Saroja said, already shifting to the next cell. "Send me the screenshot. Not tomorrow morning when you've talked yourself into Monday. Tonight. And tell that office of yours you're going for family. They can survive without you for a few days. The world survived before product managers."

"Okay."

"Okay is not a screenshot."

"I'll send it."

"Love you. Eat something that is not water. You sound like fluorescent lights."

"Love you, Amma."

The call ended with Saroja's small click, the sound of a person who had gotten what she needed and still had three other calls to make. Meera lowered the phone.

Slight annoyance at her own mouth, that traitor, offering *tonight* like a gift. Under it, the familiar gravity of family: not cruelty, not quite choice. She set the phone on the counter face-up this time, as if honesty of position could substitute for the booking she had not yet made.

The stove clock read 8:58.

Twenty-eight minutes gone. A night ahead in which the word *tonight* would sit on her shoulder, warm and exacting, until she made it true.

## S02 — Non-Refundable

By 9:30 she had moved the problem to the bed, as if geography could make obligation softer.

Laptop open on a pillow that still held the dent of last night's poor sleep. Kurta exchanged for an old T-shirt. Fan on high, its round shadow crossing the wall in steady arcs. Phone dark beside her knee, screen asleep, Saroja's satisfaction temporarily fed. The bedroom held the day's heat in the mattress and the curtains. Laptop fans whirred; the machine added its own small summer to her thighs.

The booking site loaded with cheerful colours and a stock photo of a bus that looked cleaner than any bus she had taken. She searched the route she knew by habit more than longing: Bengaluru to the belt of towns that thinned into autos, cousin-voices, jasmine in plastic bags, the house where childhood had smelled of rain on red earth and Lakshmi's kitchen.

Options appeared. Dates in neat rows. Seat maps. Prices that pretended to be invitations.

She scanned for something that suited her, midweek later, after the project spit out its current fire, after the release window stopped punishing absences with looks. A Tuesday evening. A Wednesday with buffer and dignity. Her finger hovered. Optimism, brief as AC during load-shedding: maybe family math would bend if she arrived smiling hard enough on a later bus.

Then Saroja's constraint returned, exact as a calendar invite she had not accepted: *before the fifth if you can — better fourth evening.*

The dates that suited Meera arrived too late for being seen, for helping with flowers and Mysore cousins and coffee every twenty minutes, for not becoming the relative who sent a parcel and a carefully worded excuse. She ran the work week in her head without opening the work calendar, mid-project, dependencies named after people, the particular tightness in her manager's jaw when personal life became an out-of-office. Double bind. No date wore both hats without tearing a seam.

She typed Saturday, 4 April.

The seat map bloomed. A price sat there, not yet cruel, the ordinary cruelty of interstate travel. She imagined the bus: evening departure, AC that would fail near Maddur, a seatmate with snacks, village air at night smelling of something green and burnt. Saroja fussing on the road before Meera could arrange her face. Lakshmi's lemon rice waiting. She erased the date, backspace clicking soft in the hot room.

Tried Sunday the fifth, morning bus. Too late for the smile-and-not-argue window. Saroja would call from the kitchen with hurt sharpened into practicality. Ananya would have already started missing her in the pointed way of brides who counted faces. Lakshmi would say nothing and mean everything. Erased.

Typed 4 April again. Selected a seat near the front because she hated the smell of the toilet at the back. Hovered over confirm. Read the small print about changes and cancellations. Her finger lifted. She erased a second time, slower, as if delay were a conversation she was still allowed to have with herself.

When she searched again, same route, same Saturday, the price had risen by a little. Not a catastrophe. Enough to feel like a comment. Time punishing the luxury of hesitation.

Third time.

Saturday 4 April. Evening bus. Arrival that would put her on the house road after dark, bag strap cutting her shoulder, dogs barking at a stranger who was not a stranger. Her finger completed the confirm before her pride finished its minority report. A spinner. A pause in which the fan seemed louder. Then green checkmarks. Thank you for your booking.

Her email pinged soft in the hot room.

Confirmation. PDF attached. She opened it with the caution of someone opening a verdict.

Her name in clean letters. The date. Seat number. Boarding point. The route spelled out like a sentence she could not edit in Google Docs. And there, in the tidy typography of companies that had learned how to end negotiations: **non-refundable**.

The word landed in her body before it landed as meaning. The future had become a file. Her body would be on that bus whether her mood filed an objection or not. Work would be told on Monday. Nav would be told or not told, she had not decided. The apartment group would continue its governance without her for a few days, Asha's blue ticks accumulating in a chat Meera would mute if she was kind to herself.

Reluctant anticipation settled under her ribs. Not excitement, not dread alone. A road opening whether she liked its direction. She thought, briefly, of lemon rice, and of Ramesh-mama in a mood about land, and of Ananya measuring entrances, and then she stopped thinking in lists because lists were how Saroja won.

She stared until the screen's glow felt like a second climate on her face. Phone dark beside the laptop, no missed calls left to hide behind, no fourth buzz to postpone the irreversible.

Outside, Bengaluru continued its Friday: horns, a dog, someone's late music. Inside, the ticket held. She did not close the laptop. Not yet. A bus seat now existed with her name on it, waiting, non-refundable, already on its way toward her whether she slept or not.
