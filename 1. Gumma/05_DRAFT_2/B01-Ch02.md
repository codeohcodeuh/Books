# Chapter 2 — The Hallway Promise

## S01 — The Shoulder Touch

Nav stood three metres from Vikram's glass cabin with his phone open to notes he had rewritten twice that morning and once in the auto on the way in.

Bullet points. Clean. Professional-casual. *Equity structure — ask, don't accuse. Vesting clarity. Timeline that isn't "soon." Calendar hold before we leave the conversation.* The words looked like a person who had slept. He had not slept well. Outside the corridor windows the March glare sat white on the glass, turning the street into a washed photograph; inside, the AC hummed its expensive indifference. Carpet under his shoes, the slightly too-soft kind that made every step sound like apology. Somewhere down the hall a printer coughed out a page and went quiet.

He had asked for a quick sync. Vikram had said, earlier in the week, with the brightness of a man who meant it in the moment, *catch up*. Today was the catch-up. Nav's pulse knew it before his face arranged itself into something suitable for fluorescent light.

The cabin was empty when he arrived. Vikram's jacket hung on the chair inside, good jacket, better cut than Nav's, and a laptop open to something dense with slides, the board deck probably, the thing that ate weeks. Through the glass the open-plan floor swam in calm: heads, monitors, the soft clatter of keyboards pretending urgency was evenly distributed. Meera was at her desk in the far cluster, headphones on, frowning at a ticket the way she frowned when a dependency had lied to her. She did not look up. Good. He did not want an audience for hope. Hope looked stupid in hallways.

A colleague from growth stood at the printer with a sheaf of papers, scrolling his own phone between page grabs. Close enough to see a conversation. Not close enough, Nav told himself, to hear one. Public enough that pressing would become a story. He knew that. He had still come.

Eleven twenty-two. He refreshed nothing on his notes. The notes did not need refreshing. They needed a person on the other side who would stay still long enough to answer them.

The cabin door opened.

Vikram came out moving already, phone in one hand, the other lifting in greeting before his eyes had fully found Nav. The smile arrived on schedule: warm, quick, the smile of a man who remembered names and birthdays and the precise temperature at which loyalty stayed liquid. His shirt looked pressed. His watch caught the glare and threw it.

"Hey — yes, I wanted to catch you too," Vikram said, as if Nav had not been the one waiting with a script in his palm.

Nav's prepared opener shortened in his mouth, the way prepared things always did in weather. "Got a minute? On the ESOP thing — just want to understand the structure, lock a proper time on calendar—"

Vikram's hand landed on his shoulder.

Warm. Firm. The kind of touch that photographed well in a company culture deck. Nav felt the heat of it through cotton and felt, stupidly, younger.

"Absolutely," Vikram said. "Let's lock it."

Relief arrived so fast Nav almost believed the sentence had edges.

Then, still smiling, still touching: "After the board deck ships next week. Once that's out of the way we can sit properly. You know how it is — this week's a wash. Back-to-backs. You of all people get it."

The hand stayed a beat longer than the sentence needed, as if duration could substitute for a calendar invite. Vikram's body had already angled toward the cabin again, toward the next eight minutes, toward whatever meeting waited behind glass with its slides and its hunger. His tone did not cool. The deferral arrived wrapped in the same heat as the promise, gift-paper over an empty box.

"Next week," Nav said, and heard how little it contained. He had three follow-ups ready. None of them fit in a corridor with a colleague at a printer and Vikram's phone lighting up against his thigh.

"Next week," Vikram echoed, brighter. "You've been patient. I see it. We sort this. You're early team, Nav. That doesn't evaporate because we're busy."

Early team. The phrase landed with the soft weight of something reused.

At the printer, the growth colleague glanced over, saw the shoulder, the smile, the performance of care, and looked back at his papers with the mild envy of a person who had not been patted. Appearance filed. Content unread. Meera, far across the floor, might have seen the same silhouette through glass and desks: two men, one hand, a conversation that looked like mentorship from ten metres away.

Vikram squeezed once, released, and turned.

His back crossed the carpet, passed through the glass door, became a shape behind glare and slides. The cabin sealed with a soft corporate click. Nav stood with his phone still open to talking points he had not used, the shoulder warm where a hand had been, the warmth already beginning to leave the way heat leaves a mug.

Same cadence.

The recognition did not ask permission. It simply occupied the space the meeting had pretended to fill.

2021. Night. Vikram's old car smelling of coffee and new plastic, the city sliding past in streaks. Streetlight over the dashboard. Nav still high on a pilot that had nearly failed and then somehow hadn't. Vikram's voice easy, intimate, the voice of a founder before the vocabulary of decks. *You're early team. When we raise, we take care of our people. I'll make sure you're looked after.* Hand on shoulder then too. Same pressure. Same brightness. Same *after* tucked inside the *yes*, so neatly you could miss it if you wanted the yes enough.

Series A had come in 2023. Parties. Photos. A cake with the company logo slightly wrong. The paper had not come. Nav had told himself timing. Timing had become weather. Weather had become the corridor.

He closed the notes app without saving anything new. There was nothing new to save. Somewhere a Slack notification pinged from a pocket that was not his. His own shoulder cooled by degrees he could measure only because he was standing still enough to notice.

He walked back toward the open floor carrying nothing he could show on a calendar. Only the fading print of a hand, and the particular hollow of a man who had been told *absolutely* in a voice that had already left the room.

## S02 — Cooling Coffees

At his desk the tickets refused to become English.

Nav opened one about a latency spike. Closed it. Opened Slack. Closed Slack. The corridor replayed not as dialogue but as geometry: Vikram's angle of departure, the colleague's glance that had mistaken theatre for substance, the glass door sealing on a week that would become another week. Eleven-forty. Eleven-fifty. He drank water from a bottle that tasted of plastic and office. His notes app sat unused under other apps, a fossil of preparation.

People moved around him with the noon hunger of open plan. Someone microwaved something that smelled of sambar. Someone laughed too loudly into a headset. Ordinary Thursday. His body had not received the memo.

Meera pulled one headphone off when he stopped beside her monitor. Her screen showed a sprint board in mid-argument with itself, red tickets, a dependency named after a person who was on leave. She looked tired in the precise way of people who had slept and still owed the night something.

"Lunch?" he said.

She glanced at the clock, at him, at the hallway that led past Vikram's glass. Whatever she had seen from her desk that morning sat behind her eyes without announcing itself. "You look like a person who needs coffee more than food."

"Both. Neither. Come."

"Five minutes. Let me park this comment so it doesn't look like I vanished mid-war."

He waited. She typed three lines with the economy of someone who had learned that long comments were where blame hid. Then she stood, took her phone, left the headphones on the desk like a promise to return to a different kind of noise.

They took the stairs because the lift meant standing still with other bodies and the risk of Vikram's voice arriving bright from a different floor. Down one level the café breathed its noon crowd. Laptops, salads in plastic, other people's deadlines braided low. They ordered without ceremony, the way they had ordered since the month they both started, when this table by the window had been theirs before it belonged to anyone. Two coffees. No food. Nav almost added a sandwich and didn't. Appetite was elsewhere.

The table came free. Cups arrived with a soft ceramic knock. Steam lifted, thinned, began to fail.

Nav wrapped his hands around his cup and did not drink. Meera slid his saucer a centimetre toward him without looking, the way she always did when his elbows wandered. A habit from their first year, when standups ran long and lunch was the only place a sentence could finish.

"Had the chat with Vikram," he said, keeping it light, keeping it like a status update you could put in a standup. "The equity thing."

Meera blew once across her coffee and waited. She had always been good at waiting without filling.

"He said absolutely. Let's lock it. After the board deck next week."

She tilted her head the way she did when a requirement sounded complete and wasn't. "Did he say no?"

The question landed exactly where he had hoped it would not. Clean. Practical. A blade made of common sense.

"No," Nav said. "But—"

He stopped. The *but* had nowhere factual to go. Vikram had not refused. Vikram had affirmed. Vikram had called him early team with the warmth of a man who believed his own myth in real time. The wound was in the temperature of the affirmation, in the body already leaving, in the shoulder used as punctuation. Saying that aloud sounded like a person inventing injury for sport.

Meera watched him. Not unkind. "Then what happened exactly?"

Nav looked at the window glare, at a scooter flashing past outside with a delivery bag strapped on, at the two cups between them still full, steam now only a rumour.

"He said the same thing in 2021," he said, and his voice dropped without his deciding it. "Car ride. After we closed that first ugly pilot, the one that nearly killed us. Same words. Same—" He touched his own shoulder once, a small stupid gesture, then let his hand fall to the table. "Same hand. *We'll lock it. You're early team.* Then Series A. Cake. Photos. Then nothing on paper. Today was the same movie with better lighting."

Meera's face changed. Not much. A shift around the eyes, the mouth holding still as if a laugh or a curse had been considered and rejected. She had seen the hallway from her desk; now she had the rhyme. The recognition crossed her expression the way weather crosses water: visible, not loud.

Neither of them said *he's lying*. The word was too clean for what had happened. Lies were edges you could point at in a meeting. This was weather. This was a pattern that survived by never becoming a no.

Silence held between them. Spoons. A laugh from the next table about someone's wedding. The espresso machine's short violence. Their coffees cooled by degrees you could see: surface still, heat retreating from the ceramic into the room's indifference.

Meera's phone lit on the table beside her wrist. A WhatsApp preview, apartment group, Asha's name half-visible in the stack, the particular blue of a world that enforced itself with screenshots. She dismissed it with one thumb, no comment, eyes returning to Nav. He saw it. She did not offer it. He did not ask. Parallel weather, untranslated.

"I don't know what you do with that," she said finally. Not advice. An admission of limit, offered without apology.

"Neither do I."

She held his gaze. Witnessing was all that fit inside a lunch hour before standups resumed and Vikram's board deck continued its claim on next week. Nav felt the relief of having said the pattern aloud, the shame of needing to.

He almost said *maybe I'm overreading*. He did not. She had already seen his face when he named 2021. Overreading was a story you told yourself to keep opening tickets.

Meera turned her cup a quarter-turn on the saucer. "Next week," she said, testing the phrase the way you tested a loose tooth with your tongue.

They had a name for it between them, never written down, from the month Vikram first used the phrase and Meera had looked at Nav across this same table and said, without smiling, *There it is. Next week.*

"Next week," Nav agreed, and neither of them smiled.

A tray clattered somewhere behind the counter. Someone called a name that was not theirs. The world continued its Thursday with insulting competence.

The cups stayed full. The coffee went on cooling toward the temperature of the room. Her eyes stayed on him, recognizing something she could not fix, and not pretending otherwise, while the café's noise washed around a quiet that had become, for the length of an undrunk lunch, the only honest part of the day.
