# Chapter 27 — Crooked Stamp

## S01 — Compromised Between Parties

Tuesday morning the police station compound was bright.

Puddles drying in the sun. The weather had been shifting since Sunday's first shower, rain and clearing alternating, the taluk town holding its breath before monsoon settled in properly. Priya sat at her desk under the slow ceiling fan, its rhythm familiar from every hour she had spent in this room since the path file landed on her desk. The file open in front of her. Stale. Ten days since the statements. Ten days of waiting for escalation that never came.

The station smelled of old paper and wood and the faint ink of forms that had been filled and filed and forgotten. Through the window: compound wall, a constable moving a bike, bright light on drying puddles, the ordinary commerce of a small police station performing its daily fiction that the world arrived in legible pieces.

The file wanted drama her notebook had predicted. The village gave her none.

She flipped through the pages.

Hegde version: path exclusive, wire reasonable, shove provoked. Gowda version: shared access, wire provocation, shove defensive. Contradictory on every point that mattered. Who shoved first, whether the path was shared or exclusive, whether the wire was provocation or response. Names and dates and signatures that proved people had been angry enough to come to a police station but not angry enough to agree on what had happened when they got there.

Her notebook with arrows still in the desk drawer. She opened the drawer to reach for the stamp and saw its edge, buff cover, the social-history instinct that had pointed toward connections she couldn't use officially. Arrows drawn in margins. Names linked. Questions that led somewhere the FIR format couldn't follow. She didn't open it. Didn't need to. The arrows were filed with the case whether she looked at them or not.

No new incident since the warning held. The wedding had happened. The shove had aged. Prasad's shoulder healed or didn't, off her desk either way. The village had gone quiet in a way her forms couldn't capture. Temperature dropped without her doing anything except waiting and watching and noting contradictions she couldn't resolve into charges.

Last time, the festival file—

Brief memory, one beat. Three years ago. Two families, a festival boundary, her premature closed notation before tempers cooled. One side escalated to district. Warning on her record. Transfer application stalled in the queue where applications went to wait and die. She couldn't afford closed too early again. Couldn't afford left open too long either. PSI wanted communal-sensitive files cleared before transfer season, and the pressure was professional even when the stake was personal.

She checked the wording twice in her head before putting pen to paper.

Yesterday's messages had arrived through the ordinary channels: word via a Saroja-adjacent relative on the Hegde side, a Gowda messenger on the other. Neither side wanted the police matter to continue. Not formal. Not signed. Not affidavits. Just: word through relatives. The informal communication sufficient for her to recommend closure. Sufficient and thin.

She drafted carefully.

*Compromised between parties. Both sides communicated via relatives declining further action. No further action recommended.*

Each word weighed. Accurate. Thin. Didn't capture what happened. What happened wasn't her job to capture. Only that it stopped. The statements still contradicted each other on the record. No resolution in legal terms. Parties declined pursuit. That was what she could document without fabricating agreement that didn't exist on paper.

She read it once. Changed nothing. The language was correct. It was also empty, the way official language was always empty when the thing it described had happened somewhere forms couldn't reach.

The wedding had happened in the middle of this file. The shove had happened before the wedding. The village had gone quiet after both, in a sequence her forms listed as dates and incident numbers without capturing the temperature change that made yesterday's messages possible. She didn't need to capture it. She needed to not close too early. Not leave open too long. Get the wording right. Stamp the folder. Shelve it.

A constable brought tea. "Tea, ma'am?"

She nodded. He set the cup on the desk edge, waited without comment, then returned to the compound. The fan turned. The tea steamed. The file waited for its mark.

She reached for the CLOSED stamp.

The ink pad was dry. Hadn't been used recently, the station's bureaucratic decay visible in the dusty surface, the red faint even before she pressed. She pressed the stamp to the pad twice, harder the second time, getting what she could. Then to the file cover. Firm. Routine.

It landed crooked.

Partial impression. The C faded at one edge. The D clear. The red mark drying at an angle that would annoy anyone who cared about straight lines. Not careless. The pad was dry, the angle was what dry pads produced, the partial impression was what this station's tools gave you when you asked them to close a file.

Priya looked at it.

Re-stamp?

She looked at the ink pad again. Dry. Would need fresh ink from the store room. Would need walking. Would need caring about straight lines on a buff folder that would join a shelf of other buff folders that nobody would open unless someone new got transferred in and decided to organize what previous officers had organized badly.

No. It's legible.

Flat. Practical. Move on.

She closed the file cover over the crooked red mark. The constable returned. She handed him the folder without ceremony. He carried it to the shelf against the far wall. The path file joined others, civilian disputes that came and went, documented and shelved, the row of buff folders like a cemetery of arguments the state had witnessed without understanding. He placed it between a property complaint and a domestic matter from last month. Done.

Her desk cleared.

Pen capped. The notebook's edge visible when she pushed the drawer shut, arrows inside, unnamed, filed with everything else she couldn't officially use. The weight lifted in her body the way paperwork lifted when it stopped being your problem. Shoulders lower. The desk breathed.

She looked at the empty space where the file had sat for ten days.

Ten days ending in a crooked stamp and a shelf and a desk that could hold the next file, the next contradiction, the next village that would solve itself before she finished the paperwork.

Compound outside: constables moving, a bike starting somewhere, puddles shrinking in bright clearing light, a vendor's voice distant beyond the compound wall. The village was quiet. Not because of her stamp. Her file caught up to its quiet, late. Social peace had already happened somewhere she hadn't seen, on ground her forms didn't map. The state recorded. It did not create.

She did not think about why the village had quieted. She only knew: they stopped pursuing. That was enough for the file. That was all the file would ever hold.

## S02 — Quiet Now

Farooq's store smelled of soap and spice.

Priya walked the fifteen minutes from the station with her desk clear and the morning's procedural weight already settling into the background where paperwork went when it was done. Bright street. Warm breeze. The town drying after rain the way everything dried, gradually, without announcement. Auto-rickshaws at the stand. A woman buying matches from a vendor near the bus depot. Ordinary Tuesday commerce resuming around the silence the path fight had occupied for weeks without most of these people knowing it by name.

The store interior was familiar. Bottles lined on shelves, rice sacks stacked against the wall, packets of dal and spice arranged with the care of a man who knew his regulars. Counter facing the door. Bright street visible through the glass. The geography of a town small enough that police and shopkeepers recognized each other without intimacy. She had been here before in other contexts. Provisions for the station, change for the compound canteen, the ordinary errands that made a police officer a person in a town before they were a uniform.

Today: civilian errand. Biscuits for lunch, or for the station, or for no reason except that buying something small was what people did when a thing ended.

Farooq behind the counter. Warm. Easy. The grin of a man who didn't need anything from her and didn't fear her, the particular ease of a shopkeeper who had watched police come and go for years and had learned the difference between a file and a person.

"The cream ones?"

"These ones."

She pointed at the packet in the tin. He reached for it. Small coins on the counter, exact change, no bargaining, no performance. Transaction brief. Normal.

The tin on the counter, lid ajar, cream and plain visible inside, the same container that had sat here for years serving whoever came through the door with small hunger and small coins. She noticed it the way you noticed furniture in a familiar room. Same tin. Different buyer. Same ordinary.

He wrapped the packet. Cellophane crackle. The store smelling of provisions and the particular cleanliness of a man who stacked soap with care.

"Quiet now, no?"

Observation. Not question. Not sage. Not oracle. A shopkeeper who had noticed the village was calmer. Fewer raised voices at the bus stand, less phone-forwarding at the counter, the particular temperature of a town when the thing everyone was watching stopped requiring watching. Two words plus the particle. Warm.

Priya looked at him.

"Quiet now."

Echoed. Agreement. Period. Neither added why. Neither said because of. Neither said finally. The observation confirmed. Done.

She took the biscuit packet. Farooq's grin held, ordinary, unchanged, professional warmth without depth or explanation. The tin lid still ajar on the counter behind him. Soap smell. Spice smell. The street bright through the door.

She walked out into afternoon light.

Bright street drying. Village resuming its unhurried rhythm. Biscuit packet in her hand, cellophane crackling slightly with each step. Behind her: the store, the grin, the open tin. Before her: warmth and ordinary and the small relief of being a person buying biscuits instead of a file-closer stamping crooked red marks on buff folders that would join a shelf and wait to be forgotten.

Quiet now.

Both of them had said it. Neither needed to say more.

She walked toward the compound with biscuits and the ordinary afternoon ahead.
