# Chapter 20 — Jasmine in Steel Bowl

## S01 — Extra Jasmine

Meera's hands were in dishwater when the morning decided what kind of day it would be.

Coriander on the board, chopped fine, the knife rhythm Saroja liked, the green smell mixing with soap and the last of yesterday's sambar clinging to a pot edge. Saroja dictated a shopping list across the kitchen with the authority of a woman who had been managing this household's hunger long enough to treat hunger as a logistics problem.

"Thin dal, not the fat one. The good tamarind if Farooq has it. Soap that does not smell like the cheap soap — you know which."

Revathi complained about onion prices with the energy of a woman who needed somewhere to put her annoyance that was not the path and not the men and not the silence that had settled between houses since the courtyard meeting. "Thirty rupees for the small ones now. They think we grow money in the yard."

Tea steamed on the small table. The fan moved humid air without improving it. Pre-monsoon haze pressed against the windows. Men were away. Fields, town, the usual geography that left the kitchen to women and their work and their talk. Meera rinsed a plate, set it on the rack, reached for another. Water cool against her fingers. The ordinary friction of a household morning. No mystery, no weight beyond the ordinary weight of dishes and lists and the price of onions.

The village had resumed routine after Bengaluru. Her body had recovered from buses. Her mind carried the AGM's half-smile like a private joke she could not explain to anyone here without sounding like a city cousin who had discovered obvious things and mistaken them for wisdom.

Somewhere a rooster. Somewhere a scooter that needed silencing. The house held its breath in the particular way houses breathed when the men's quarrel was still present but not being performed. Present in closed doors, in shorter answers, in the women's willingness to keep chores loud enough to fill silence.

She wiped her hands on the towel. Saroja added something about chili powder. Revathi argued for a different brand. The morning continued.

Gayatri arrived at the courtyard door around half past ten with the ease of someone who belonged in doorways.

"I was at the temple," she called. "These were extra. Thought you'd want them."

Jasmine in a steel bowl. White flowers packed loose, fragrant, the smell stronger in the pre-monsoon moisture that made everything bloom a little louder than it needed to. She was middle-aged, cotton saree practical rather than ceremonial, body language confident in the unremarkable way of village women who moved through houses as naturally as weather. Meera filed her: wedding periphery maybe, someone's aunt, not important enough to have stuck until now.

Gayatri set the bowl on the counter. Tea offered. Accepted. The women settled into the circle that morning tea created. Unhurried, domestic, the social texture of a visit that required no announcement and no apology.

"How was the queue?" Saroja asked.

"Long. But the flowers were generous today." Gayatri arranged a stem between her fingers without performing delicacy. Just the habit of hands that knew flowers. "The fabric store raised prices again. Revathi, you'll have a heart attack."

"I already had one," Revathi said. "Thirty rupees for onions. Now fabric. We should all wear gunny sacks."

Laughter. Easy, real. Temple timings discussed. Whether the rains would come early this year. A neighbour's son's exam results. Gayatri laughed at the right moments. Asked the right small questions. Did not hurry. Did not linger with the intensity of someone who wanted something. Just the unhurried presence of a woman dropping by because dropping by was what women did when men were in fields and the house had room for talk.

Jasmine distributed into portions. A handful for the pooja room, some carried toward Lakshmi's door by Saroja's hand, stems arranged on the kitchen counter where the smell would linger and attach itself to curtains and hair and the day's memory whether anyone planned it or not.

Meera dried her hands and sat with her tea.

Saroja mentioned the shopping again. Soap, dal, the thin kind. Revathi volunteered to walk to Farooq's later if the sun stayed manageable. Normal logistics. Normal morning. The kind of morning that made you forget, for minutes at a time, that fathers on both sides were not speaking properly and police files still existed in drawers and weddings had happened under conditions that were not peace so much as truce with garlands.

Gayatri asked about the temple festival preparations with genuine interest. Saroja answered. Revathi had opinions about which committee handled flowers badly every year. The conversation moved like water. Finding the lowest point, settling, moving on.

"How is the Gowda girl settling?" Gayatri asked Saroja, casual as weather. One sentence among many, embedded in the flow of visit-talk the way a stone was embedded in a stream. Visible if you looked, invisible if you didn't. "Karthik's wife now — she must be adjusting."

Saroja's face softened. The guard that Ramesh's silence had built around that subject seemed to thin in the women's morning.

"Fine, I think. Ramesh won't ask but..." She trailed off, looked at her tea, found the rest without drama. "I might send something."

Revathi leaned in immediately. Bridge-builder by nature, amplifier by habit, the woman who heard an opening and walked through it because walking through openings was what she did when men were busy being proud in other rooms.

"Let's send the coconut pickle. Their side likes it. The one with extra chili — not too much, the way Venki's mother taught us."

Saroja nodded, already planning. Container. Who would carry it. Whether tomorrow or the day after. The conciliatory gesture forming in real time, born not from command but from an opening someone had left in the conversation without appearing to leave anything at all.

Gayatri did not push. Did not say *what a good idea*. Did not claim the moment or look pleased in the way of someone who had scored. She nodded once and returned to fabric prices while Saroja and Revathi built the pickle mission between them with the warmth of women who had been waiting for permission to be warm and had not known they were waiting until the permission arrived dressed as a question about a daughter-in-law.

More tea poured. Temple gossip resumed. Gayatri mentioned a sari she had seen in town. Colour, price, whether it was worth it. Revathi had opinions. Saroja had opinions. Someone's grandson had failed maths. Someone else's cow had calved. The visit continued on its ordinary rails. The rails that made Meera wonder, later, how anything important could happen on rails so familiar you did not notice the switch until you had already changed direction.

Meera helped distribute the last of the jasmine. Fingers brushing cool petals, the smell attaching to skin. She said nothing that would mark her as observing too closely. City training: do not make the room conscious of the observer. Village training, newer and still awkward: pay attention anyway.

The visit wound down the way normal visits wound down. Rising, collecting the steel bowl now empty, warm goodbye at the courtyard door.

"I'll come again when the fresh batch is ready."

She left. Footsteps fading. The bowl gone with her. Empty, clean, practical, the kind of object that belonged to every kitchen and meant nothing except what the morning had used it for.

Saroja was already at the shelf looking for the right container. Not too small, not the one with the cracked lid. Revathi discussing whether to add extra chili. The room was two degrees warmer than it had been when Gayatri walked in.

She tried to replay the visit and could not find the hinge. Only the door opening, the question about Karthik's wife, the pickle rising out of Saroja as if it had been waiting under tea leaves. Nothing dramatic. Nothing that would sound like anything if she described it at dinner in Bengaluru. And yet the room was warmer. The pickle was planned. Something had moved.

Jasmine everywhere now. Pooja room, kitchen counter, a few petals escaped to the stone near the sink. The steel bowl's absence on the counter where it had briefly sat full. Saroja planning pickle. Revathi humming. The ordinary morning continuing as if something small and important had not just happened in plain sight while everyone was looking.

Meera stood with tea cooling in her cup and the quiet alarm of having watched a room change temperature without anyone announcing the weather.

## S02 — Two People, Two Rooms

The courtyard was empty when she sat down.

Women dispersed to other rooms. Saroja to the shelf, Revathi to the stove, the morning splitting back into tasks. Midday approaching. Jasmine in the air, stronger than it should have been for so few flowers, the humidity carrying scent into corners and making the courtyard feel briefly like a place that had been chosen for beauty rather than for arguments.

On the courtyard ledge Gayatri's steel bowl sat empty, cleaned, left behind or forgotten in the way practical objects were left when they had done their work and did not require ceremony to depart.

Meera sat on the low wall where the stone held the day's warmth.

*That woman came, talked about nothing, and something changed.*

The sentence formed clearly now that Gayatri was gone. Observation, not analysis. The kind you made when you did not yet have vocabulary for what you were seeing and had to use the vocabulary of ordinary life instead.

Then the other memory arrived. Uninvited, parallel.

The courtyard in a different configuration. Chairs. Men. An old man who knew dates and trees and words no one else remembered with that particular precision that made denial feel childish. A room that changed temperature when he spoke. Fathers flinching. Tea going cold. Something shifted that did not shift back when he left and Lakshmi followed him inside without explanation.

Two people. Two visits. Neither announced. Neither explained. Neither asking for thanks.

*Is this a pattern? Or am I imagining it?*

One instance was a person. Two instances was maybe a line. Maybe coincidence. Maybe the paranoia of a city cousin who had been living too long inside other people's arguments and was now finding patterns in jasmine and tea the way tired minds found faces in clouds.

Asking anyone would cost something. Ask the wrong person and she sounded paranoid. Ask the right person and she might break a peace nobody had asked her to protect, might turn a soft morning into an interrogation, might make herself the kind of visitor who needed everything explained because explanation felt like control.

She could tell Saroja. She could mention Gayatri to Revathi. She could send a text to Nav with a question that would sound half-crazy out of context. Each option had a price. Each price was hers to pay if she chose it.

She chose not to.

Lakshmi passed through carrying an empty cup.

Her face was soft today. The jasmine had reached her room. Meera could see it in the warmth around her eyes, the particular gladness that was also a wall. Not hostile. Not inviting. Present the way old trees were present. Offering shade without offering entry. She moved through the courtyard without hurrying, without stopping, the cotton saree familiar, the footsteps brief on stone.

Meera almost spoke.

*Ajji, who is Gayatri-aunty?*

The question formed completely. Her mouth stayed closed.

Not from fear of the answer. From respect for the peace itself. Lakshmi's softness was rare. The jasmine had given her something visible, the loosening around the mouth, the absence of the guarded watchfulness Meera had learned to read without naming. To ask now would be to solve. To solve would be to own. She had spent weeks trying to understand and fix. This morning she recognized the instinct and stopped it. She let Lakshmi pass. Let the question stay inside where it would have to live until another day offered a different cost.

Lakshmi's back disappeared into the house. The same visual as weeks ago when the old man left and the courtyard emptied and Meera sat with questions she could not yet afford to ask aloud.

Silence returned. Bird. Distant pot. Fan hum from the kitchen window. The empty steel bowl on the ledge. Practical, clean, unremarkable. Jasmine scent everywhere, clinging to humidity. Meera's unasked question sitting beside her like a third presence in the courtyard.

The pattern was visible only from the angle where she happened to be sitting.

She was not sure she had chosen the seat on purpose. Unease settled. Not crisis, not revelation. Attention sharpened. The morning's beauty and the morning's disturbance coexisting the way green rows and a cut thumb could coexist for a man she had not spoken to today and would not ask about tonight. Something was happening in this village that did not announce itself. She had two data points and no permission to call it a line.

She sat a while longer in the humid air and the jasmine and the not-knowing.

Then she went inside to help with lunch, the question folded small in her chest, carried like a seed that might grow or might not, depending on what the soil decided without consulting her.
