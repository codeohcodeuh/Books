# Chapter 17 — Cutting Leaf

## S01 — Between Rows

The sugarcane stood in grey morning light like something that had not been told about yesterday.

Ramesh had left before anyone woke. Before Saroja could put a plate in his hands, before the courtyard could look at him the way the courtyard had looked at him since the chairs were carried back inside and the tea went cold in cups nobody finished. Old shirt. Old trousers. Feet in chappals that knew these rows better than the house floor. He had not eaten. His stomach was a closed fist. Shame sat in his chest like heat that had nowhere to go.

Between the rows the world was green against cloud.

Dew clung to the leaves in beads that caught what little light the sky offered. Yesterday's cuts still breathed at the field's edges: sweet, green, slightly fermented where the stalks had dried open in the humidity. Earth damp underfoot. Birds early in the margins, unconcerned with human arrangements. A myna scolded something in the neem line. The cane taller than his shoulder in places, enclosing him the way walls enclosed a room, except these walls moved when the wind moved, and the wind did not ask who owned what.

He walked because standing in the house had become impossible.

Not toward anything. Back and forth along the lines he had planted and tended and counted as proof that he was a man who kept what was his. The rustle of leaves when he passed. His own breathing. Silence between the rows that was not peace, just absence of audience. Nobody could see him from the road. Nobody could see him from the porch. That was the point.

The beauty registered before the shame found its full voice.

That was the cruelty and the mercy of fields. They continued. The cane did not lean toward his grief. The dew did not dry out of sympathy. Green against grey, row after row, the crop doing what crops did regardless of what had been said in a courtyard he could not yet face.

*Let the path be wider than our pride.*

The words from yesterday. Not his voice. Mallappa's voice, carried through another man's mouth with a precision that still made Ramesh's teeth ache when he replayed it.

His father's words. The words he had built a life on without ever having heard them whole until a stranger spoke them in his courtyard while both families sat with their tea going cold and their certainties draining out through the cracks.

*I became the pride.*

The recognition arrived without ceremony. His fence. His certainty. His *our path* spoken over and over at every gathering, every boundary dispute, every moment when sharing would have cost him face and narrowing gave him a story. All of it the tightening his father had warned against. He was what Mallappa had cautioned Chandrappa about. Actually. The son who heard *share* and translated it into *hold* because holding felt like protection and protection felt like love.

He snapped a leaf. The sound was small and sharp. His hands needed occupation. His mind would not grant it.

Maybe the man was wrong. Maybe memory bent with age and old men polished stories until they shone with virtue nobody living had witnessed. Ramesh tried to hold that thought.

The tamarind tree killed it.

He remembered that tree. Not from a story told at a wedding. From his body. It had stood where the old men said it stood, at the bend where the path narrowed and the shade fell thick in afternoons. He had been young when it fell in '88, young enough that his back still remembered the work of clearing it, the sour smell of broken wood, the cursing in two dialects of exhaustion, Venki's father swearing at a root that would not release while Ramesh's own hands bled slightly on bark that did not care. Shankarappa had not guessed. He had named a thing Ramesh's own palms had touched.

*Let neither of us need a document for what our sons should remember.*

Sons should remember. He was the son. He had not remembered. He had inherited a dispute he thought he understood and discovered, in front of everyone, that his understanding was the dispute.

Hours passed. The grey sky did not change its mind. Humidity pressed against his skin until his shirt clung damp at the back and his throat went dry and still he did not think about food because thinking about food would mean thinking about the kitchen and Saroja's eyes avoiding his.

Venki would be processing too. Ramesh did not think about Venki long. Thinking about the other man's shame would have been a relief, and relief was not available to him today.

He passed the same row markers twice, three times, losing count on purpose. The cane did not care how many loops a man made when his mind refused a straight line. A grasshopper jumped from a cut stalk. Phone left at home on purpose. He had not wanted to be reached, had not wanted Arjun's voice or Saroja's worry or any human sound that would require him to perform being a man who knew what came next.

His hand dragged along a row without attention, fingers brushing the sharp edges the way you brushed a wall in darkness.

The leaf caught his thumb. Not dramatic, not a wound, the slow paper-cut kind sugarcane gave when you treated it carelessly. Thin green blade. A line of blood appearing clean and small against brown skin. He stopped. Looked at it.

Absurd. One breath where the loop broke and air entered his lungs as air instead of accusation. The shame had peaked. It had not lifted. It had simply stopped climbing. Present now. Heavy. Alive. Part of him the way the cut was part of his thumb. Minor, real, not requiring a bandage so much as acknowledgment.

He turned toward the house.

The rows behind him. Grey sky above them. Beauty and hurt in the same frame, neither canceling the other. He had not solved anything. He had not spoken to anyone since yesterday evening. He had walked until his body interrupted his mind, and now he could walk home. Not forgiven, not restored, just capable of re-entering the geography of his life.

Blood drying in a thin line as he went.

## S02 — Just Write It Down

The back porch held him the way a chair held a man who had finally stopped moving.

Ramesh sat with the field smell still on his clothes. Cut stalk, sweat, the particular damp of a morning that never decided to be morning. His thumb throbbed faintly where the blood had dried into a thin brown line. The phone in his hand felt heavier than phones were supposed to feel. Messages waited. He did not read them yet. Saroja was somewhere inside, giving him space the way she gave him space when she knew words would not help and food would be rejected and silence was the only kindness available.

The courtyard was visible from the angle of his chair. Empty now, ordinary, the ghost of yesterday's meeting present only in the way empty chairs could be present if you had sat in them when your story broke.

It rang.

"Appa." Arjun's voice, Mysuru clear through the speaker, professional edges softened by concern that did not know how to enter a village shame without bringing a spreadsheet. "I heard about what happened yesterday. Are you all right?"

The grapevine had done its work. Cousins. WhatsApp. The family architecture that moved news faster than bullock carts and with less mercy.

"I'm here," Ramesh said.

A pause on the line. Arjun had learned to read his father's *I'm here* the way you read weather. Not as forecast but as current condition.

"If he says there were terms, put it in writing now. Get both sides to sign." The words came fast. The modern answer, clean and confident, the voice of a man who solved problems by making them legible to systems that did not care about tamarind trees or fathers' warnings. "Written agreement. Survey adjustment. Legal standing. Then this can't happen again, Appa. Boundaries. Writing. Signatures."

Ramesh listened.

Part of him wanted it. After yesterday, paper sounded like a wall you could stand behind. Certainty. Protection. A way to stop the ambiguity from eating the rest of his life. He could almost feel the relief of it. Names on a page, boundaries measured, signatures drying in ink while the oral memory that had failed him became someone else's problem.

Another part heard his father underneath.

*What our sons should remember.*

The sons were supposed to remember. He was the son. He had not. And now his son was asking for a document because the sons had failed, and accepting that document would be admitting the failure in a language the failure itself had made necessary.

"I'll think," Ramesh said.

"Think about what? This is simple."

Silence on the porch. A fan turning somewhere inside. Cooking smell, something with coconut, that belonged to a house continuing its day without waiting for his soul to catch up. The field direction visible beyond the rail. The same rows he had walked, the place where two old men once agreed without anyone writing it down, where trust was the instrument because paper would have insulted the trust.

"It wasn't supposed to need that."

The sentence left his mouth before he knew it was coming. His father's position surfacing through him like water finding a crack in a tank that had held the wrong water for years. He heard himself say it and went still with surprise.

Arjun went quiet. Not angry quiet. Processing quiet, the quiet of a practical man encountering a principle he could not file.

"Okay, Appa." Warm again. Careful. "Just think about it. I'm here if you need."

"Yes."

The call ended. Not hostile. Unresolved, the way things ended when two people loved each other and lived in different centuries and both were right in their own weather.

Ramesh set the phone on the porch rail.

His thumb, dried blood in a thin line, rested against the warm wood. He looked toward the field. Toward the direction where the tamarind had once stood. Toward the absence of any document that had ever held what those men agreed to, and the absence that had failed their sons anyway.

Both things true. Both things his. The problem and the principle sharing the same mouth.

Paper would end the ambiguity. Paper would also end the faith. The faith he had violated, the faith his father had asked the sons to carry without ink. Arjun was not wrong from Mysuru. Arjun was simply standing in a world where promises without signatures were considered naive, and Ramesh was standing in a world where yesterday had proved that the sons' naivety was not the old men's fault alone.

He touched his thumb without thinking. The dried line stung faintly.

He sat alone with them until the grey afternoon thickened and the house moved around him, a door closing, water running, the ordinary machinery of family, and he did not move, and Saroja, somewhere inside, let him be.
