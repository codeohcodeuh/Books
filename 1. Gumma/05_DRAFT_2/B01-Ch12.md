# Chapter 12 — Aligned Sandals

## S01 — The Dull Guest

The week between police and wedding eve had been a cold war dressed as preparation.

Thursday: complaint filed; Meera helping Saroja with garland strings, Ramesh returning tight-lipped from the station, path topic avoided at dinner. Friday: vendor lists, chair counts, muhurtham timing, both families polite-cold in shared spaces. Saturday: errands in town, flowers and sweets, the path visible from the road, empty, wire still up. Sunday: relatives arriving early; Meera reading in the corridor; Venki's side mentioned in low voices she did not pursue. Monday: henna prep for younger cousins, kitchen overdrive. Tuesday: final decoration check; Ramesh and Venki passing at the gate without greeting. She noticed, said nothing, filed it beside Raju's bruised face. Wednesday: seating chart arguments resolved by Saroja's notebook. Thursday: last full prep day, Ananya's wedding-night room packed, tension everywhere but no new incident. Wedding proceeding on schedule because stopping it would require an explosion no one was ready to detonate.

Meera's city sandals were still clean. The week had not required her to walk the path.

Friday evening. Wedding eve.

The Hegde courtyard had been transformed. Tube-lights strung overhead in loops that made the sky irrelevant, turning on early against the warm dark, food being served from vessels that steamed and smelled of the particular abundance that preceded a ceremony. Rice. Sambar. Sweet. Pickle. Fried items in newspaper cones. The geometry of a feast that said *we are fine* louder than any speech. Women in the kitchen still bringing replacements for emptied vessels. Men near the senior row holding cups they refilled for each other with the ritual politeness of alliance. Children underfoot until corralled. The ordinary chaos of a house that had decided, for one evening, to perform unity.

Thirty, maybe forty people. Both families. Hegde sarees and Gowda dhotis and the bright synthetic colours of younger cousins who treated weddings as festivals they had earned by being born into the right generation.

Ananya in the centre of attention, performing bride-joy with a brightness that felt genuine underneath the performance. Meera could see it, the way you saw someone choosing joy when anger had been an option and had been set aside for tomorrow. A tightness around the eyes that released when she laughed. But the laugh was real when it came.

Meera circulated. Festive churidar. Saroja had insisted, *not office clothes, not black, not mourning colours, we are not mourning, we are marrying*. Helping with serving, exchanging pleasantries she had learned to deliver without meaning all of them. *Congratulations. Looking beautiful. So happy for you.* The phrases of wedding-eve fluency, spoken into a room that needed fluency to keep from examining what lay underneath.

Performing belonging while the path-violence sat in her memory like a bruise on Raju's face she had not been given the story for. Ramesh had come back from the station twice and returned each time tighter, the way men returned from places where paper recorded what bodies had done. Families strained. Tonight was truce. Fragile, necessary, the kind of peace that existed because tomorrow required it and because both sides had invested too much rice and garland string to detonate now.

She passed Ananya near the food. The bride laughing at something a cousin said, head thrown back, bangles catching tube-light. Brief warmth. Then she picked up another stack of plates and moved on.

Ramesh performed host-dignity near the senior row, refilling cups he did not drink from, greeting Karthik's father with the correct amount of warmth. Tight underneath. Saroja logistics overdrive with a cheerful surface that could crack if you looked too long. She moved between kitchen and courtyard like a general who believed food could win wars. Karthik's family polite-cold in the way alliance families were polite-cold when the alliance was costing more than anyone had priced. Everyone smiled too hard. Meera saw it. Could not fix it. Served another plate.

She passed the door where footwear accumulated. Chappals, sandals, slippers in the democratic scatter of a crowd that did not think about feet. She served. Smiled. Noticed without staring, or tried to.

Around six-thirty, a guest arrived.

Quietly. No announcement. No drum. No relative calling his name across the courtyard. Gray hair, simple dhoti, clean but unremarkable, rubber chappals removed at the threshold with a neatness that would have been invisible except that Meera had already passed the door twice and registered the scatter of everyone else's footwear against something different forming at the edge.

Three elders from both sides paused their conversations.

Not dramatically. Head turns. Hegde side, Gowda side, the rare simultaneity of attention that Meera had not seen all evening even when Ananya's name was spoken. Slightly more formal greetings than the stream of relatives had received. A nod deeper, a *avare* where *anna* might have sufficed, voices lowering half a tone as if volume itself were respect. Space made near the senior row without being asked. A chair adjusted, a gap opened, someone shifting to make room without appearing to make room, the social geometry of people who knew where someone belonged before anyone said the word.

Meera noticed. Started to stare. Caught herself. Wedding-eve etiquette punished curiosity. The city cousin who stared too long at elders' micro-deferences would become a story by tomorrow, whispered between serving rounds: *She watches too much. Bengaluru habit.* She looked away. Busied herself with serving. Plates, spoons, the performance of busyness that was also real work.

Returned her glance when she could do it as part of movement, not inspection. Carrying empties back toward the kitchen, passing the senior row at an angle that looked like purpose.

The guest looked dull.

That was her word for him, arriving without judgment and without admiration. Unremarkable. The kind of older man who existed in every gathering as furniture with a plate. Quiet. Present. Not performing. Not loud. Gray hair. Clean dhoti. Nothing that explained why Hegde and Gowda elders had reoriented like flowers toward light that did not seem bright to her.

Lakshmi's hand paused on the serving spoon.

One beat too long when she saw him. The ladle mid-air, sambar cooling, Lakshmi's eyes finding the guest and holding one fraction longer than serving required. Then resumed. Only Meera saw it. Others occupied, noise covering the pause. But the pause had happened. Lakshmi knew something.

Subbanna passed with refills, already over-generous with hospitality in the way of uncles who measured love in second helpings and would be wounded if you refused.

"Another helping? Please. You're family tonight."

The guest declined gently. One plate only. Thank you, no.

Later, Meera did not see the interval, only the result. Tea. She passed the senior row with a tray and saw him accept a cup and set it down on the saucer with exact centering, the handle aligned, the cup's weight distributed as if the saucer were a diagram. Precision that could be personality or discipline, habit or care, unreadable to an observer who did not know what to look for.

She filed it. Moved on. Served another plate. Exchanged congratulations with a cousin she barely remembered from childhood visits. *You've grown*, *you've grown too*, the ritual exchange of people who shared blood and not daily life. Ananya's genuine smile caught her for a moment when she passed the bride. Real warmth, chosen joy. Then she was moving again, circulating, the evening continuing around the question she could not articulate.

When she passed the door again on a return trip from the kitchen, she looked.

His sandals. Rubber chappals. Sat aligned at the threshold with geometric precision. Paired. Parallel. Toes pointing the same direction. Everyone else's footwear scattered in the ordinary chaos of a crowd. One chappal upside down, a sandal half under another, the democracy of people who removed shoes without thinking. The contrast visible. Not analyzable. Just there.

A guest who mattered to old people for reasons she could not parse. Dull to her eye. Significant to theirs. No display. No volume. Just presence acknowledged by people who acknowledged few.

She returned to helping. The evening continued. Tube-lights humming. Plates clattering. Children chased near the gate until an aunt redirected them. Her body carried the observation without words. Sandals, spoon-pause, head-turns, tea cup centered. A question filed, not pursued.

Shankarappa's sandals aligned at the door. Everyone else's scattered. The contrast visible to Meera but not analyzable.

## S02 — Old Family Connection

People dispersed in the slow collapse of a wedding eve. Relatives finding rooms, children corralled toward sleep with the mixed success of sugar and excitement, the courtyard emptying in layers until the house settled into its night geometry. Tube-lights still on in places. Saroja would turn them off when she was satisfied no guest lacked anything. But the crowd's noise thinning to murmurs, to doors closing, to the particular quiet of a house that had performed festivity and was now allowed to be tired.

Meera moved toward the sleep room. Tired from performing. Face sore from smiling, shoulders sore from trays, the emotional fatigue of a woman who had spent five hours being visible in the right ways. Carrying the filed observations. The dull guest, the deference, Lakshmi's pause, the sandals. Curious but not urgent. The kind of curiosity that could wait until tomorrow, when curiosity would be impolite and she would be required to be radiant in photographs.

The corridor was dim. One tube-light distant, casting a pale rectangle on stone floor she remembered from arrival night. Lakshmi's door half-open, light from within, the shape of her aunt adjusting pillows or folding a shawl or performing the small domestic rituals of a woman who would not sleep until the house slept and who treated sleep as something you arranged for others before claiming for yourself.

Meera paused.

Lakshmi looked up. Warm acknowledgment. Half-asleep eyes that were nonetheless present. The particular attention of elders who heard footsteps and knew whose without turning.

"You noticed the Shankarappa-avare?"

The honorific landed. *Avare*, unusual in this house's casual register, marking respect Meera could hear but not parse. Not *anna*. Not the familiar diminutive of family banter. Something older. Something that closed distance by naming it.

"The quiet one?"

Lakshmi paused. Not long. A full stop arriving early.

"He's a guest. Old family connection. Sleep now."

Two sentences. Name given. Shankarappa, held in the honorific like something wrapped in cloth. Information withheld. *Old family connection* explained nothing and could mean anything. Conversation closed. The tone was not hostile. It was love expressed as a door shut gently, completely, before Meera could follow with the questions lining up behind her tongue.

She read the room. This was not a battle. This was bedtime. Lakshmi's authority in corridors at night was absolute. Meera did not press.

"Sleep now," Lakshmi said again. Three words returning without explanation, love expressed as directive, warmth and withholding in the same breath, kanamma unspoken but present in the tone.

The door closed further. Half-open to a crack. Light reduced to a line. Lakshmi's shape disappeared behind wood that knew how to hold secrets.

Meera walked on.

The corridor smelled of old cotton. Stored blankets, naphthalene, the particular closed sweetness of the sleep room breathing its stored air through an open door at the corridor's end. The smell entered her nose before her mind could name it. Body first. She did not reach for childhood. Did not assemble a name.

Faintly, from somewhere in the house, unlocatable, the soft slap of rubber chappal on stone.

Once.

Then silence.

The flicker passed through her body and left. Shoulders tightening, then releasing. Nose still full of blanket smell. Ears still holding the sound without locating it.

Corridor. Lakshmi's door closed to a crack. Sleep room ahead, door open, naphthalene breathing out.

Unease settled into her body as she walked. Not analyzed, not named, just held in the muscles that had been performing cheer all evening and were now allowed to stop performing. The wedding was tomorrow. Shankarappa was somewhere in this house tonight. Guest room assigned without fuss, sleeping arrangement made without comment she had heard. She had a name and nothing else.

She did not knock again on any door.

She walked toward the sleep room. The corridor held her without explanation. The house settled around her like a blanket she had not chosen and would not reject.
