# Chapter 19 — Scraping Chairs

## S01 — Six-Month Trial

Meera arrived late because traffic in Bengaluru did not care that she had already given this city a night on a bus seat that had memorized her spine.

She had left the office with her laptop still warm in her bag and her brain still running client sentences she would finish on the bus if the road allowed typing. The cousin's sofa, three hours of sleep before dawn, blanket that smelled of detergent and someone else's life, felt like a transaction she paid to keep her job and her village obligations in the same calendar. Coffee after. More coffee now, going cold. Her eyes burned in the fluorescent light like they had been sandpapered.

This was her life when she was not in the village. Client deadlines. Fluorescent offices. The particular competence of a woman who translated between systems that did not speak each other's languages and made the translation look effortless because effortlessness was billable. She had built something here. Not glamour. Work. A reputation for reliability. A salary that paid for bus tickets and rent and the cousin's sofa when the night service was the only way to make Monday morning.

The community room on the ground floor smelled of cleaning product and closed air and the faint rain-approaching electricity that pre-monsoon evenings carried through windows that did not quite seal. Fluorescent lights hummed. A whiteboard with the agenda copied in block letters: maintenance, CCTV, visitor parking, drainage. The liturgy of apartment democracy. Eight or ten plastic chairs arranged in loose rows facing a front area where the building manager stood with a pen and the expression of a man who had attended too many of these and would attend too many more.

Asha was already there. Papers aligned, posture aligned, the particular readiness of someone who had prepared to win an argument and would accept winning in installments if necessary.

Meera found a hard chair in the middle-back and sat. Her spine protested immediately. The bus from Sunday night, the cousin's sofa for three hours of sleep before a full Monday of client fire-drills and skipped lunch and back-to-back calls that had turned her eyes into dry sockets and her shoulders into knots she would feel for days. Coffee from the office cup sat cold in her hand. Her fingers trembled slightly. Fatigue, not nerves. Coffee wearing off. Lunch skipped. Body running on spite and the stubborn refusal to let Asha win by default through Meera's absence.

She wanted to be anywhere else. She wanted her return bus. She wanted the village, which was a strange want for a woman who had come to Bengaluru to escape the village's gravity, but her body was honest even when her ambitions were not.

The building manager called order.

Item one: maintenance hike. Ground floor versus upper floor allocation. Voices rising and settling in the predictable rhythm of people who shared a building and did not share a theory of fairness. Someone argued that ground-floor flats used more common electricity because of the lift lobby. Someone else argued that upper floors used more water pressure. The building manager wrote nothing down and nodded as if writing would commit him to a side he did not want. Meera watched without volunteering. She had learned that apartment governance rewarded the loud and exhausted the rest.

Her phone buzzed.

Nav: *Seriously ok?*

Earlier text from him, *still alive?* or similar, sat above it in the thread. The village chaos he did not know the details of. The ESOP storm he was living parallel to her own storm in a different costume. She typed between agenda items without looking fully at the screen. One thumb, quick, the way you answered someone who had been waiting while you were elsewhere.

*Alive. AGM hell. Reply later.*

Sent. Phone away. A brief warmth in her chest that was not hope, just contact, the proof that another life existed beyond this room, then back to fluorescent reality.

Item two: CCTV proposal.

Asha stood. Data. Three vendor quotes printed and highlighted. Her voice was rule-bright, prepared, the voice of a woman who believed systems existed because chaos needed defeating and chaos, in her experience, wore the face of people who parked creatively.

"Recent incidents in parking," she said, without naming anyone, without looking at Meera, "demonstrate why monitoring is necessary. We cannot rely on goodwill alone."

The prickliness arrived. Old, familiar, the old ghost of a sticker and a humiliation and Asha's blue ticks in the group chat. It faded in seconds. She was too tired for ego. Too tired to perform the person who had fought about a parking slot as if parking were the only shared space that mattered. The fatigue stripped her down to observation.

Residents argued. Privacy versus security. Cost per flat. Who would watch the footage. Who would be watched. A man in the second row worried about cameras seeing his guests. A woman near the front wanted cameras everywhere because her scooter had been scratched and nobody had admitted anything. Asha held her ground with numbers. The building manager looked at the clock.

Meera listened with new eyes. Not smarter eyes, just stripped ones.

The building manager called for order again when two voices overlapped. Someone near the window complained about the fan not working. Wrong meeting, wrong grievance, but grievance was grievance and the room received it with the tired patience of people who understood that everything connected if you stared at it long enough. Asha's jaw tightened when a resident suggested the CCTV budget could fund lift repairs instead. The compromiser shook his head. Not now, wrong item, stay on track.

Meera's spine sent a fresh protest when she shifted. She ignored it. Ignoring bodies was a skill Bengaluru had taught her and the village had reinforced.

This was the same fight.

Smaller scale. Same structure. Parking was a passage between places that both sides needed. CCTV was *who watches whom*. Maintenance was *who pays for what both use*. The vocabulary was different. The costume was different. No courtyard, no old men, no wedding pressure. But the animal underneath was the same one she had been living with for weeks in a village where men sat in chairs and spoke terms that changed the temperature of entire families while women made tea and pretended the tea was the point.

She almost smiled. Not triumph. Recognition, the amused kind that came when you were too exhausted to pretend you were above anything.

A resident Meera did not know, older, practical, the face of someone who had survived enough AGMs to know formatting mattered more than righteousness, raised a hand.

"Cameras in parking only. Not corridors. Six-month trial. We revisit with data."

Murmurs. Some relief. Some suspicion that trial meant forever in slow motion.

Asha hesitated. She had wanted full coverage. She had prepared for full coverage. Her papers said corridors. Her instinct said total visibility was the only visibility that counted.

Another resident added, gently: "Asha-ji, let's try six months. If it works, we expand. If not, we save money."

Not *you're wrong*. *Let's test first.* An exit that preserved dignity. Face-saving as method. A frame that let everyone stop arguing without declaring defeat.

Asha agreed. A small nod. Papers folded slightly, the presenter becoming a resident again.

The room moved on. Quickly. No celebration. No *I told you so*. Just: done. Chairs scraped on tile as people shifted. The sound of agreement arriving not from victory but from exhaustion and the right sentence at the right time.

Visitor parking rules, revised, debated, accepted with the speed of people who wanted dinner. Drainage, a complaint, a promise to look, a date for looking. Meera half-listened, the recognition settling in her body like a weight she had not known she was carrying until it found its proper shelf. She was amused. Slightly sad. Changed in a way that did not feel like improvement so much as clarity.

The meeting ended. People stood. More chairs scraping. Bodies moving on because bodies always moved on even when arguments deserved monuments nobody would build. The building manager collected papers with the relief of a man who had survived another month of democracy. Someone joked about dinner. Someone else complained about the lift again.

Meera stood with them, half-smile intact, spine still protesting, bus ticket in her bag like a promise she intended to keep before the night bus left without her.

## S02 — The Nod

The corridor was cooler than the meeting room. Wind finding cracks, pre-monsoon wet on the air when someone opened the building door below and the building exhaled.

Asha walked ahead, shoulders dropped in the universal posture of a person who had just survived two and a half hours of democracy. Tired. Not defeated. The fight had taken something out of her too. Meera could see it in the slower step, the papers no longer held like weapons.

Meera saw her and felt the small internal question arrive: keep walking, offer false warmth, or something else.

She chose the nod.

Not a smile. That would have been dishonest, would have suggested a friendship neither of them wanted or deserved. Not a scowl. That would have been unnecessary, would have restarted a war the AGM had politely ended. Not looking away. That would have been the old fight continuing by other means.

She nodded as they drew level. Minimal, deliberate, her head moving once while her feet kept moving. Two seconds. In passing.

Asha received it. Nodded back. Neither stopped. Neither spoke. They passed in the corridor's fluorescent light and continued toward their separate doors. Two women who shared a building and had stopped bleeding over a slab of concrete, which was not love and was not peace and was, somehow, enough.

In her flat Meera packed fast. Bag half-ready from the morning, essentials grabbed, toothbrush, charger, the spare kurti she kept for village visits when city clothes felt like a costume. Bus ticket confirmed on her phone screen. Departure time circled in her mind like a deadline that would not negotiate.

Five minutes. The flat looked back at her with the closed-up smell of a place she visited between other lives. She looked once at the kitchen counter, the balcony door, the muted apartment group chat she would not read tonight. The parking fight was over. Not won. Not lost. Truce, the minimum honest gesture between people who would coexist in shared space because coexistence was cheaper than war.

She was not who she was when this fight started. That was not triumph. That was fact. Slightly melancholic, slightly amusing, the way growth often was when you noticed it in a mirror you had not chosen.

The flat would wait. Asha would wait. The visitor parking rules would operate without her commentary. The six-month trial would run its clock whether she was in Bengaluru or in a village where trials were measured in generations and still did not produce clean verdicts.

She checked the bus ticket one more time. Platform, time, the night service that would put her back at the village by morning if the road cooperated. Cold rice in the fridge she did not eat. Keys. Bag zip. The small checklist of departure that city life had trained into muscle.

She closed the door. Locked it. Bag on shoulder. Corridor light behind her. The nod still in her shoulders like a small completed transaction her body remembered even when her mind moved on.

Bus ticket in pocket. Village tonight.

She was not sure she was better for any of it. She was sure it was true.
