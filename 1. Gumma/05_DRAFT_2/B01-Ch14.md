# Chapter 14 — Overturned Chair

## S01 — Won't Give One Inch

The courtyard still looked like a wedding.

Strings of dead lights hung from the neem branch where someone had hung them three days ago with optimism about evening photography. Plastic chairs scattered in configurations that suggested conversations had ended mid-sentence. Plates stacked on a table near the kitchen door. Leftover payasa crusting at the edges, rice hardened into architectural failure, the honest debris of a feast that had fed two hundred and would feed ants until someone washed everything tomorrow. Warm night air pressing against bare arms. Mosquitoes circling with the persistence of creatures who understood post-celebration blood was sweeter. Meera carried three plates toward the plastic table where another aunt was sorting what could be saved from what should be thrown, moving with the tired competence of a woman who had decided helping was easier than sleeping.

Post-wedding warmth lived in her shoulders.

The ceremony had been beautiful. Ananya's face at the fire. The laugh-cry that had made even Venki's side soften for one unguarded second before the fathers resumed their cold geometry. Meera had spent the day clearing plates, refilling water, accepting compliments about her Bengaluru job with the minimal answers that prevented follow-up questions. She had stood in the courtyard during the feast while children ran between tables, and for a few hours the wedding had felt like what weddings were supposed to feel like: a temporary country where disagreement suspended its passport.

She had allowed herself, briefly, to feel part of this family rather than adjacent to it. Nodding at cousins, folding chairs. Tired. Content. The performance had held through the day. Families had behaved. Ramesh stiff but present. Venki stiff but present. No scenes at the hall. The morning's *no scenes today* had held long enough for muhurtham and feast and photographs where everyone smiled at the camera while refusing to smile at each other. Maybe, she had thought an hour ago while stacking marigold that was already dying, maybe enough goodwill had been manufactured to survive the hangover.

Men's voices from the drinking circle near the compound wall.

Four, maybe five Hegde men. Not Ramesh, thank whatever kept uncles asleep when damage needed preventing. Seated on plastic chairs with a whisky bottle between them, glasses catching courtyard light, laughter rising and falling in the rhythm of post-celebration bonding. Normal post-wedding wind-down. Meera noted it and did not note it. Men drinking after weddings was weather, not news. She stacked her plates on the table edge and reached for two more from a chair someone had abandoned mid-meal.

Two Gowda-side guests crossed the courtyard toward the gate.

Tired politeness in their walk. The particular gait of people who had stayed long enough for form and now needed their vehicle before the road became fully dark. One carried a leftover box Saroja had insisted on. The other checked his phone for the driver. Meera stepped aside with her plates. One guest nodded. Minimal, correct. The other murmured a goodbye to someone near the kitchen that Meera did not catch. Departure timing. Dignified. Almost done. The truce's last metres.

The drunk relative's voice rose.

Not gradually. Suddenly, as if volume had been a switch he had been waiting to flip once the guests were close enough to hear but far enough that he could pretend he was speaking to his circle alone. Meera's steps slowed without her deciding to slow. Alert. Something off in the pitch. Expansive, satisfied, whisky-emboldened, chair-scooting closer to the group as if proximity to agreement could substitute for being right.

"Let them say what they want about the path." Glass lifted. Gesture broad. "My family has walked it for three generations. We won't give one inch. Wedding doesn't change that."

LOUD. Clear. Carrying across warm air to the Gowda guests mid-departure. Ten, fifteen metres, voice as weapon aimed at his circle but calibrated to wound beyond it.

Fifteen seconds of words. Truce destroyed.

The guests' shoulders stiffened. Both of them, simultaneously, the body language of people who had heard exactly what they heard and would not turn around to give the speaker the satisfaction of acknowledgment. Would not dignify it with confrontation. Pace quickened. Not running. Dignity maintained. But faster, shoes on compound stone, toward the gate where headlights already suggested a vehicle waiting. Door sound. Engine turning. Vehicle pulling away. Gone. Carrying the boast home to Venki's house where it would arrive before morning.

Meera froze.

Plates in her hands. Can't move. Looking at the drunk relative. Flushed, gesturing now with the glass, satisfied with himself, unaware of what he had done. And at the empty space where the Gowda guests had been, the night air still holding their stiffened shoulders like an afterimage.

He's saying this *now*?

The wedding was a costume. Performance ended, real position returned. Embarrassment bloomed. Secondhand, vicious, for him, for her family, for herself for believing even briefly that ceremony could rewrite what men said when the whisky came out. She was part of this family. This was her family member. Implicated by proximity, by shared name, by the plates in her hands that suddenly felt like evidence.

The drunk relative continued. Another man laughed. "Ayyyy!" Agreement without content, post-wedding social bonding mistaking volume for strength. A chair overturned as the relative's gesture overshot its audience, plastic hitting packed earth with a sound too loud for plastic, legs pointing at the sky like a small surrendered animal. No one righted it. No one told him to stop. The circle absorbed its own noise and called it courage. Obliviousness the worst part. He didn't even know what he'd done.

Meera put the plates down.

On the plastic table among whisky-glass rings. Brown circles on white, evidence of an evening that would not clean itself. Her hands needed to be empty. Body processing what mind had not finished arranging. Transition from witness to carrier.

She moved inside.

Away from the courtyard. Away from the overturned chair and the laughter and the damage floating in night air like smoke from a fire no one would admit to lighting. Carrying the knowledge without processing it yet. Not ready, not willing, the plates' weight replaced by something heavier that had no vessel.

Interior corridor. Half-closed door to an alcove where the light was dimmer than the courtyard and the house's sleeping geometry suggested privacy without promising it.

Glimpse. Three seconds, peripheral, the way you saw things when you were walking past and not stopping:

Karthik's hand on Ananya's shoulder. Both still. Ananya still in wedding-day exhaustion. Meera could see the crease in her saree, the loosened jasmine. But upright, present. Karthik's face not visible from this angle; only his hand, steady, and Ananya's profile turned slightly toward him. Ananya's exhale. Half laugh, half surrender. And a murmured fragment: "We'll manage."

Not path. Not fathers. Not the cousin's voice still ringing in Meera's ears from the courtyard she had fled. Two people choosing each other in a house that had just proved the alliance was poisoned. The marriage real in a way the ceremony's applause had been performance and this private stillness was not.

Meera did not stop. Did not comment. She passed. The corridor accepted her movement. The courtyard sounds faded behind her like a radio turning down. Moving toward sleep room. Heavy. Everything she had seen tonight. None of it good.

Except that they chose each other.

Overturned plastic chair in the dismantled courtyard. Whisky-glass rings on the table. The space where Gowda guests had been, empty now, vehicle's sound fading into night. Meera inside, carrying the damage.

## S02 — Held Alone

The sleep room was dark before she turned on nothing.

She entered. Closed the door enough for privacy. Crack of corridor light, fan above spinning shadows. Lay on the bed. Sat first on the edge, then lay, the sequence of a body that had been standing too long with plates and shock. And let the dark arrange itself around her. Shapes resolving slowly: bed against wall, stored trunk in corner, window showing night-pale sky. Courtyard sounds still audible through stone. Laughter from the drinking circle, fainter now, persistent, the night continuing without her participation. Mosquito somewhere near the window screen. Distant cricket. The house's ordinary night sounds layered over catastrophe.

The boast replayed.

*"Won't give one inch."* His voice. Drunk satisfaction. Three generations. Wedding doesn't change that. The overturned chair she could picture without seeing. Legs up, plastic cheapness exposed, no one caring enough to right it because righting it would require admitting something had been knocked over that mattered.

Tell Ramesh?

If she told him, he would wake angry and perform the uncle's appropriate rage in the morning. The Gowda guests had already left with the words in their pockets. Damage done. Telling added noise. Did not undo.

If she didn't tell, she carried it.

Tomorrow she would know something Ramesh didn't know yet. They would report. Venki would know by morning. The truce dead because a man with whisky in his blood had said what the family believed when it forgot to perform otherwise. Meera had no standing to fix that. Tonight she had even less. Only darkness, only the choice of whether to wake her uncle with news that would change nothing except the quality of his sleep.

She would not tell tonight.

Not paralysis. Decision. This was what it meant to be in this family rather than observing it from the city.

One slow breath.

In. Out. Body resetting after the spike. Not hope, not resolution. Just the organism choosing sleep because sleep was the only action that did not make anything worse.

Dark room. Courtyard sounds fading. The overturned chair vivid in her mind though she couldn't see it. She pulled the stored cotton up. Naphthalene and old cloth. And let the embarrassment settle into her body.

She lay still. Fan blade shadows moved across the ceiling. The house slept around the knowledge she had chosen to hold alone. She knew; Ramesh did not. Tomorrow would arrive with consequences she could not yet see.
