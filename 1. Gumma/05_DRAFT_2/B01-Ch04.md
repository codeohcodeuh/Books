# Chapter 4 — Wire Shift

## S01 — Fresh Cement

Ramesh left the house before the women had finished with the stove.

Chappals on the washed step. Walking stick in the right hand because the left knee remembered last year's harvest and liked to announce itself on cold dawns, even when the dawn was not cold, only dry, only April, only the particular hour when heat had not yet chosen its full arrogance. The sky was still deciding between night and day: a pale band over the eastern fields, mist lying low on the sugarcane like breath that had not yet been claimed. Pre-monsoon. The kind of morning that punished anyone who waited until eight and then pretended surprise.

He took the path he always took.

Red earth under the rubber of his chappals, packed hard by years of feet and cart wheels and the stubbornness of routes that existed before anyone needed a sentence to defend them. To his right, Hegde cane, his cane, stood in rows that would need water soon and did not yet look thirsty enough to panic. The leaves held a dull green that meant the night had been kind. To his left, beyond the wire, Gowda land began with the same crop and a different accounting of pride. Between them, the path: narrow enough to keep a man honest, wide enough that two people could pass without becoming enemies, wide enough that a loaded cart could take the turn without scraping dignity off either side.

Birds started their noise as if someone had switched them on, not melody, work. A distant pump coughed and failed and coughed again, the sound of a motor arguing with sediment. Somewhere a dog barked once and thought better of it. Ramesh's body knew this walk the way it knew swallowing. Check the stand. Check the channel openings. See what the night had done to edges and intentions. Wedding in sixteen days sat somewhere behind his thoughts like a date circled in someone else's handwriting, Saroja's calls about flowers and cousins, the alliance with Venki's people that made every smile in the house a little tighter, Ananya moving through rooms as if the air itself were being measured. He did not carry the wedding into the field if he could help it. The field was older than alliance. The field did not care who sat where at a feast.

He walked.

Mist burned off in patches, showing the earth's true colour, that iron red that stained hems and ankles and the ends of arguments. His eye ran the cane the way a hand runs a child's fever: looking for wrongness without wanting to find it. A yellowing leaf here. A break in the planting line there, nothing urgent, the ordinary imperfections of living crop. The stick tapped once against a stone and continued. His shoulder remembered Mandya, two days of hardware shops, bolts, the cousin's too-sweet tea, sleeping in a room that smelled of mothballs and scooter oil. He had come back yesterday evening. This was the first proper dawn walk since.

Then the path felt wrong underfoot.

Not dramatically. Not a hole, not a washout. A compression, as if the ground had been asked to do the same work in less space. His stride shortened without instruction from his mind. The stick found earth a fraction sooner than habit expected. He stopped.

Looked at the wire.

The fence ran along the Gowda edge the way it always had, except it did not. Six inches. Eight, maybe. Inward. Toward him. Toward Hegde walking space. The kind of shift a hurried man might miss and a man who had walked this line since his father's funeral would feel in the ankle before he had a word for it. He stepped closer, the mist thinning around his knees. Followed the wire with his eyes: old posts, familiar rust, the particular lean of one that had been reset after the '19 storm when the whole belt had looked like a broken broom. Then, newer wood. Cleaner cut. A post that did not belong to the memory of this fence, pale where the others were dark, upright with the innocence of something that had not yet learned weather.

He crouched.

Knees complained in the language of age. He ignored them. At the base of the new post, cement. Pale against the red earth. Smooth where a trowel had finished its work, a small apron of grey holding the wood as if permanence could be poured. He put his fingers on it.

Cooler than the surrounding ground.

Not last month's work pretending to be permanent. Fresh. Yesterday's work, or the day before, while he had been in Mandya arguing mildly about the price of wire he had not imagined would be used against him. Someone had waited for the path to be without him. Someone had brought a post and a bag of cement and the quiet confidence of men who move boundaries when the owner's back is turned and the wedding's smile makes loudness expensive.

He pressed his palm fuller against the cement, as if temperature could lie. It did not lie. Cool. Recent. Planned.

He stood. Blood returned to his knees in pins. The path's reduced width showed itself fully now. What had been normal walking looked squeezed, apologetic, as if Hegde feet were being taught to ask permission of a line that had never required the asking. His body had already measured.

His father's walk had been wider.

The thought arrived without poetry and without permission. Appa's stick on this earth. Appa's voice, not loud, not legal, saying the path was for use, for carts, for the kind of neighbourliness that did not need a stamp from a man in a office who had never stood in red dust. Width as inheritance. A habit worn into dirt by decades of not needing to explain.

Ramesh looked toward the Gowda side. Empty cane. Empty bund. Dawn had not yet produced a body to accuse, which was its own kind of accusation. Mist lifting off their rows the same as his. Work done and workers gone, a change left to be discovered like a note without a signature.

He walked to the old gatepost, the original, dark with years, rough under the palm, scarred by rope and weather and the leaning of men who had argued here before, and set his hand flat against it. Fingers spread. Heat already waking in the wood from a sun that had barely cleared the trees, overnight radiance turning into day's claim. He held the boundary as if bone and wood could push back what cement had claimed. Stick hanging from the other hand, useless. Birds continuing, cheerfully irrelevant. Somewhere the pump finally caught and ran, a mechanical relief that did not relieve him.

Pride lit in him the way dry cane lit, fast, once the spark found the right leaf. Not a plan yet. A heat in the chest and behind the eyes. The wire stayed where it had been moved. His palm stayed on the post, fingers wide, holding.

He did not take his hand away at once. The gatepost's heat entered his skin. The new cement's coolness still lived in the memory of his other palm. Two temperatures. One path. A man standing between them while the mist finished dying and the fields became only themselves, cane, wire, red earth, and the six or eight inches that were somehow an entire sentence.

## S02 — Honor Frame

He released the gatepost and turned toward the house.

Walking back through his own field should have calmed him. Crop on either side, familiar, paid for in seasons of watching and worrying. Instead the familiarity arrived charged, every leaf a witness, every furrow a reminder that land remembered what men did to edges even when men smiled at feasts. The stick struck earth harder than necessary. Mist gone completely now. Heat establishing itself with April's arrogance, the sky clearing to a hard pale blue. Red dust already thinking about attaching to his hem and his mood.

Confront Venki.

He saw it without wanting the vision: the two of them at the boundary, voices careful because of the wedding, then less careful when carefulness failed. Words about inches becoming words about fathers. Alliance poisoned before the jasmine arrived at the gate. Ananya's face learning a new kind of weather. Saroja's face, logistics cracking into grievance. Venki's relatives counting insults like items in a list that would be recited later in kitchens. He rejected the picture before it reached the part where someone raised a hand. Not yet. Not like that.

Stay silent. Do nothing.

Wire stays. Path narrows permanently. Tomorrow's walk becomes this walk's smaller brother, and next month's smaller still, until Hegde feet learn a new width and call it fate. His mouth tasted metal. The cement's coolness returned to his fingers like a taunt. Rejected.

Tell Saroja. Tell the house.

Women upset before the wedding. Phone calls to relatives who loved advice more than earth, the dispute becoming theatre performed for people who had never put a hand on this particular grey apron of cement. Meera arriving Saturday into noise. Ananya carrying alliance and insult in the same week. Rejected. The house could have its flowers. This was not flower business.

Ask Venki. Diplomatically. Man to man. Over tea. Over the kind of smile that pretended inches were misunderstandings.

Asking meant standing in the posture of doubt about your own boundary. Asking meant Venki could smile and explain farmhands and weed-clearing and good intentions, and shift another three inches next month while calling it maintenance. Dignity did not survive the sentence *Did your people move my fence?* when the cement was fresh and the absence timed. Rejected.

A bird called from the bund, sharp, ordinary, the same call as yesterday and the year before. The field smelled of dry leaf and yesterday's irrigation, a green bitterness he had known since he was tall enough to see over the cane without jumping. His chappals found the packed line home. For a few steps the senses held him: not solving, only present. He breathed through his nose. Dust. Green. The faint smoke of someone's early trash fire. The heat in his chest did not leave. It changed shape, from flare to coal.

Nuisance could be waved away with a shout and a laugh that saved both faces. This could not. He would not ask. He would not raise it yet, not with sixteen days and alliance sitting on the house like a second roof that everyone praised and no one could sleep under easily. He would not forget. The wire lived in him now as the new outline of Venki, of Gowda, of the path that had been his father's and was still his whether poured grey agreed or not.

The back porch came into view: washed step still damp in the shaded corner, steel tumbler waiting where it always waited after the morning's first round, the house beginning its noise of water and vessels and a radio briefly alive then killed. Saroja's voice somewhere inside, already on a call, already making the wedding behave with the ferocity of love that scheduled.

A farmworker passed on the side path with a hoe and a coil of rope, heading toward the far channel. Young man, respectful, morning written on his face without complication, the blessing of not knowing what the fence had become overnight.

"Namaskara, saar."

Ramesh nodded. Said the one word he always said, the word that kept the day looking like other days. The boy continued, implements clinking softly, into cane that had not been rewritten by cement. Dust lifted behind his heels and settled. Normalcy performed itself for an audience of one who was not watching.

Ramesh took the steel tumbler from the porch ledge. Chai hot enough to announce itself through metal into his palm, the ordinary burn that meant the house still functioned, still cared, still believed morning could be tea and work and the wedding's approaching noise. He did not sit so much as stop: porch edge under his weight, body turned half toward the field direction, eyes finding the line where the path ran even when distance softened posts into a single dark suggestion. The chai sent its comfort up his arm, ginger, sugar, the particular taste of milk boiled too hard, and could not reach the place where the wire had settled behind his sternum.

Sixteen days to the wedding. Meera arriving Saturday, Saroja had said twice, city niece, useful for smiling, useful for hands that knew how to carry trays without becoming drama. None of that moved the post. None of that warmed the cement under yesterday's sun into something forgivable. He drank. The tumbler's heat and the gatepost's heat and the cement's remembered cool braided into one morning.

He looked at the fields. Held the steel and the decision that was not peace: silence as storage for pride, pride as the refusal to make his boundary small enough for someone else's convenience, the wire already living in him as the shape of days to come, days in which he would walk a narrower path and feel every missing inch like a name he would not say aloud.
